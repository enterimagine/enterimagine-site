Quantum Computing

Exploring a different way to compute the seemingly impossible.

Explore with Leo

The idea, simply

A different way to see what is possible

Ordinary computers process bits. Quantum computers use qubits, whose behaviour follows quantum physics. Algorithms use effects such as superposition, entanglement and interference to shape the probabilities of measurement outcomes. The aim is to solve particular kinds of problems differently; quantum computers do not simply try every answer and reveal the best one.

Current reality

Where we are today

Quantum processors and hybrid software are available for research. Noise, errors and limited scale constrain what can be done reliably. A result on one problem does not establish an advantage for every task, and useful comparisons need strong classical alternatives.

Future possibility

What could come next

More reliable qubits, error correction and better algorithms could open new possibilities. The long-term goal is useful, repeatable computation on selected problems. The path from an experiment to an economical application remains uncertain.

Potential areas of impact

Why it matters

Nature itself follows quantum rules. Better ways to study molecules and materials could eventually help people design medicines, batteries or industrial processes. These are research directions, not guaranteed outcomes.

  • Molecular and materials research
  • Selected optimisation problems
  • New approaches to scientific simulation

Real-world momentum

Why this is more than just imagination

Hybrid quantum-classical computing is still early, but it is no longer only a thought experiment. A European quantum computer already runs beside a supercomputer, supercomputing centers around the world are adopting a common link between quantum processors and GPUs, and companies have announced further deployments.

In operation · Public research infrastructure

Euro-Q-Exa at the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre

Announced 2026-02-12

In use since February 2026; 150-qubit upgrade planned

The EuroHPC Joint Undertaking inaugurated Euro-Q-Exa, a 54-qubit superconducting quantum computer built by IQM, at the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre in Munich.

It is integrated with the SuperMUC-NG supercomputer, so researchers can run hybrid classical-quantum workloads. Access is open to European researchers, industry and the public sector through EuroHPC.

An upgrade to 150 qubits is planned; the announcement gives the end of 2026, while a quote in the same release mentions early 2027.

EuroHPC Joint Undertaking announcement: Euro-Q-Exa ↗

Announced adoption · Shared infrastructure

Supercomputing centers adopt NVIDIA NVQLink

Announced 2025-11-17

Adoption announced; no deployment dates given

More than a dozen supercomputing and research centers in Europe, Asia and the Middle East, among them Jülich, CINECA, GENCI and RIKEN, announced they will use NVQLink to connect quantum processors to GPU systems, joining nine US national laboratories.

NVQLink is an open interconnect for hybrid quantum-GPU work through the CUDA-Q software platform. NVIDIA reports throughput of 400 Gb/s with latency under four microseconds.

NVIDIA gave no deployment dates, so this marks a shared direction rather than finished systems.

NVIDIA announcement: supercomputing centers adopt NVQLink ↗

Announced partnership · Planned deployment

IonQ + NVIDIA

Announced 2026-09-23

Installation planned for 2027

IonQ announced that Superion 256 is planned to become the first on-premises quantum processor at NVIDIA’s Accelerated Quantum Research Center.

The system is expected to link directly to NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 AI infrastructure through NVQLink, with workloads orchestrated through CUDA-Q. The joint program targets hybrid software development, large-scale system prototyping and quantum-GPU co-design.

The September 2026 announcement schedules installation for 2027. IonQ also expects the first Superion 256 customer deliveries in 2027.

IonQ announcement: IonQ + NVIDIA ↗

Announced partnership · Planned deployment

IonQ + Florida International University

Announced 2026-09-24

Installation expected in late 2027

IonQ announced a contract to deliver Superion 256 to FIU, selecting the university as its flagship academic partner in Florida. IonQ describes the agreement as the first sale and planned deployment of Superion 256 in the state.

On-campus installation is expected in late 2027, following completion of FIU’s data center. Intended uses include research, education and workforce development.

Expected research areas include materials science, AI, logistics, cybersecurity, health and power grid management. This announcement does not itself establish a GPU-integrated deployment at FIU.

IonQ announcement: IonQ + Florida International University ↗

Together these show concrete institutional momentum, not proof of broad, mature commercialization or large-scale quantum advantage. One system is already in use; the others are announcements, and their installation dates and intended outcomes remain expectations.
