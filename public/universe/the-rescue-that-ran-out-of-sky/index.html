The Rescue That Ran Out of Sky

A small robot was launched to catch a 22-year-old NASA telescope and lift it to safety. Its own steering failed first. Here is what that near miss teaches about repairing machines in orbit.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

RoboticsSpaceInnovation

A fireball over Sumatra

At 7:39 on a Wednesday morning, Eastern time, a bright streak crossed the sky above North Sumatra in Indonesia [1][12]. The date was November 4, 2026. The streak was NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory coming home after almost 22 years in space [1].

Swift was built for a two-year mission. It worked more than ten times that long, catching over 2,000 gamma-ray bursts and over 1,400 supernovae [2]. Astronomers have written about 7,000 papers with its data [2].

Satellites in low orbit always fall in the end. What makes this ending unusual is what happened four months before it.

In July, a rocket dropped from the belly of an airliner carried a robot called LINK into orbit [6]. It had three robotic arms, three ion thrusters and nearly 20 feet of solar panels [6]. Its job was to fly up to Swift, grab a spacecraft never designed to be grabbed, then push it back to a safe height [6].

LINK never touched Swift. The robot fell first, over the Pacific in September [9][10].

So was the rescue a failure? Mostly yes. Yet the way it failed is one of the most useful lessons space robotics has had in years.

The slow thief called drag

Space is not quite empty a few hundred kilometers up. A thin trace of atmosphere still brushes past every satellite. Each brush steals a little speed. Lose enough speed and the orbit sinks.

How thick that trace is depends on the Sun. When solar activity rises, the upper atmosphere heats up and swells outward, so a satellite meets more air at the same height. NASA said higher solar activity than expected had increased the drag on Swift [3]. Swift had no engine of its own to climb back up [3].

Think of a cyclist coasting on a road that slowly turns to sand. Nothing dramatic happens on any given day. The bike just keeps losing ground.

Swift started near 600 kilometers [10]. By early February 2026 its average height had dropped below about 400 kilometers [4]. NASA's engineers watched a second number even more closely: about 300 kilometers. Below that line, operating the spacecraft gets hard and the fall speeds up [4]. Late in 2025, forecasts showed Swift could reach it as early as July 2026 [6].

That left two options. Let the telescope go, or try something nobody had done.

NASA tried. In September 2025 it awarded Katalyst Space Technologies of Flagstaff, Arizona, a $30 million contract to attempt the boost [5]. The award came through the Small Business Innovation Research program, a federal route for funding small companies to turn ideas into hardware [5]. NASA called it the first time a commercial robot would try to capture a government satellite that was not built for servicing [5].

There was a catch. Swift had no grapple fixtures, the handles that servicing robots normally hold on to [10]. The robot would have to find its own grip.

What actually happened

The mission moved fast. Here is the record, in order.

  • A race against the clock. In December 2025, the Penn State team that flies Swift began pointing it in attitudes that cut drag. By February 2026 they had switched to this mode completely, which bought months of altitude [9]. Most science was paused on February 11 to support it [4].
  • Launch. LINK flew on July 3, 2026, aboard a Northrop Grumman Pegasus XL rocket. The rocket was released from a modified L-1011 airliner over Kwajalein Atoll in the Marshall Islands, after three earlier attempts were scrubbed [6].
  • The spin. Within weeks, LINK lost control of its orientation. It tumbled and spoke to the ground only sporadically. Two of its three reaction wheels had stopped working. Its small cold-gas thrusters had partly failed too [7]. Reaction wheels are spinning flywheels that turn a spacecraft without fuel, a little like a skater turning by moving her arms. The robot still had power [7].
  • A change of plan. On August 19, NASA announced that LINK would not capture or boost Swift. It would attempt rendezvous and close-range flying instead [8]. A week later Swift's team restarted some science observations [17].
  • The diagnosis. Katalyst's lead investigator later told a National Academies committee that a power switch serving the reaction wheels failed about a week into the flight. A stuck thruster valve and a second failed switch followed. All three arms deployed and the electric thrusters worked [10]. These details are company-reported. The company also reports that LINK's closest approach to Swift was about 12 to 15 kilometers [11].
  • The ending. NASA ended its part in the mission on September 3 [9]. LINK reentered on September 24 or 25; sources give both dates [9][10]. Swift made its last science observation on October 20, by then below 275 kilometers [2]. It fell two weeks later [1].

The arms were never the weak link

When people picture a satellite rescue, they picture the grab: metal fingers closing around a spinning machine. That is the dramatic part, so it gets the attention.

LINK's story points the other way. By the company's account, its arms deployed and its thrusters fired [10]. What failed was housekeeping: a switch, a valve, the wheels that keep the robot facing the right way [7][10]. A surgeon with steady hands still cannot operate on the deck of a ship in a storm.

That matters because the hard part of servicing is not one dramatic move. It is weeks of patient flying beside another spacecraft, matching its motion within centimeters. A robot that cannot hold its own attitude never earns the chance to reach out.

Speed was both the point and the risk. The robot went from contract to launch in under a year [9]. NASA itself called it a high-risk, high-reward mission [8]. Was the schedule to blame? No public analysis says so. A slower program might have caught the faulty switch, or it might simply have launched after Swift had already fallen.

It is also worth remembering that docking robots have worked before. Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicle 1 latched onto the Intelsat 901 satellite in February 2020 [13]. Its twin docked with Intelsat 10-02 in April 2021 [14]. Both grabbed a part found on about 80% of satellites in that high orbit [14]. In July 2026 a DARPA robotics payload with two arms of seven joints each launched on its way to that same orbit [15].

Swift was harder on every count: lower, faster-decaying, with no handle and a hard deadline.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built from today's trends. No agency or company has announced them.

Satellites born with handles. Every new science satellite carries a simple standard grip point and a visual target for a robot's cameras. The cost is a few kilograms at launch. The payoff is that a telescope's life no longer ends the day its orbit sags or its fuel runs low.

The tow truck in a parking orbit. Instead of building a robot for each emergency, an agency keeps a fueled servicer waiting in a storage orbit. When a satellite reports trouble, the servicer is already up there, already tested, already known to steer well.

Low-drag flying as a standard playbook. The Penn State trick of pointing a spacecraft to cut drag becomes part of every mission's emergency plan [9]. Satellites ship with drag-saving modes preloaded, the way cars ship with a limp-home mode.

Practice on the dead. Before a servicer approaches a precious telescope, it rehearses on retired satellites and spent rocket stages. Each rehearsal tests the dull parts that failed on LINK: wheels, switches, valves and long stretches of formation flying.

A second look at Hubble. NASA has asked industry for ideas on Hubble, from a boost to a controlled reentry [16]. A future servicer could learn from LINK by proving its steering for months in orbit before it ever comes near the target.

Back to the fireball

The streak over Sumatra lasted seconds. It ended a career of nearly 22 years and a robotic rescue that ran out of sky.

The takeaway is not that rescuing satellites is impossible. Robots have docked with satellites before. More are on the way [13][15]. The lesson is narrower and more useful: in orbit, the hands only matter if the body can hold still. The next rescue will be won by the boring parts.

There is a clock on the next attempt too. Reporting on Swift's fall notes that Hubble is expected to come down by the mid-2030s, so any rescue would need to happen by about 2032 [16].
