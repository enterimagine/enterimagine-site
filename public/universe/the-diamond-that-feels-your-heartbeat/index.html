The Diamond That Feels Your Heartbeat

A crumb of diamond smaller than a sesame seed has picked up the magnetic whisper of a human heart at room temperature. Here is what that could change for cardiology, plus what it still cannot do.

31 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

Quantum ComputingBiotechAI & Data

A pulse you never touch

Imagine lying on a hospital bed with no sticky pads on your chest. No wires, no cold gel. A small probe hovers near your ribs. On a screen beside you, the familiar rhythm of your heartbeat scrolls past.

The probe is not listening for sound or reading voltage on your skin. It is sensing magnetism. Every time your heart contracts, a wave of electrical current sweeps through the muscle. Moving current creates a magnetic field, so each beat sends a faint magnetic pulse out through your chest and into the room.

Doctors have known this for more than 60 years. The first magnetic recording of a heartbeat dates to 1963 [6]. What has changed is the sensor.

In September 2026 a team led from Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz reported recording human heart signals with sensors made from diamond [2]. The working part of each sensor is a tiny truncated pyramid with a volume below half a cubic millimeter [1][2]. It runs at room temperature. It needs no liquid helium.

That last detail matters most. The question is whether a speck of gemstone can do a job that today takes a room-sized machine.

A whisper inside a roar

The heart's magnetic field is astonishingly weak. Physicist Bradley Roth puts it at roughly 50 to 100 picotesla [5]. The Earth's own field, the one that swings a compass needle, is about 30 million picotesla [5]. In other words, the planet is hundreds of thousands of times louder than your heartbeat [5].

For decades the only sensor good enough to pick out that whisper was the SQUID, short for superconducting quantum interference device. It is a ring of superconductor that responds to tiny changes in magnetism. The catch is that superconductors only work when they are extremely cold, so a SQUID sits in a flask of liquid helium a few degrees above absolute zero [5]. The pioneering heart recordings of 1970 used one inside a magnetically shielded room [6].

A newer option is the optically pumped magnetometer, or OPM. It uses a small glass cell of atomic vapor read out with laser light. OPMs avoid liquid helium, but their vapor cells must be heated. Most systems still need magnetic shielding [5].

So magnetocardiography, the medical name for recording the heart's magnetism, has stayed a niche tool. A handful of hospitals in Germany installed systems over the past decade [6]. Yet the method has a real appeal. It needs no skin contact. It is not distorted by how well different tissues conduct electricity, which affects the standard electrocardiogram (ECG) [2]. It also works where electrodes cannot go, such as on badly burned skin [2].

The bottleneck is hardware. A sensor that is small, cheap, safe to hold close to the body and comfortable at room temperature could move this method from the specialist lab toward the bedside.

Flawed diamonds, perfect sensors

The trick relies on a flaw. In a nitrogen-vacancy center, or NV center, one carbon atom in the diamond lattice is replaced by nitrogen and its neighbor is simply missing. That tiny defect behaves like a single quantum compass needle. Shine green laser light on it and it glows. How brightly it glows depends on the magnetic field around it, so a light detector can read magnetism as a change in brightness [9].

Here is where the field stands, as of this autumn:

  • First human hearts, three ways. The Mainz-led study, published in Science Advances on September 16, 2026, used three diamond systems built independently by Mainz, by the Universities of Stuttgart and Freiburg, plus the startup Q.ANT [2][3]. The preprint lists 30 authors [1]. They recorded heart signals both inside and outside magnetic shielding [1]. The sensors need no extra magnetic bias field, unlike some rival designs [3].
  • A sensor cool enough to hold close. At Institute of Science Tokyo, a team led by Takayuki Iwasaki reported in Applied Physics Letters on July 22, 2026, a diamond magnetometer that runs on a 210-milliwatt laser, about a tenth of what typical systems need [4]. Its diamond stays near 38°C, under the 42°C threshold where heat starts to hurt [4]. It sits just 2 millimeters from its target and reached a sensitivity of about 3 picotesla per root hertz [4]. Carmaker supplier DENSO was among the partners [4].
  • A track record in animals. Iwasaki's group had earlier used a diamond chip to map electrical currents in a living rat heart, with a resolution of 5.1 millimeters [9].
  • AI enters the clinic. SandboxAQ, an Alphabet spinout, is testing CardiAQ, a room-temperature magnetic heart scanner that uses AI to strip out background magnetic noise [8][7]. The company says it needs no shielded room and scans in about five minutes [7]. In company-reported results, a Mount Sinai emergency department study of 127 people found 76% sensitivity and 84% specificity for the blocked-artery problems it targeted [7]. The device is investigational and has not been cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration [7].

That last example is not a diamond sensor. It shows where the whole field is heading, though: small quantum sensors paired with software that separates a heartbeat from the hum of a busy building.

Why does this matter in practice? Chest pain accounted for about 6.5 million US emergency visits in 2017, according to figures cited by Fierce Healthcare [8]. A fast, contact-free test that helps sort those patients would be valuable, provided it proves itself in larger trials.

One beat is not enough

Now the honest part. The diamond sensors in the Mainz study did not capture each heartbeat cleanly the way an ECG does. The researchers averaged the signal over a few hundred to several thousand beats to pull the heart's pattern out of the noise [1]. Their sensors reached a sensitivity of 6 to 26 picotesla per root hertz [1]. That is impressive for a crumb of diamond, but it is not yet enough for a single clean beat.

The team says so plainly. Established SQUID and OPM systems remain more sensitive with a better signal-to-noise ratio [2][3]. The authors frame their results as groundwork for future single-beat recordings [1].

The Tokyo sensor tells a similar story from another angle. Its best figures come from bench tests. It detected a simulated brain-strength signal of 77.7 picotesla at about 2.5 millimeters, using a dry model rather than a person [4].

There are paths forward. One is a flux concentrator, a piece of magnetic material that gathers field lines and funnels them into the diamond like a lens focusing light. The Mainz team suggests this could boost the signal more than a hundredfold, though that is a projection, not a measured result [2]. Another is gradiometry: pairing sensors so that distant noise, which hits both equally, cancels out while the nearby heart signal does not [1].

The clinical evidence has its own limits. The SandboxAQ results come from the company, from studies of a few dozen to about a hundred people [7]. Promising early accuracy often shrinks in larger, more varied groups of patients. None of these devices is a substitute for a doctor's assessment today.

So the real breakthrough is narrower than a headline might suggest. Diamond has shown it can feel a human heart. Whether it can do so fast, cheaply and reliably enough for a busy ward is still an open question.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments. They are not announced plans by any company, hospital or research group. Nothing here is medical advice.

The ambulance that maps before it arrives. A paramedic slides a palm-sized magnetic probe over a patient's chest during the ride to hospital. No electrodes need to stick to sweaty or injured skin. Software cleans out the roar of the engine and the radio, then sends a heart map ahead so the emergency team knows what to prepare.

A burn unit without wires. Patients with severe burns are among those for whom skin electrodes are hard to use. A ring of diamond sensors mounted above the bed keeps track of the heart's rhythm from a few millimeters away, never touching damaged skin.

Two heartbeats, one belly. Separating a baby's heart signal from the mother's is one of the uses the Mainz researchers point to. In this imagined clinic, a pregnant patient sits in an ordinary chair while paired sensors tease apart the two rhythms, with no shielded room in sight.

The surgeon's magnetic flashlight. During an operation near delicate nerves, a diamond probe on the tip of an instrument feels the faint magnetic signals of active nerve fibers. It warns the surgeon before a cut goes too close. Room-temperature operation is what makes a probe like this conceivable at all.

One sensor, many organs. The same pocket-sized device that reads hearts also reads brains, the way a camera serves for portraits and landscapes. Clinics that could never afford a helium-cooled scanner share a few rugged diamond units, swapping software for each job.

Back to the bed with no wires

Return to that hospital bed with nothing stuck to your chest. Today it is still a picture, not a product. The diamond sensors that exist can feel a human heart, but they need many beats to do it [1]. The AI-assisted scanners being tested in emergency rooms are still investigational [7].

Yet the direction is clear. For 60 years, recording the heart's magnetism meant liquid helium, shielded rooms and specialist staff [5][6]. In 2026 researchers showed the same faint signal can be picked up by a crumb of diamond at body-friendly temperatures [2][4]. The cold has left the room. What remains is an engineering race for sensitivity, plus the slow, careful work of clinical trials.

The lesson reaches beyond cardiology. Quantum technology is often pictured as giant machines in frosty labs. Some of its most practical uses may instead be small, warm and close to the body.

If this story sounds familiar, it should. In The Brain Scanner That Lets You Fidget, we met quantum sensors that read the brain without pinning the head in place. The heart is the next organ in line.
