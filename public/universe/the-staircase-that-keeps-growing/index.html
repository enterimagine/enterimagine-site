The Staircase That Keeps Growing

Mexico City's Angel of Independence has gained 14 steps since 1910 without growing an inch. Radar satellites now measure that kind of sinking in hundreds of cities at once. The data is free for anyone to use.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart CitiesSpaceSmart Mobility

The monument that climbs

On Paseo de la Reforma, the golden Angel of Independence has stood on its column since 1910. Over the decades, the city has added 14 steps to its base [1][2].

The monument did not rise. The street around it fell.

Mexico City is built on the clay of an ancient lakebed. About 20 million people live on top of it [1]. An engineer first documented the sinking in 1925 [1]. In the 1990s and 2000s some parts of the metro area dropped about 35 centimeters a year, a little more than the length of a school ruler, enough to damage the subway [1].

This year the sinking was measured in a new way. A radar satellite passed over the city again and again, peering through cloud. From those passes it produced a map of where the ground was dropping fastest. Around the international airport the map turned dark blue, the color for ground falling more than 2 centimeters a month [1][2].

Here is the surprise. The same kind of measurement now covers 465 large cities around the world. For almost half of them, no one had ever measured their sinking from space before [3][4].

A slow emergency underfoot

Land subsidence is the gradual sinking of the ground. In big cities the usual cause is groundwater pumping. When an aquifer is drained, the clay layers that held water compact like a squeezed sponge. They rarely spring back [1][2]. In Mexico City the weight of the buildings themselves adds to the squeeze [1].

The real damage comes from uneven sinking. When one side of a block drops faster than the other, foundations crack, sewers break, subway tunnels bend and flood water collects where it never used to [4]. In Mexico City residents have seen tilted historic buildings, buckled streets, sinkholes and burst water pipes. A section of Metro Line 9 was partly closed for repairs in 2023 [5].

That makes sinking a smart-city problem as much as a geology problem. Water managers, transit agencies and road crews all need to know where the ground is moving and how fast.

Ground surveys can measure a few points with great precision. Surveyors set benchmarks, return months later and compare heights. What they cannot do cheaply is watch every street of a megacity every couple of weeks.

Satellites flip that trade-off. They see everything at once, in less detail per point but with a view no survey crew could match. NISAR has already shown the range of the idea: it also mapped how the ground shifted near Caracas and La Guaira after two earthquakes in Venezuela in June 2026 [6].

Why now? Two things happened in 2026. NASA and India's space agency began releasing radar data from their joint satellite to the public for free [6]. In October, a study in the journal Nature Cities gave the first consistent global picture of how fast big cities are sinking [3].

Measuring millimeters from orbit

The technique is called interferometric synthetic aperture radar, or InSAR. A satellite sends radar pulses to the ground and records the echo. When it returns to the same spot days later, it compares the two echoes. A tiny shift in the timing of the wave reveals that the ground moved closer or farther away. Think of it as a tape measure made of microwaves, read twice and compared.

Here is what is verified so far.

  • A new satellite with a giant dish. NISAR, the NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar, launched from India in July 2025 [1][6]. It is the first satellite NASA and ISRO have built together. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory supplied the L-band radar and a 12-meter antenna reflector, the largest radar antenna NASA has sent to space. ISRO supplied the spacecraft and a second, S-band radar [1][2]. L-band waves are about 24 centimeters long, long enough to pass through cloud [2].
  • A map of Mexico City. NISAR images the same land twice every 12 days [1][6]. Its first Mexico City map used passes from October 25, 2025, to January 17, 2026 [1][2]. Landmarks on it include Benito Juárez International Airport, Lake Nabor Carrillo to the northeast and the Chalco wetland to the south [1][2].
  • Free data. Public release of NISAR's L-band data began on July 20, 2026, through the Alaska Satellite Facility. The full first-year science record is expected by the end of 2026 [6]. An earlier batch of more than 100,000 data products came out in February [7].
  • A global atlas. The Nature Cities study used radar from Europe's Sentinel-1 satellites to survey 465 cities with more than a million residents in 107 countries, covering 2014 to 2023 [3][8]. The data were processed in one consistent way and tied to satellite positioning stations on the ground [8].
  • Who is sinking. In Mexico City the fastest-sinking 5% of land drops about 217 millimeters a year, while the citywide median is 8 millimeters [3][4]. Medan, Indonesia, has the worst citywide median at about 33 millimeters a year. Bandung follows at 22 millimeters, with hot spots near 120 [3][4]. Boston, New York, London, Tokyo and Sydney were broadly stable [3].
  • Who stopped. Tokyo halted most of its sinking by regulating pumping and switching to surface water. Beijing and Tianjin took similar steps [4][8].

The map is not the cure

The numbers need care. Different sources describe Mexico City's sinking in ways that look contradictory. NASA's map shows more than 2 centimeters a month in the worst areas over one dry season [1][2]. One magazine headline used 14 inches a year, a figure NASA attributes to the 1990s and 2000s [1][9]. The global study reports 217 millimeters a year for the fastest 5% of land but only 8 millimeters as the city median [3]. All can be true at once, because they measure different places over different periods.

NISAR's map is also early. It covers about four months. NASA notes that some yellow and red patches are probably leftover noise that should fade as more data arrives [1][2].

The global study names its own limits. It may miss sinking just outside city boundaries, it is less certain where ground stations are sparse and its trends need more years of monitoring to confirm [3].

The bigger caveat is that satellites diagnose; they do not repair. Sinking is cumulative and largely irreversible, a point researchers stressed in coverage of the study [4]. Clay that has compacted does not refill. The brake that has worked so far is water policy, as Tokyo showed [4].

Mexico City is trying that too. It has limited pumping from city wells, sought new water sources and repaired leaking pipes [5]. A map tells officials where to act first. It cannot make the choices for them.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below imagine where today's trends could lead. None describes a project, budget or decision that any city or space agency has announced.

The subway that reads the sky. A transit agency feeds fresh radar maps into its maintenance planning. Track sections over fast-sinking clay get inspected more often. Crews arrive before a tunnel bends rather than after.

The bridge list that sorts itself. Amsterdam already uses satellite radar to check movement at about 100 of its roughly 1,600 bridges and to rank repairs [10]. In this future every old canal city does the same with free data. The order of repairs follows the ground rather than the calendar.

The pumping meter in orbit. A water utility compares each well's output with the ground motion above it. Wells sitting over the fastest sinking are rested first. The effect shows up on the next maps a few weeks later.

Your street on the forecast. A city publishes a sinking layer next to its flood maps. Homebuyers and builders see whether a lot is stable, slowly settling or tilting. Foundations get designed for the ground they will actually sit on.

The Angel's last step. Mexico City's pumping limits and new water sources slow the sinking enough that the monument goes decades without needing another step. Satellites record the slowdown month by month, turning the Angel into a symbol of recovery.

Counting steps

Go back to the Angel of Independence. Fourteen extra steps are a crude measuring stick, collected over more than a century. Now a satellite can produce a sharper version for an entire metropolis every couple of weeks. Anyone with an internet connection can download it.

The takeaway is simple. Cities can finally see the slow emergency under their streets in detail. What they do with that view, mostly decisions about water, will decide whether the staircase keeps growing.
