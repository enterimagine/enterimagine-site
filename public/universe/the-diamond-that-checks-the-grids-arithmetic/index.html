The Diamond That Checks the Grid's Arithmetic

A flaw in a diamond may let one small instrument check both kinds of electric current our grids now carry.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

Quantum ComputingEnergySmart Mobility

A saucer of metal with a gap in it

Imagine a ring of metal about the size of a saucer, with a slot cut through one side. Two coils of wire wrap around the ring. In the slot sits a chip of diamond barely a centimeter wide, bathed in green laser light and glowing faintly red. The whole assembly weighs about a kilogram [2].

It looks like a classroom demonstration. It is closer to a referee. Its job is to check whether one electric current is an exact multiple of another, down to a few parts in a hundred million.

In August 2026, Japan's National Metrology Institute, part of the research agency AIST, announced with university and national-lab partners that this device could compare both alternating and direct currents with the same hardware [1]. Its results appeared in the journal Scientific Reports [1]. Usually, those two jobs need separate instruments built on different physics. The most precise DC versions need cryogenic cooling [2].

Why should anyone outside a calibration lab care? Because every meter that bills a power plant, a substation or an electric-car charger ultimately borrows its honesty from instruments like this one. The surprise is that a flaw in a gemstone might be about to simplify that chain.

Two kinds of current, two rulebooks

Electricity grids were built on alternating current, or AC, which flips direction dozens of times a second. Solar panels, batteries, data centers and fast chargers for electric vehicles speak direct current, or DC, which flows one way. AIST frames the coming grid as one where both kinds coexist at every level, from generation to distribution [1].

Measuring large currents precisely is harder than it sounds. You cannot push hundreds of amperes through a delicate lab instrument. Instead, engineers scale the current down by a known ratio, then measure the small version. The device that checks that ratio is called a current comparator.

Here is the idea in plain terms. Wind two coils around one magnetic ring and send currents through them in opposite directions. If the currents match the intended ratio exactly, their magnetic fields cancel inside the ring. Any leftover field means the ratio is off. A sensor that detects that leftover field acts like the needle on a balance scale [2].

The trouble is that AC and DC have needed different needles. Conventional AC comparators use a closed ring of special magnetic metal with a detection coil of up to 500 turns; the cores run about 30 centimeters across and weigh around 20 kilograms [2]. Precision DC comparators rely on other sensors, such as fluxgates or SQUIDs (superconducting magnetic detectors). The best of them, cryogenic current comparators, need deep cooling to work [2].

That split matters for traceability: the unbroken chain of comparisons linking an everyday meter to the international definition of a unit. Since 20 May 2019, the ampere has been defined through the fixed charge of a single electron [6]. Fragmented AC and DC chains make linking a meter to that definition slower than it needs to be [2].

The pressure is not abstract. In the United States, NIST works with state inspectors who test commercial EV chargers much as they test gas pumps, under a dedicated section of its weights-and-measures handbook [5]. DC charging needs DC standards to check against.

What the diamond actually did

The key ingredient is a nitrogen-vacancy center, or NV center: a spot in a diamond crystal where a nitrogen atom sits beside a missing carbon atom. That tiny defect behaves like a compass needle for magnetic fields. Shine green light on it and it glows red; the brightness shifts with the magnetic field around it, which can be read out with microwaves and a light detector [2]. Crucially, it works at room temperature.

Here is what the AIST-led team reported, based on its preprint and the agency's announcement.

  • The detection coil is gone. The diamond sensor sits in a 2-centimeter gap cut into the magnetic core and reads the leftover magnetic flux directly, replacing the detection winding of a conventional AC comparator [2]. AIST says the central section is less than one-third the size and less than one-tenth the mass of conventional comparators [1]; the paper describes a core 10 centimeters across weighing about 1 kilogram [2].
  • One sensor, both currents. The same diamond detector handled a 1-ampere AC test at 67 hertz (chosen to avoid mains noise) and a DC test using one-second current pulses [2].
  • The headline precision. The statistical uncertainty of the comparison, known as type-A uncertainty, reached 5 parts in 100 million for both AC and DC. The team describes this as a 20-fold improvement over the roughly one part in a million typical of national-lab AC comparators [2].
  • DC performance near the field's workhorse. The measured DC ratio error was 1.5 parts in 10 million, close to the roughly one part in 10 million cited for conventional DC comparators [2].
  • A jump for diamond sensing. The authors note that NV sensors in ordinary conditions typically resolve no better than about 200 microamperes in this kind of measurement. Their system detected changes of 50 nanoamperes [2].

For scale, other diamond current sensors are aimed at very different jobs. A German team reported a calibration-free NV sensor measuring 0 to 30 amperes of DC in a busbar with 0.28% accuracy [7]. A Tokyo team built a diamond sensor pair to watch electric-vehicle battery currents, detecting busbar currents as low as 10 milliamperes [8]. The AIST device is different in kind: it is not a meter but a referee for meters.

Commercial interest is stirring too. Japan's national quantum institute began working with an instrument company and a spin-out in August 2026 on NV-based sensing instruments, according to trade reporting [4].

Reading the fine print on 10⁻⁸

Coverage of this device has led with a 10⁻⁸ figure [3]. The paper deserves a more careful reading.

First, the word changed. The 2025 preprint title promised "10⁻⁸-level accuracy" [2]. The published title, as AIST reports it, says "10⁻⁸-level type-A uncertainty" [1]. That shift matters. Type-A uncertainty measures how much repeated readings scatter, the way a bathroom scale might wobble by a few grams between weigh-ins. It does not capture systematic errors, the way a scale might read 200 grams heavy every single time. A full metrology claim needs both. The paper reports only the first [2].

Second, the systematic errors are still large in places. In the AC test, the measured ratio error was 76 microamperes per ampere, more than a thousand times bigger than the statistical scatter [2]. The authors say this is not a fundamental limit and could be corrected by calibration. It is still well short of the roughly one part in a million that national labs typically expect [2].

Third, the scope is narrow so far. Only a 1:1 ratio was tested. The system's bandwidth tops out at 300 hertz, limited by its readout electronics, which leaves out much of the higher-frequency distortion that power-quality engineers study [2]. DC measurements needed about 40 times longer averaging than AC because of slow drifts in the sensor [2].

Finally, the cold machines are not obsolete. Cryogenic current comparators still reach about one part in a billion on DC, far better than this prototype [2].

None of this makes the result less interesting. A room-temperature device that handles both AC and DC with one sensor, at a twentieth of the weight, is a real step. But it is a promising prototype, not yet a replacement standard. Nobody knows yet whether the systematic errors will shrink as fast as the team hopes.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built on the research described above. None of them describes an existing product or a commitment by any organization.

The suitcase standard. Today, some calibration chains depend on shipping instruments to a national lab and back. Picture a diamond comparator compact enough to travel in a padded case to a utility's own lab, where it checks the instrument transformers on site. The national standard comes to the grid instead of the grid queuing for the standard.

One bench for both currents. A calibration lab today might keep separate AC and DC setups built on different physics. Imagine a single bench where a technician checks a substation's AC transformer in the morning and a solar farm's DC sensor in the afternoon, with the same diamond detector doing both jobs. Fewer instruments, one chain of traceability.

The honest fast charger. Inspectors already test commercial EV chargers the way they test fuel pumps [5]. Picture the reference meters they carry being checked, in turn, against a room-temperature comparator at a regional lab rather than a cryogenic system at a national one. A driver paying for a DC charge would never see it, yet the number on the screen would rest on a shorter chain.

The DC microgrid audit. Data centers and battery parks increasingly move power as DC internally. Imagine an auditor verifying the energy flowing between a solar array, a battery and a server hall with portable equipment traceable to a diamond standard. The bill between neighbors on a shared DC network becomes easier to trust.

The diamond in the substation. Further out, the same sensing trick could migrate from the lab into hardened field instruments that watch high currents continuously. Picture a substation where a sensor flags a drifting transformer weeks before it would fail a scheduled calibration. That would move metrology from an annual check toward constant care.

Back to the saucer

Return to that metal ring with a slot in its side. When the two currents in its coils balance perfectly, the diamond in the gap sees nothing at all. That nothing is the whole point: a null reading is how the grid's arithmetic gets checked.

The takeaway is twofold. A room-temperature quantum sensor has matched the statistical precision of far heavier machines for both AC and DC. The systematic errors that matter most to metrologists still need taming. If they fall, the instruments that keep every meter honest could become smaller, lighter and shared between the two kinds of current our grids now carry.
