The Dust That Leaves on Its Own

Solar farms wash themselves with water they do not have. Electric charge can do the job instead — and it has already been proved on the Moon before any desert.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

EnergyInnovationSpace

A pane of glass cleans itself on the Moon

In March 2025, a camera on a lander in the Moon's Mare Crisium region watched a small pane of glass get dirty, then watched it get clean again.

Nothing touched it. No brush, no cloth, no jet of gas. The glass was furred gray with lunar regolith — the shattered, electrically clingy dust that coated every Apollo spacesuit and ground through the seals of every mechanism that met it. A voltage was applied to a pattern of electrodes printed beneath the surface. The dust walked off the edge.

The instrument was NASA's Electrodynamic Dust Shield, carried to the surface on Firefly's Blue Ghost lander in January 2025 and switched on within hours of landing [9][10]. On the glass, it removed 97% of the dust. On a thermal radiator surface, running in a simpler mode, 82%. What stayed behind on the glass was mostly coarse grains, the kind that would slide off a tilted panel on their own [10].

Here is the part worth sitting with. On Earth, where the same problem costs the solar industry billions of euros a year, the equivalent technology is still in laboratories, published mostly on single test panels and ten-centimeter glass samples.

The physics has been demonstrated on another world. It has not yet been demonstrated on a solar farm in Morocco.

Washing glass in places with no water

Solar panels are built where the sun is reliable. The sun is most reliable where it does not rain, which is also where the dust is.

The industry calls the resulting loss soiling, a mild word for a large number. An international solar energy program estimated in 2023 that soiling costs the world around 3 to 5% of annual photovoltaic output, worth roughly €3 to 5 billion [11]. Its October 2025 fact sheet revised that upward, to an average loss of 4 to 7% worldwide [12]. The estimate went up, not down, even as cleaning practice improved.

Local figures are sharper than global ones. A 44 MWp plant beside a Moroccan mine lost an average of 0.30% of its output per day over a year of monitoring, peaking at 0.97% per day in January [13]. Left entirely uncleaned, the plant would have shed 5.8% of its annual yield; with cleaning scheduled intelligently, 1.9% [13]. Each cleaning cost about $9,460 [13].

The standard remedy is water; the bill is both financial and physical. One widely quoted estimate puts global panel washing at around 10 billion gallons a year, comparable to the drinking water of two million people, with water cleaning accounting for roughly 10% of a plant's operating cost [1][2]. A 1% output loss at a 150 MW plant is worth about $200,000 a year in revenue [1][2]. Those figures come from a single research group and should be read as an estimate rather than an audited total.

Trucking water into a desert to wash glass is an awkward thing for a technology that exists to reduce environmental strain. Which is why the appealing alternative is to make the dust leave by itself.

Four ways to push dust with electricity

All of these use the same basic idea: give the dust a charge, or induce one in it, then push it. They differ in how.

  • Charge induction with a moving bar. A group at MIT coated panel glass with a transparent conductive layer of aluminum-doped zinc oxide just 5 nm thick, then passed an aluminum electrode above it at about 12 kV, roughly 1.5 cm away [4][5]. Dust grains pick up an induced charge and are repelled from the grounded surface. In the laboratory prototype, up to 95% of the lost power came back [3][5]. Almost no current flows, so the energy cost is essentially the small motor that moves the bar [4][5].
  • Texturing the glass so dust barely holds on. A later MIT paper etched features around 200 nm into the glass and coated it with 15 nm of indium tin oxide, shrinking the contact area between grain and surface. The force holding a 5 µm silica bead dropped from roughly 120 nN to about 10 nN [6]. For 5 to 10 µm dust, power recovery rose from about 8% on flat coated glass to about 90% on textured glass [6]. A brief pass at around 12 kV across a 1 cm gap draws under 12 W/m², against the roughly 500 W/m² the panel produces [6].
  • Electrodynamic screens. Instead of a moving bar, a fixed grid of electrodes carries a travelling wave that marches dust across the surface. This is the lunar method [10]. A low-cost circuit-board version published in July 2026 used 576 electrodes, each 80 µm wide, driven at about 1.2 kV stepped up from 12 V, removing a peak of 52% of the dust at 0.85 Wh/m² per cycle [8].
  • Corona wind. A group in India used a 1,000 V electrode to generate an ion wind that simply blows sand off the glass, with a mesh to stop it settling back. The authors report more than 95% cleaning for grains between 20 and 200 µm, though they do not quantify the energy used [7].

One company, a spin-out from Boston University, says its screen clears over 90% of dust in 30 to 40 seconds, with pilots in California, India and Saudi Arabia [14][15]. No performance data has been published.

The honest summary of the terrestrial state of play: several mechanisms work convincingly at bench scale, one works on the Moon; none has published field results from a commercial solar farm.

What the method needs to work

The enemy turns out to be the ingredient

Ask anyone who maintains panels in a dry climate what ruins a surface; the answer is dew. Moisture arrives overnight, dust settles into it, the sun bakes the mixture, then what was loose powder becomes a crust that a brush struggles with.

So it is an odd result that the MIT method depends on exactly that moisture. Desert dust is mostly silica, an insulator; charge induction needs the grains to conduct a little. Above roughly 30% relative humidity, a film of adsorbed water a few molecules thick makes them conductive enough to be pushed [2][5]. Tests across the full range from 5% to 95% humidity found removal nearly complete above that threshold and markedly harder below it [2][5].

Many deserts cross 30% at night [2]. The same nightly moisture that cements dust onto glass is the moisture that lets charge lift it off — if the cleaning runs before the sun does the baking.

The second limitation is size. Grains below about 30 µm resist removal [6], which matters because fine particles both stick hardest and block light most effectively per unit mass. The nano-textured glass was built specifically for this, lifting recovery for 5 to 10 µm dust from roughly 8% to roughly 90% [6]. For grains of 20 to 40 µm and larger, textured and flat glass performed about the same [6].

Then there is the arithmetic that should temper any enthusiasm. The 2026 circuit-board screen blocks 7.9% of incoming light, cutting transmittance from 96.2% to 88.3% [8]. Over a week of operation the net result was negative, −3.76%; the authors calculated that such a screen only repays its shading when soiling reaches around 15% between cleanings [8]. The cure cost more than the disease.

Safety and cost also deserve naming. A researcher at Boston University, commenting on the MIT work, warned that a conducting plate held near 10,000 V is a genuine hazard needing substantial extra equipment, then questioned whether the transparent conductive layer might cost more than the solar cell beneath it [5]. The MIT projection of 80 to 90% lower cleaning costs is explicitly pending field trials [5].

These are not reasons to dismiss the approach. They are the reasons it has stayed in laboratories while robots with brushes went to work on actual solar farms.

Imagined futures

What follows is speculation: physically plausible extrapolations from the published work above, not announced products, plans or timelines. No figures in this section are measured.

Cleaning scheduled by the weather, not the calendar. The Moroccan study showed that when you clean matters as much as whether you clean. A charge-based system that costs almost nothing per pass could run whenever humidity crosses its threshold, which is a decision a forecast can make rather than a crew.

The coating arrives with the panel. Retrofitting conductive film onto installed glass is awkward and probably uneconomic. Specified at manufacture, the same layer is a process step in a factory that already deposits thin films. Whether that happens depends less on physics than on whether a panel maker believes buyers will pay for it.

Two methods, split by grain size. Nothing requires one technique to do everything. A surface that sheds coarse grains electrostatically and gets an occasional water wash for the fine fraction would still cut water use substantially, which is a less elegant answer than full self-cleaning and an easier one to deploy.

Dust control as shared infrastructure. Lunar and terrestrial dust problems are being solved by the same physics in different gravity. Hardware developed for solar panels in Morocco and hardware developed for radiators on the Moon are converging on one question: how do you move a charged particle off a surface with no moving parts.

The screen that stops shading the panel. The transparency penalty is the clearest target in the field. Electrodes thin enough or placed well enough to vanish optically would change the calculation from "only worth it in extreme soiling" to "worth it everywhere" — nothing about that is physically forbidden.

Back to the Moon

That pane of glass in Mare Crisium is worth one more look.

It worked because the Moon made the problem easy in one specific way: no air, no humidity, plus dust that is already electrically charged by sunlight. Everything that makes lunar dust horrible also makes it responsive to an electric field. On Earth the dust is less cooperative and the air gets a vote.

Still, the lunar result settles something. The mechanism is not speculative. A travelling electric field moves dust off a surface, repeatably, in the hardest dust environment anyone has tested. What remains is not a question of whether charge can clean glass but whether it can do so cheaply, safely and without shading the panel it protects.

The gap between those two statements is where most technology actually lives. The 97% figure from the Moon and the −3.76% net figure from a laboratory screen describe the same physics under different constraints [8][10].

It is also worth noticing what the sources do not contain: no published field trial from a commercial solar farm, no peer-reviewed cost per square meter, no durability data over years of thermal cycling. Those absences are the honest state of the field as of late 2026.

In The Night Shift on the Solar Farm, we followed the robots that currently do this work with brushes, rolling between rows in the dark. The charge-based approach is the attempt to make those machines unnecessary. For now the brushes are winning, because they exist.
