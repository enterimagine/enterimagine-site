The Solar Cell That Gave Up Germanium

Almost every high-end satellite panel is built on a wafer of a scarce metal that one country mostly controls. A new generation of space cells is learning to grow without it.

30 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

SpaceEnergyInnovation

Wings with a hidden passenger

A few minutes after reaching orbit, a new satellite unfolds its wings. Panel after panel swings open and locks, revealing a dark mosaic of solar cells that will feed the spacecraft for years. From a distance it looks like nothing more than glass and silicon.

Look closer at a high-end cell, though and you find a passenger most people have never heard of. The light-harvesting layers that do the real work are only 10 to 20 micrometers thick. They sit on a germanium wafer about 150 micrometers thick, roughly ten times deeper than the part that makes power [1]. A standard satellite carries somewhere between 6,000 and 15,000 of these wafers and larger ones need tens of thousands [1].

Germanium is a gray, brittle metal, a cousin of silicon. Most of the world's supply comes from a single country [4] and since 2023 its exports have been subject to government controls [2].

So here is the puzzle. Space solar power, one of the cleanest technologies we have, rests on a thin slab of a material that mostly serves as a platform. What if the platform could simply be left behind?

A metal with a deadline

The best space solar cells are "multi-junction" cells: several thin layers stacked like colored filters, each tuned to catch a different slice of sunlight. For nearly three decades, the standard recipe has grown those layers on germanium [5]. Germanium is both the foundation those layers grow on and one of the ingredients of the cell itself [8].

The trouble is supply. China accounts for about 91% of primary germanium metal production and more than 90% of refining capacity, according to an S&P Global market report from August 2026 [4]. Beijing introduced export licenses in August 2023, then banned germanium exports to the United States in December 2024 [2]. China's reported germanium metal exports through September 2025 were less than half of the same period in 2024 [2].

Prices followed. The European average price for high-purity germanium metal roughly doubled from 2024 to 2025, according to the US Geological Survey [2]. The cost bites hardest in space cells, where the germanium wafer alone accounts for about 30% of a standard cell's cost [1].

Then comes the date that makes this story urgent. In November 2025 China suspended its US export ban, but only until November 27, 2026 and its licensing system stayed in place [3]. That is less than a month from today. Satellite makers planning launches years ahead cannot easily bet on what happens next.

Three ways around the bottleneck

The space industry is attacking the germanium problem from three sides at once: change the cell, change the material, or change the supply.

  • Grow the cell, then drop the base. In September 2026 Rocket Lab announced the production release of a cell called IMM Apex, designed to contain no germanium [5]. The company reports a beginning-of-life efficiency of 31.5%, meaning it turns 31.5% of incoming sunlight into electricity when new and a cell mass about 40% lower than older triple-junction products [5]. It is meant as a drop-in replacement, so array builders would not need to redesign their panels [5]. For comparison, the best germanium-based cells reach about 32% [6].
  • A trick proven on Mars. "IMM" stands for inverted metamorphic. Instead of building the cell from the bottom up on a permanent wafer, the layers are grown upside down on a temporary base, the stack is glued face-down onto a handle and the original base is etched away, as described in a laboratory recipe from the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory [7]. Rocket Lab says the same underlying technology powered NASA's Ingenuity Mars helicopter [5]. The company is scaling production in Albuquerque, New Mexico, toward what it calls multi-hundred-kilowatt yearly volumes [5].
  • Use the cheap stuff. A review led by the University of Surrey, published in September 2026, argues that ordinary silicon cells could cut satellite power costs by up to 90% compared with triple-junction cells, which cost about $250 to $450 per watt [8]. Silicon cells give about 28% less power per area, but they are so light that they can deliver roughly twice the power per kilogram [8]. Rocket Lab itself has also announced silicon-based arrays aimed at future orbital data centers [6].
  • Find more germanium. Outside China, germanium refining capacity stands at about 31 metric tons and projects in Canada, the United States and South Korea could add about 95 tons more, according to S&P Global [4]. The European Space Agency has backed a process that recovers 80% of the germanium lost during cell manufacturing [1].

In a standard cell, the germanium wafer is roughly ten times thicker than the layers that make power [1]; the inverted design grows those layers on a temporary base and then removes it [7].

Germanium-free is not mineral-free

The cleverest part of the inverted cell is also its catch. Removing the germanium base does not remove critical minerals from the cell. The light-catching layers of high-end space cells are built from compounds of gallium and indium [8] and in the classic laboratory recipe the temporary base is made of gallium arsenide [7]. Gallium sits under the same Chinese export-control system as germanium and the suspension that ends in November covers both [3]. One bottleneck is eased; another remains.

There is a small performance price as well. IMM Apex is not even Rocket Lab's own most efficient cell and it trails the top germanium-based cells by about half a percentage point [6]. Its headline numbers are company-reported and have not yet been backed by independent published flight data.

Silicon brings a different trade-off. Under the same thickness of protective cover glass, silicon is about 2.6 times less resistant to radiation than triple-junction cells, which are also expected to keep more of their output after five years in orbit [8]. The Surrey authors call for fresh tests of modern silicon cells under real space conditions before anyone leans on them widely [8]. Silicon may suit cheap, short-lived satellites in low orbit, while long missions through harsh radiation are likely to keep their premium cells.

New mines and refineries will not close the gap quickly either. S&P Global estimates that even if five refineries outside China are running by 2030, they would cover only about 48% of that year's demand outside China [4].

The honest summary: these technologies widen the menu. None of them makes the supply question disappear.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities grounded in today's trends. They are not announced plans by Rocket Lab, ESA or any other organization named in this article.

The two-tier constellation. Satellite operators stop treating solar cells as one product. Long-lived spacecraft in harsh orbits keep premium multi-junction cells, while swarms of short-lived low-orbit satellites fly cheap silicon and mission planners choose cells the way builders choose between steel and timber.

The cell factory with no scrap. Cell makers treat every gram of germanium and gallium as reusable stock. Offcuts, failed wafers and etched-away material flow straight back into refining, so a factory's need for fresh metal shrinks year after year.

Power wings for orbital computers. If data centers ever move into orbit, they will need solar wings far larger than today's. Light, cheap cells become the deciding factor for whether those orbiting server halls make sense at all.

Panels made on the Moon. ESA has studied whether lunar soil could one day feed solar-panel production [1]. Imagine a lunar base that builds simple, low-efficiency panels from local regolith for its own power, saving its few precious Earth-made cells for vehicles and instruments.

A supply map with more dots. New refineries in several countries each produce a modest stream of germanium. No single change in export rules can halt satellite production any longer, because buyers can switch between suppliers, cell designs and materials.

The wing, unfolded

Go back to that satellite opening its wings above the clouds. The cells look the same as ever. What is changing is what lies beneath them: for some, a thick germanium wafer; for others, nothing but a thin film that was grown upside down and freed from its base; for still others, plain silicon.

The lesson reaches far beyond space. Supply chains are not only shaped by where minerals are mined, but by how cleverly engineers can design around them. A platform that once seemed essential can turn out to be optional.
