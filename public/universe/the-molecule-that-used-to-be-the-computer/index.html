The Molecule That Used to Be the Computer

In 2001 a molecule served as a quantum computer. In 2026 a quantum computer returned the favor by simulating a molecule's fingerprint. The real test is whether chemists can trust it.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Quantum ComputingBiotech

A fingerprint in a magnet

In a quiet room of a pharmaceutical lab, a chemist lowers a glass tube no thicker than a pencil into a white cylinder taller than she is. Inside the cylinder, a superconducting magnet bathed in liquid helium holds an intense, steady magnetic field. A few minutes later a screen fills with a jagged line of peaks.

That line is a molecular fingerprint. Each peak reports how the nuclei of particular atoms wobble in the magnet and how they tug on their neighbors. Chemists read it the way a mechanic reads an engine's sound. Drug makers use it to check which compounds stick to a target protein, how tightly they bind and what shape they take [1].

Here is the surprising part. In 2001, molecules sitting in exactly this kind of magnet became some of the first quantum computers. Researchers at IBM used the nuclei of a specially made molecule as seven qubits and factored the number 15 into 3 times 5 [2].

A quarter of a century later the roles have flipped. In September 2026, a team from Quantinuum and the German software company HQS Quantum Simulations posted a study in which a trapped-ion quantum computer simulated the full NMR fingerprint of a 22-spin molecule [3]. The molecule used to be the computer. Now the computer is pretending to be the molecule.

Why a few dozen spins can stump a supercomputer

NMR stands for nuclear magnetic resonance. Many atomic nuclei behave like tiny bar magnets, a property physicists call spin. In a strong field those spins precess like spinning tops, each at a rate set by its chemical surroundings. Neighboring spins also nudge one another, splitting single peaks into clusters. The final spectrum is the sum of all those interactions.

Predicting that spectrum from first principles is a quantum problem in the purest sense. Every spin added doubles the number of possible combined states the computer must track [3]. Ten spins are easy. Twenty are hard. Classical software survives by using clever approximations, which can break down when spins are strongly coupled or when the magnet is weak [3].

How far can conventional computers go? A separate study by Swiss teams notes that exact classical simulation becomes impractical beyond about 20 spins. Tensor networks, a compression method that keeps only the most important correlations, stretch that to roughly 32 spins [4].

Why does this matter beyond the lab bench? Many promising drug fragments, catalysts and battery materials have spectra that are hard to interpret. A reliable way to predict what a molecule should look like helps chemists confirm what they made or spot what went wrong.

NMR also has a gift for early quantum computers. Real spectra are naturally blurred, because nuclear signals fade as spins drift out of step. A simulation only has to be as sharp as the instrument, not infinitely precise [5]. That makes NMR one of the few chemistry problems where today's imperfect machines can do useful practice runs.

What the trapped ions actually did

The new study is a preprint, meaning it has not yet passed peer review. Its details are unusually concrete, though. An independent trade publication has walked through them [6].

  • The molecule. The target was 1,2-di-tert-butyl-diphosphane, a small molecule with 22 magnetically active nuclei: two phosphorus atoms and 20 hydrogens. The team simulated how it would appear in a standard 500 megahertz spectrometer [3][7].
  • A clever shortcut. The two phosphorus spins were combined into a single effective spin, leaving 21. That trimmed the number of two-qubit operations needed per time step from 69 to 20 [6][7].
  • The machine. The run used Quantinuum's H2 computer, which traps individual charged atoms in electric fields and lets any qubit interact with any other [8]. The simulation used 42 qubits: 21 to stand in for the spins plus 21 helpers that detect when a qubit "leaks" out of its proper states [7].
  • The workload. The team stepped the simulated molecule forward through up to 70 moments in time. The deepest circuit contained 1,442 two-qubit gates. Its two-qubit gate error was about one in a thousand [7]. The machine fired 35,500 shots in all. Roughly a quarter were discarded because a helper caught a leak. The runs took place between January 21 and March 7, 2026 [7].
  • The result. The quantum spectrum agreed with classical reference calculations [3]. The company reports that it captured a double peak between 3.9 and 4.3 parts per million that earlier hardware runs had missed [9].

That earlier attempt is worth a look. In late 2025, a separate team simulated the same molecule on a 156-qubit IBM processor and a second commercial machine. The main peaks came out right, but the finer structure was lost [4].

Quantinuum describes the new run as the most accurate large-scale digital NMR simulation on quantum hardware so far. It notes that the claim rests on its own review of the literature [9]. HQS, which sells NMR software, calls the work a step toward practical usefulness [10].

Other groups are taking different routes to molecules. In October 2025, Google Quantum AI used its 105-qubit Willow chip to run an algorithm it calls Quantum Echoes. The company claims the algorithm ran 13,000 times faster than the best classical method for that task [11]. In a companion study it estimated distances inside small molecules such as toluene from the data [12].

Not an advantage, yet

It would be easy to oversell this. The paper itself does not.

The authors state plainly that classical NMR software is highly optimized and can simulate this molecule perfectly well. To matter in practice, they write, a quantum approach must beat those classical solvers, not just match them [7]. The trade write-up adds that classical tools remain more efficient for everyday high-field spectroscopy today [6].

Then there is sharpness. The quantum run resolved features about 0.07 parts per million apart. Some of the molecule's fine structure is closer to 0.01. Resolving it would require simulating at least about 200 milliseconds of the molecule's life, which means more than 500 time steps [7]. That is roughly seven times as many steps as this run. Even the collaborators who build a leading classical NMR package say the resolution is not yet enough for working spectroscopists [9].

So why is this exciting at all? Because the authors point to a different target. Everyday NMR uses strong magnets, a regime where classical shortcuts work well. In zero- to ultralow-field NMR, the field is weak and the spins couple tightly to one another. Those spectra are much harder for classical computers [7]. An independent analysis concluded that such spectra are a promising target for future error-corrected quantum machines [13].

There is also a reality check from history. The first quantum simulation of an NMR experiment, published by a multi-university team in 2021, handled just four spins. Its authors estimated that larger molecules would need around 100,000 gates at error rates near one in ten thousand [5]. Hardware is moving toward that line. Quantinuum's newer Helios machine has 98 qubits. The company reports a two-qubit fidelity of 99.921% [14]. But it has not crossed it.

The honest summary: this is a rehearsal on a molecule classical computers already understand. That is exactly how you test whether a new instrument can be trusted.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities, not announced plans of any company or lab named in this article.

The spectrum checked twice. A quality-control lab gets a puzzling NMR trace from a new batch of a drug. Overnight, a cloud quantum computer simulates the spectrum the ideal molecule should produce. The mismatch points straight to the stray peak of a contaminant. The batch is held before it ships.

NMR without the giant magnet. Compact zero-field NMR sensors, small enough for a field clinic or a mine site, record spectra that classical software struggles to decode. A remote error-corrected quantum computer translates them into molecule identities, turning the hardest part of the measurement into a software problem.

The chemist's sparring partner. An AI model proposes dozens of possible structures for an unknown compound. A quantum simulator predicts the NMR fingerprint for the most promising few. The chemist compares them with the real trace and keeps the one that fits, with the machines doing the tedious elimination.

The circle closes. Students in a quantum computing course load the very molecule IBM used as a computer in 2001. They simulate its NMR spectrum on a modern machine, then compare it with the real thing in the university's spectrometer. A historical curiosity becomes a standard benchmark.

A battery seen from the inside. Solid materials, such as the electrolytes in next-generation batteries, have spin networks that are hard to simulate classically. A materials lab uses quantum simulations to interpret their NMR spectra, helping engineers see where lithium ions move and where they get stuck.

Back to the magnet

Return to the chemist and her jagged line of peaks. For now, the software that explains those peaks runs on ordinary computers. It does the job well. The quantum version has only just learned to sing along.

What makes the moment interesting is the loop it closes. The same physics that let a molecule act as one of the first quantum computers is now a benchmark that tells us whether today's quantum computers are trustworthy. NMR is a fair test: nature has already written down the answer.

Quantum machines will not replace classical ones here. They will lean on them, a partnership we explored in The Quantum Computer's Secret Partner.
