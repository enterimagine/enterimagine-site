Who Will Give Hubble a Lift?

The most famous telescope ever built cannot steer itself. NASA has just asked industry who could catch it, push it higher or guide it home safely.

SpaceRoboticsInnovation

A handle nobody has used

On the back of the Hubble Space Telescope sits a piece of hardware that has never done its job. It is a docking ring, left there by the crew of the final space shuttle servicing visit in 2009 [3][8]. Think of it as a handle screwed onto a suitcase for a porter who has not yet arrived.

The ring was meant for a future robotic visitor. Its designers imagined a spacecraft that would one day grab it, latch on and take control of the telescope's fate [6].

Hubble needs that help because it has no engine of its own. It can swing its mirror toward a distant galaxy with great precision. It cannot climb [1].

Every orbit takes it through the thin outer wisps of Earth's atmosphere. Each pass steals a little speed, the way a bicycle coasting on a flat road slowly runs out of momentum. Over years, the orbit sinks.

On October 29, 2026, NASA formally asked companies how they might reach Hubble. It wants to hear how industry could raise its orbit, upgrade it or bring it down under control [1]. Responses are due by November 30 [1].

The surprise is not that NASA wants to keep Hubble alive. It is who NASA is now asking. The question is no longer addressed to astronauts on a shuttle that retired years ago. It is addressed to robots built by private companies.

A deadline written in thin air

NASA's own analysis puts Hubble's natural reentry at roughly 2033 to 2034. Any mission to change that would have to fly by about 2032 [1]. For a spacecraft that has to be designed, built, tested and launched, six years is not long.

The Sun is part of the reason the clock feels tight. When solar activity rises, the upper atmosphere heats up and swells outward. A thicker atmosphere means more drag, so the orbit decays faster [3].

The telescope is also aging in other ways. Hubble points itself using gyroscopes, spinning sensors that work like the balance organ in your inner ear. Three of its six gyroscopes have failed and a fourth is degrading [3].

Since 2024, Hubble has run on a single gyroscope, keeping another in reserve. That mode costs about 12 percent of its weekly scheduling efficiency, according to NASA figures reported at the time [5]. The observatory still does first-rate science. It simply does a little less of it.

Money is tight too. Hubble's budget has stayed roughly flat for years, which means it shrinks once inflation is counted. One former NASA science chief estimates the telescope is already about 30 percent down in real terms [3].

So why spend anything on an old instrument? NASA's leadership frames it as value for money. An observatory already in orbit, still working, may be cheaper to extend than to replace [1][2]. NASA is still developing the flagship mission meant to follow Hubble [1]. A longer life would bridge the gap.

There is a second reason that rarely makes headlines. Hubble is large. If nobody intervenes, it will eventually fall on its own schedule, along a path nobody chooses. Even the option of a safe farewell needs someone to come and get it.

What is actually on the table

NASA's request is a formal "request for information," a way for an agency to survey what industry could offer before it commits money. Here is what is known so far.

  • Three jobs, one request. NASA wants ideas for boosting Hubble to a higher altitude, for servicing it to improve its capabilities and for a controlled reentry or orbital disposal at the end of its life [1]. The request was issued on October 29 and made public a day later [1][8].
  • A handle built for robots. The 2009 servicing crew carried up a device called the Soft Capture Mechanism. It gives a future vehicle a standard docking interface plus navigation targets to aim at. Its stated purpose was to let a visiting spacecraft attach and steer Hubble to a safe, controlled descent [6].
  • A study that went nowhere, then came back. In September 2022, NASA and SpaceX signed an agreement to study whether a Dragon capsule could dock with Hubble and push it higher, at no cost to the government. Hubble was then about 335 miles up [4]. In June 2024, NASA said it would not pursue that plan for now, citing the risk of losing science early along with technical challenges [5]. The new request reopens the question to every company, not just one.
  • Robots have docked with old satellites before. In February 2020, Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicle latched onto the aging Intelsat 901 communications satellite. It was the first docking between two commercial satellites. The visitor then took over steering for its host [7].
  • A recent near miss. NASA paid Katalyst Space Technologies about $30 million to boost the Swift observatory. Control problems kept the company's spacecraft from reaching Swift, which was expected to reenter on November 4 [2]. IMAGINE told that story in The Rescue That Ran Out of Sky.

That last item explains the mood. Swift showed that a commercial rescue can be bought quickly. It also showed that money and good intentions do not guarantee a spacecraft arrives.

Whatever NASA chooses, a robotic visitor must first reach Hubble and latch onto the ring left in 2009 [1][6].

The danger of a helping hand

Here is the uncomfortable part. Hubble is not broken. It is a working observatory. Every contact with it carries risk.

When NASA turned down the SpaceX study in 2024, that was the core of its reasoning. A docking that went wrong could end Hubble's science sooner than drag ever would [5]. Imagine asking a tow truck to hook onto a priceless antique car while both are moving at orbital speed. The car might be saved. It might also be scratched beyond repair.

The ring itself raises questions. It was designed with a controlled descent in mind [6]. Whether it suits a long climb to a higher orbit, or the steady pushing that would take, is something any proposal will have to show.

Then there is the calendar, which is less certain than it looks. Estimates of when Hubble will fall have moved around:

  • NASA's current analysis says roughly 2033 to 2034 [1].
  • In 2024, NASA's calculations had Hubble staying up until at least 2035 [5].
  • An independent astronomer's plot, reported in February 2026, suggested reentry could come as early as 2028 if no boost happens [3].

These numbers are not necessarily contradictory. They are forecasts of drag. Drag depends on how active the Sun turns out to be. A stormy Sun pulls the date closer. A quiet one pushes it away. Nobody can say for sure how the Sun will behave over the next few years.

Finally, the request commits no money. It is a survey of what is possible [1]. The real test comes later, when NASA weighs price against risk and decides whether a rescue is worth more than a planned goodbye.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, grounded in the technologies described above. No company or agency has proposed them.

A tug that never leaves. The Mission Extension Vehicle showed that a visitor can latch on and stay, steering its host for years [7]. Picture a small robotic tug that docks to Hubble's ring and simply remains. Whenever the Sun swells the atmosphere, the tug gives a gentle nudge. Hubble gets an engine it was never built with.

A pit stop with fresh gyroscopes. Hubble was designed to be captured and serviced in orbit [3], though its past repairs were done by astronauts' hands. A dexterous servicing robot could, in principle, carry replacement gyroscopes and swap them in. It would be the most delicate repair job a machine has ever attempted in space. Getting it right would bring back a telescope that no longer has to ration its pointing.

Push now, goodbye later. One mission raises the orbit and buys years of extra science. A second, much later, returns to the same ring and steers the observatory into a planned descent over an empty stretch of ocean. The story ends on a date someone chose, not one the Sun chose.

Rehearsal on a retired satellite. Before touching a working treasure, a servicer could practice on an old, dead satellite that nobody minds risking. Every approach, latch and push would be tested where a mistake costs little. Only then would the robot fly to Hubble.

Handles on everything. The deepest lesson may be about the next generation. If Hubble's rescue works, satellite designers might fit standard docking rings and fuel ports to new spacecraft as a matter of routine. Rescue would stop being a heroic exception and become a scheduled service call.

Back to the ring

For seventeen years, that docking ring has ridden through every sunrise and sunset in orbit, waiting. It was a small act of foresight by people who knew they would not be the last visitors.

NASA's request turns that foresight into a live question. Can a commercial robot reach a working telescope, latch onto a handle installed nearly two decades ago and push without harming the precious instrument inside? Or is the wiser path to plan a calm, controlled goodbye?

Either way, the takeaway is the same. In space, how long something lasts is no longer fixed on launch day. It depends on whether anyone can come back for it.
