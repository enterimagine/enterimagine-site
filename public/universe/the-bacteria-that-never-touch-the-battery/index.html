The Bacteria That Never Touch the Battery

Waste sulfur from a Danish biogas plant, a flask of acid-making microbes and a shredded electric-car battery: a new experiment freed all the lithium in about an hour, at room temperature.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe6 min read

BiotechEnergySmart MobilityInnovation

Yellow powder, black dust

At a biogas plant in Denmark, a cleaning unit strips sulfur out of the gas before it can be used. What is left behind is usually treated as waste [1].

Some of it took a detour. It went to a laboratory in Austria, where it became dinner for two species of bacteria that live in some of the most acidic places on Earth. Over two to three weeks they turned the sulfur into sulfuric acid strong enough to reach a pH of 0.44 [1]. On the pH scale every step down means ten times more acidic, so this is far harsher than vinegar or lemon juice.

Then the microbes were left behind. Only their acid moved on, into a one-liter reactor holding "black mass," the dark powder that remains when used electric-car batteries are shredded.

The reactor was not heated. It sat at 22 °C, an ordinary room temperature. Within about an hour, all of the lithium and manganese in the powder had dissolved into the liquid, ready to be recovered [1].

The surprise is the arrangement. The bacteria never touched the battery. They worked as a tiny acid factory, fed on another industry's leftovers, while the hard chemistry happened next door.

Mining the junkyard

Electric cars are filling the roads. Their batteries will eventually wear out. The paper behind this experiment cites a projection of about 965 million electric vehicles by 2050 [1]. Many of these batteries hold lithium, nickel, cobalt and manganese, metals that otherwise have to be mined.

When a pack reaches the end of its road life, it is inspected. Packs that fail inspection go to recyclers, who shred them into black mass. Getting the metals back out of that powder is the hard part.

Today's main routes have trade-offs. Smelting runs at 1,200 to 1,500 °C. It recovers more than 99% of the cobalt and nickel, but the lithium ends up in the slag and needs extra treatment [2]. Chemical leaching dissolves the powder in strong acids made in chemical plants.

Biology offers a gentler path, called bioleaching: using microbes, or the acids they make, to dissolve metals. It has a long record in mining, where bacteria help pull copper from low-grade ore. Batteries are a harder meal, because the metals, solvents and binders in black mass can poison the microbes [1][3].

Why now? European rules are tightening. Under the EU Batteries Regulation, recyclers must recover 50% of the lithium from waste batteries by the end of 2027 and 80% by the end of 2031 [4]. New batteries will also need minimum shares of recycled lithium and cobalt from 2031 [4]. Any method that recovers lithium cheaply and cleanly has a deadline to meet.

What the experiments show

The August study, published in Frontiers in Microbiology, came from a team spanning Austria, Finland and Canada [1]. Here is what it found, along with related work.

  • Two separate steps. The design is "indirect." In step one, a 1:1 mix of Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans and Acidithiobacillus caldus fed on the biogas sulfur and made acid. Each batch took 14 to 21 days. Ten batches produced about 12 liters [1]. In step two, that acid met the black mass with no microbes present [1].
  • Real battery material. The black mass came from nickel-manganese-cobalt batteries of the type used in electric cars, supplied by a commercial recycler in Germany [1].
  • The results. With the powder making up 10% of the mixture, lithium and manganese dissolved completely in about an hour at 22 °C. Cobalt reached 61% after one hour and 67.5% after a day. Nickel reached 33% and then 36.6% [1].
  • Tuning. Adding hydrogen peroxide raised cobalt and nickel recovery by about 10 and 15 percentage points. Extra doses did not help further. Adding natural soap-like molecules made by microbes slightly lowered recovery [1].
  • Industry interest. The work was funded through Austria's COMET research program. Industrial partners include the carmaker AUDI, the steelmaker voestalpine and the recycler Saubermacher [1].
  • A mold from a distillery. In July, South Korea's National Nakdonggang Bioresources Institute reported a different biological route. It used Aspergillus luchuensis, the mold used to make Okinawa's awamori liquor, which secretes metal-dissolving organic acids [5][6]. The institute reports its mold broth recovered 92.4% of the lithium from electric-car battery powder, against 56.3% for sulfuric acid under its test conditions [5][6].
  • Direct contact is slower. In a Greek lab study where microbes worked directly on battery powder, six to seven days recovered 64% to 70% of the cobalt and 52% to 60% of the lithium. That was with the powder at only 1% of the mixture. At 2%, cobalt recovery fell to 35% to 45% [2].
  • Tougher microbes help. A July review of the field collected better results from hardier microbial teams, including 97.1% of the lithium and 96.1% of the cobalt at 5% powder [3]. It also found that cobalt and nickel are the metals most toxic to these microbes [3].
  • Start-ups are trying too. Cell Cycle, a UK start-up, says its engineered bacteria recover lithium, nickel and cobalt at about 37 °C. Those claims have not been independently verified [7].

Lithium was the easy part

The headline result, all of the lithium, is less dramatic than it sounds. In the Austrian study, runs without the peroxide boost also released all the lithium and manganese [1]. These metals dissolve readily in strong acid. The experiment mainly shows that acid made by microbes from waste does the job of acid.

The other metals are another story. Cobalt and nickel stayed between roughly a third and two-thirds recovered. Aluminum stayed below 40% [1]. A recycler would still need extra steps.

The experiment was also small. Runs were done in duplicate, with one scale-up to a single liter. No cost estimate or full life-cycle analysis was published. The authors say one is needed [1]. Making each batch of acid took two to three weeks, while smelting works in hours [1][2].

The Korean results come from an institute announcement, not a peer-reviewed paper. The mold process also ran at 80 °C for a full day [6]. A July review found that most bioleaching research on batteries is still at lab scale, with high powder loads especially under-studied [3]. Scale-up problems include toxicity, contamination and batch-to-batch variation [2][3].

So the fair summary is "bacteria made the acid," not "bacteria ate the battery." The idea is promising because it links two waste streams. It is not yet a factory.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below imagine where today's trends could lead. None describes a plant, product or partnership that any company or institute has announced.

The biogas neighbor. A battery recycler sets up next to a biogas plant. Sulfur that once left by truck as waste flows through a pipe into acid tanks. The recycler buys less acid made from mined sulfur.

The two-stage line. A recycling plant runs microbial acid for the easy metals, lithium and manganese, then sends the leftover powder to a short chemical step tuned for cobalt and nickel. Each stage does what it is best at.

The cold-room refinery. Because the leaching step works at room temperature, a small recycler in a cold climate runs it without big furnaces. Energy bills drop, though the slow acid batches mean many tanks working side by side.

The brewer's toolkit. Fermentation companies that already grow molds and bacteria at scale offer acid as a product. Recyclers order it the way bakeries order yeast, with strains chosen for the battery chemistry on hand.

The car that knows its recipe. Carmakers record the exact chemistry of each pack at birth. When a pack fails inspection years later, the recycler reads that record and picks the matching microbial acid and process before shredding begins.

Back to the yellow powder

Return to that Danish biogas plant. Its waste sulfur has always had to go somewhere. In this experiment it went into a flask of microbes and came out as acid that freed lithium from a dead car battery in about an hour, with no furnace involved [1].

The takeaway is that biology may not replace battery recycling chemistry, but it could quietly supply it. The win is in the connection: one industry's leftovers becoming another's raw material.
