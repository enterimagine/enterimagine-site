Who Sets Out the Triangles When No One Is Driving?

Driverless trucks already haul freight on Texas highways. A small federal rule about roadside warning triangles shows what it really takes to hand a big rig to software.

29 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart MobilityRoboticsAI & Data

Three triangles on the shoulder

There is a small ritual on America's highways that most motorists never notice. A big rig eases onto the shoulder, its hazard lights start to blink and the driver climbs down carrying three red reflective triangles. The driver sets them out at measured distances along the road, so that traffic coming up from behind has time to notice and change lanes. Federal rules give the driver ten minutes to finish the job [4].

Now picture the same truck stopping on Interstate 45 between Dallas and Houston, with nobody in the driver's seat. The hazards come on. Then what?

For years, that tiny question sat in the path of a very large change. Aurora Innovation began hauling customer freight with no driver aboard on that Texas corridor in May 2025 [1]. By September 2026 the company reported more than 500,000 driverless miles [2]. Yet the law still assumed that a person would walk back along the shoulder with the triangles.

In October 2026, regulators gave their answer. It says a lot about what handing a truck to software really involves: not only a clever digital driver, but a world of rules, chores and habits rebuilt around the absence of a human one.

A rulebook written for people

Trucking regulations are full of quiet assumptions about human bodies. A driver may drive at most 11 hours after 10 hours off duty and may not drive past the 14th hour of a shift [6]. Those limits exist because people get tired. Software does not, which is a big part of the appeal: Aurora says its trucks run at an annualized rate of more than 225,000 miles a year, more than double a conventional truck [2].

A rulebook written for people also assumes that people do the chores. Someone checks the rig, deals with the weigh station and walks the shoulder with warning devices. The federal warning-device rule requires three reflective triangles or at least six flares, placed at set distances behind and ahead of a stopped truck, as soon as possible and within ten minutes [4].

The stakes are human-sized. About 2.2 million people worked as heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers in the United States in 2025 [8]. Any technology that wants a place beside them has to prove it can handle the dull moments as safely as a person, especially the moment when something goes wrong and the truck must stop on a busy road.

Aurora's answer to the first big question, whether software can drive safely, was a "safety case": a documented argument, built from testing and analysis, that the system is acceptably safe for public roads. The company says it closed that case before going driverless and briefed federal safety agencies and Texas authorities [1]. The second question, who does the chores, turned out to be harder to settle with engineering alone, because the answer was written into law.

That is how a debate about plastic triangles became one of the most closely watched decisions in autonomous trucking.

On the road in Texas

Here is where things stand at the end of October 2026.

  • Driverless freight since 2025. Aurora started driverless deliveries between Dallas and Houston in the week of May 1, 2025. The company reports that it first logged more than three million supervised autonomous miles and over 10,000 customer loads [1].
  • Half a million miles, on time. The company reports passing 500,000 commercial driverless miles in September 2026, with every delivery on time since launch. It now also runs autonomously on the Dallas–Laredo corridor with Charger Logistics [3].
  • The triangle problem, settled for now. On October 7, 2026, the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) granted a five-year exemption that lets Level 4 automated trucks use amber beacons mounted on the cab instead of triangles [4]. Level 4 means the system drives itself within a defined area and set of conditions, with no human expected to take over. Other operators can use the exemption by notifying the agency first [4].
  • Strings attached. The beacons must flash at a different rate from the hazard lights, run on redundant power and switch on within five minutes of a stop, half the time a human gets. Operators must report crashes within five days and the exemption excludes hazardous materials and passengers [4].
  • Production lines, not prototypes. Aurora says its upfitting partner, Roush, aims to build 20 trucks a week by early December and that Volvo plans to deploy more than 300 driverless trucks in 2027 [3]. A rival, Kodiak AI, has named the same I-45 corridor as its driverless launch lane and targets driverless highway service by the end of 2026 [7].

Half a million miles is not the finish line

The mileage sounds enormous. It is less so once you think about rare events: a blowout in fog, a stall just past a blind curve, a trailer jackknifed across two lanes. Events like these may surface only after far more driving than any single fleet has done so far.

The beacon decision shows the tension clearly. FMCSA received 402 public comments [4]. Critics worried about trucks stopping near curves, a single electrical fault knocking out the lights and trailers swinging so that the beacons are hidden [4]. The Owner-Operator Independent Drivers Association argued that triangles and flares are more dependable because they do not rely on technology that can be disrupted [5].

The agency answered with conditions rather than a refusal. It pointed to waiver operations covering 34 trucks and more than 500,000 miles, with about 10 hours of beacon use and no reported malfunctions or collisions while stopped and to two 2022 studies, one by Aurora and one by the Virginia Tech Transportation Institute, that found drivers reacted to beacons about as well as to triangles [4]. Both sides would agree on one thing: the evidence is still young.

The economics are unsettled too. A technology chief at the carrier Werner said a clear view of the costs would take months and an insurer said autonomous trucks currently cost slightly more to cover than human-driven ones [7]. Meanwhile, federal labor statisticians still project truck driver employment to grow 4 percent from 2025 to 2035 [8].

There is one more twist. The hardest part may not be the open highway at all. Only in November 2026, Aurora says, will its trucks begin driving all the way into customer sites, including passing through security checkpoints [3]. The last few hundred meters, full of gates, yards and odd jobs, are where a missing human is felt most.

Two ways to warn the traffic behind you

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities grounded in today's technology. They are not announced plans of any company or agency.

The shoulder that talks. A stopped driverless truck could broadcast its position to navigation apps and connected cars, so drivers approaching a blind curve hear a lane warning long before they see any beacon. The warning would no longer depend on line of sight at all.

The night-shift corridor. Because software does not tire, long hub-to-hub lanes might shift more heavy freight into the small hours. Daytime traffic on city ring roads could ease, while trucks glide between depots on emptier highways.

The robotic pit crew. At transfer hubs on the edge of cities, inspection gantries could scan tires, brakes, lights and sensors before every run, the way a pit crew services a race car in seconds. The pre-trip walk-around, another chore built for humans, would become a job for robots and cameras.

The human relay. Driverless trucks might handle the long, straight highway stretches, while local drivers take trailers through neighborhoods, loading docks and tight city streets. For some drivers, that could mean more nights at home rather than fewer jobs, though nobody knows yet how the balance would settle.

A fleet that reads the weather. Driverless trucks already carry cameras and other sensors that could notice wet pavement, gusts and poor visibility. Shared with highway agencies in real time, a fleet could act as a moving weather network, warning human drivers of black ice minutes before they reach it.

Back to the shoulder

Return to that shoulder on I-45. The three triangles were never really about plastic. They were a promise that someone would look out for the traffic behind. In a driverless truck, that promise has to be engineered, tested and reported on, piece by piece and the October exemption is just one of those pieces.

The takeaway is that autonomy arrives less like a single breakthrough and more like a long checklist. Each human chore, from setting out warnings to rolling through a guard gate, has to be redesigned and each redesign earns its trust slowly, in public.

In The City That Parks Itself, robots stacked cars underground so that cities could hand land back. In The Robot in the Next Aisle, humanoids started working real factory shifts. Driverless trucks carry the same question onto the open highway: which human chores can a machine take over and how do we know it is safe?
