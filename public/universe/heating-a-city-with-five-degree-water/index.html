Heating a City With Five-Degree Water

Under southern Helsinki, a new plant pulls heat out of wastewater that is barely above freezing. Here is how a city learned to warm its homes with what goes down the drain.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

Smart CitiesEnergy

Forty-five meters down

Picture a tunnel in the bedrock under Munkkisaari, a waterfront district in the south of Helsinki. Through it flows water that has already been through the city's showers, kitchens and washing machines, then through a sewage treatment plant. By the time it gets here it is about 5 degrees Celsius, roughly as cold as the inside of a refrigerator [2].

On a winter morning, most people would call that water cold. A new plant, built about 45 meters underground and partly inside old rock caverns, treats it as fuel [1]. Since August 20, 2026, the Eiranranta heat pump plant has been squeezing warmth out of that chilly stream and sending it into the pipes that heat Helsinki's buildings [1].

The water is not even fresh from the drain. Before it reaches Eiranranta, another plant elsewhere in the city has already taken heat from it once [2][4]. Eiranranta takes what is left, then sends the water on to the Baltic Sea colder than it would otherwise be [1].

That is the surprise at the heart of this story. A city that burned coal until last year now plans to heat about 300 gigawatt-hours' worth of homes and offices each year from water most of us would not want to wash our hands in [1][5].

A northern city without coal

Helsinki has a cold winter and a big appetite for heat. The city leans on district heating: hot water made at central plants and pumped through insulated pipes under the streets into radiators and taps. Until recently, most of that heat came from burning coal [5].

That era ended on April 1, 2025, when the energy company Helen shut down its last coal-fired plant at Salmisaari [5]. Coal had supplied 64 percent of Helen's district heating production as recently as 2022 [5]. Closing the plant cut Helen's annual carbon dioxide emissions by half compared with 2024 and the city's emissions by about 30 percent, the company reports [5].

Something had to fill the gap. Helen's plan relies mainly on heat pumps that recover waste and environmental heat, plus electric boilers, energy storage and sustainable bioenergy [5]. Its longer goal is to stop producing energy by combustion altogether by 2040 [1].

A heat pump works like a refrigerator turned inside out. A fridge pulls heat from the food inside and dumps it out the back. A heat pump pulls heat from something cool, such as air, ground or water. It delivers that heat somewhere warmer, using electricity to do the lifting. The warmer the source, the less electricity it needs.

So a northern city hunting for heat has to look for anything warmer than the winter outdoors. Data centers are one such source. Sewage is another. It has a useful habit: it flows all year, day and night, because people keep showering, cooking and washing.

How the plant works

Eiranranta is not Helsinki's first attempt to mine its own wastewater. It is a second pass on a stream the city already taps:

  • The first pass, since 2006. Helen's Katri Vala plant sits under a park in the Sörnäinen district and has drawn heat from purified wastewater since 2006 [4]. It can deliver 165 megawatts of heat and 100 megawatts of cooling. Helen describes it as the world's largest heat pump plant producing both [4].
  • The second pass, from August 2026. After two stages at Katri Vala, the treated water returns to the wastewater tunnel at temperatures as low as about 5 °C [2]. Eiranranta's three heat pump units recover what remains, with roughly 90 megawatts of heating and 60 megawatts of cooling capacity [1]. The company reports annual output of about 300 gigawatt-hours of district heat, comparable to the yearly heating demand of the Finnish town of Rauma [1].
  • Heat and cold from one machine. Because a heat pump moves heat rather than making it, the cold side is useful too. Eiranranta also supplies district cooling, chilled water piped to buildings for air conditioning [1][2].
  • A backstop for the coldest days. When the water is too cold for the heat pumps to work well, a 30-megawatt electric boiler warms it [1][3].
  • The price tag. Helen put the total investment at about 100 million euros, with about 14.5 million euros in state energy investment aid [3]. Part of the funding came from the EU's NextGenerationEU recovery program [1][2]. Excavation started in January 2023 [2]. The plant was first expected at the end of 2025 [3] and was completed in August 2026 [2].
  • The climate claim. Helen expects the plant to cut its heat-production emissions by more than 80,000 metric tons of carbon dioxide a year compared with fossil heat [2][3]. That is a company estimate. Helen itself notes the claim was not assessed against a new EU directive on environmental claims [1][3].

Helsinki is not alone. In Hamburg, a 60-megawatt heat pump at the Dradenau sewage works was scheduled for completion in 2026, according to a 2025 announcement [6]. It is meant to draw on treated water that averages 12 °C through the year and lift it to 95 °C for up to 39,000 households [6].

Why the end of the pipe matters

There is a subtle reason Helsinki takes its heat where it does. Sewage is not just warm water. It is the food supply for the bacteria that clean it.

Treatment plants rely on microbes to break down pollutants. One crucial group, the nitrifying bacteria, turns ammonia into less harmful compounds. These microbes slow down in the cold. Chill the sewage on its way to the plant and they may not keep up.

That is not a hypothetical worry. A 2024 modeling study of a real sewer network in Bologna, Italy, tested what would happen if heat recovery cooled the wastewater by 5 °C before treatment [7]. In February, the average incoming temperature fell from 16.6 °C to 10.8 °C [7]. At plants that keep their bacteria for a short time, ammonia in the cleaned water rose and some daily values came close to the discharge limit used in the study [7]. Plants that hold their bacteria longer coped well. The authors recommend case-by-case checks for plants at the short end [7].

Helsinki avoids the dilemma by working downstream. Both Katri Vala and Eiranranta use water that has already been treated [2][4]. The bacteria finish their work first. The heat pumps take their turn after.

The second caveat is about electricity. A heat pump is only as clean as the power that drives it. It also works harder as its source gets colder. At Eiranranta, the coldest water sometimes needs the help of an electric boiler, which turns electricity into heat one for one, with none of a heat pump's leverage [1][3]. Helen says its electricity comes mainly from wind, nuclear, hydro and solar [5]. A city on a dirtier grid would get a smaller climate benefit from the same machine.

Finally, Eiranranta is a refinement, not a breakthrough in physics. Katri Vala has been doing the basic trick since 2006 [4]. What is new is how cold a stream engineers are now willing to work with.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE thought experiments. They extend the trends described above and are not plans announced by Helen, Helsinki or any other city.

The district designed around its drains. A new waterfront neighborhood is planned with its sewer, its heat network and its cooling network side by side from the first day. Every shower and dishwasher in the district feeds a small heat pump hall at its edge, so the area's heat never leaves the block where it was made. Residents pay for heat that was, in part, their own bathwater an hour earlier.

The heat map under the streets. A city fits temperature sensors along its main sewers and finds that some stretches run warmer than others, near laundries, hospitals or busy kitchens. Planners use that map to decide where the next heat pump should sit, the way they once chose sites for substations. A sewer upgrade and a heat upgrade become one construction project instead of two.

Summer cooling, winter heat, one pipe. In July, an office district sends its unwanted indoor heat into the same network that warmed it in January. The heat pumps switch roles, making chilled water for air conditioning and storing the surplus warmth for later, so the drain becomes a year-round partner rather than a winter one.

A cooler sea. A coastal city sets itself a target for the temperature of the treated water it releases. Heat that once leaked into the bay is now sold to homes. Marine biologists watch the shoreline near the outfall to see how local life responds to water that is a little closer to its natural temperature. The outfall becomes a place to measure success, not just a place to get rid of water.

The swap between server rooms and sewers. A data center and a wastewater heat plant share one heat network. On calm, cheap-power nights the heat pumps carry the load. When electricity is expensive, the servers' waste heat takes over. The city's heat comes from whichever source is cheapest and cleanest that hour. Operators trade heat the way grid controllers already trade electricity.

The warmth that was always there

Go back down the tunnel under Munkkisaari. The water still runs past at about 5 °C, still on its way to the sea [2]. What has changed is that the city now sees it differently. Every bath and every load of laundry carries a little energy away from a building. Helsinki has decided to catch some of it before it is lost.

There is no single heroic machine here. There is a cascade: houses warm the water, bacteria clean it, one plant takes some heat, a second plant takes more and the sea receives what is left. A smart city, in this sense, is one that keeps track of where its heat goes.

If this sounds familiar, it is a cousin of The Server Farm That Warms Your Living Room, where computers were the heat source. And the sewer has surprised us before, in What the Sewer Knew First, as an early warning system for disease.
