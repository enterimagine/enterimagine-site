The Satellite That Hides Behind the Moon

A spacecraft the size of a carry-on bag wants to hear hydrogen from before the first stars. To do it, it has to hide from all of us.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

SpaceInnovationAI & Data

A radio that listens in the dark

Imagine a spacecraft about the size of a small carry-on suitcase, circling the Moon [2]. For part of every orbit it can see Earth. To a radio receiver, our planet is a glowing ball of noise: broadcasts, radar, satellites and countless transmitters, all shouting at once.

Then the craft glides around the Moon's edge. The Moon slides between it and home like a hand cupped over an ear. The shouting stops.

For about 40 minutes of each two-hour orbit, the spacecraft sits in that silence [2]. It listens through a long antenna for something unimaginably faint: a radio whisper from hydrogen gas that filled the universe more than 13.5 billion years ago, before the first star had switched on [2].

This is CosmoCube, a mission concept led by the University of Cambridge with partners including Surrey Satellite Technology, RAL Space and BAE Systems. Its design appeared in the journal Nature Astronomy in August 2026 [1].

The surprising part is the address. To hear the oldest signal in the cosmos, a telescope does not need to be enormous. It needs to get away from us.

The chapter with no light

After the Big Bang, the universe cooled enough for the first atoms to form. Then it went dark. There was gas, mostly hydrogen, but no stars to light it. Astronomers call this stretch the cosmic dark ages.

It runs from the moment atoms formed, at a redshift of about 1,000, until the first stars appeared at redshifts of roughly 30 to 12 [1]. Redshift measures how much the expanding universe has stretched light since it set out. The bigger the number, the further back in time.

A telescope that collects starlight cannot study an era with no stars. Hydrogen, however, has a trick. Each atom can flip the tiny magnetic orientation of its electron, which emits or absorbs a radio wave 21 centimeters long. Think of it as one note that every hydrogen atom can hum. Hear the note across the sky and you can map where the gas sat and how warm it was [5].

The catch is what billions of years of cosmic expansion did to that note. It has been stretched into long radio waves at frequencies of just tens of megahertz [1][5].

From the ground, that band is nearly hopeless. Telescopes on Earth effectively cannot observe below about 45 MHz, which rules out the dark ages [1]. The ionosphere, the electrically charged upper layer of our atmosphere, absorbs and distorts these waves [3][11]. What gets through is buried under human-made interference.

There is also a clock running. As more missions head to the Moon, even its far side could fill with radio traffic [1][4]. The quiet window may not stay open for long.

What exists so far

CosmoCube is not in orbit yet. It is, however, more than a sketch on a napkin. Here is what is on record.

  • A peer-reviewed mission design. The concept was published as a Perspective in Nature Astronomy on August 14, 2026 [1]. Its authors come from Cambridge, the University of Portsmouth, RAL Space, Surrey Satellite Technology, BAE Systems plus two US universities [1].
  • Prototype electronics on the bench. The team's spectrometer prototype covers 10 to 100 MHz. It splits that range into 4,096 channels, each 62.5 kHz wide, while drawing about 5.45 watts [5]. The university's announcement gives the mission's target band as 10 to 50 MHz [2].
  • A built-in reality check. The receiver keeps switching between the sky and internal reference sources, a method called Dicke switching. That lets the team separate real cosmic signal from drift in its own electronics [2][5]. It works like re-zeroing a kitchen scale before every weighing.
  • An observing plan. The team reports that it aims to collect about 1,000 hours of data over roughly two years [2][4]. The press release says prototypes have passed laboratory and thermal tests. Funding so far has come from the UK Space Agency, the Science and Technology Facilities Council plus the Kavli Foundation [2].
  • Company on the far side. NASA and the US Department of Energy are sending LuSEE-Night to the far side aboard Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost Mission 2. It will listen below 50 MHz, with launch no earlier than 2027 [9]. China has proposed Hongmeng, a lunar-orbit array of one mother satellite plus six to nine daughters working together below 30 MHz [10][11].

The far side's silence is not just theory. In 2019, the Chinese satellite Longjiang-2, which flew alongside the Chang'e-4 mission, saw interference from Earth vanish each time it passed behind the Moon. The noise returned when it came back into view [10].

A whisper under a roar

Getting away from Earth solves one problem. It does not solve the hardest one.

Radio emission from our own galaxy is many orders of magnitude brighter than the hydrogen signal [2]. Picture trying to hear a pin drop at a rock concert, then proving which sound was the pin. The team plans to pull the two apart with Bayesian modeling, a statistical method that scores competing explanations by how well each one fits the data [2].

The second trap is the instrument itself. A spacecraft's own electronics can produce a small bump that looks just like a cosmic signal [2]. Calibration, not raw sensitivity, decides whether anyone believes a result.

This field has been burned before. In 2018 the EDGES experiment in Western Australia reported an absorption dip centered near 78 MHz. It pointed to gas about 180 million years after the Big Bang that seemed less than half as warm as expected [6].

In 2022, India's SARAS 3 radio telescope, floated on lakes in Karnataka, reported no evidence of that signal [7]. A Cambridge-led follow-up then used the non-detection to rule out some models of the first galaxies [8]. The EDGES claim has still not been independently confirmed [8].

There are practical caveats too. CosmoCube remains a proposal that needs more funding. The team has submitted it to a European Space Agency call for small missions, with a cost target under 50 million euros [2]. Coverage of the project suggests a launch within about five years, but no date is set [2][4].

Finally, the quiet may not last. Growing traffic to the Moon could bring its own interference to the very place these instruments rely on [1][4].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below imagine where far-side radio astronomy could lead. None of them describes an existing mission, agreement or installation.

A quiet zone on the Moon. Space agencies agree to keep part of the far side free of strong transmitters, the way some radio observatories on Earth sit inside protected quiet zones. Landers and relay satellites learn to whisper when they pass over it.

A flock of small listeners. Instead of one spacecraft, several suitcase-sized craft fly in loose formation. Each records the same sky from a slightly different spot. Comparing their notes lets scientists build a rough map of the ancient hydrogen rather than a single averaged curve.

Dark matter's fingerprint. The team already lists dark matter as a secondary science goal [2]. Imagine the hydrogen note turns out a little deeper or shallower than the standard picture predicts. That difference could become one of the cleanest tests of how invisible matter shaped the first clumps of gas.

Store in the shadow, send in the light. A far-side listener records while hidden. When it swings back into view, it hands its data to lunar relay satellites, which pass it to Earth. The quiet side does the listening; the near side does the talking.

A puzzle for algorithms. The raw data is released openly. Students around the world train machine-learning models to separate the galaxy's roar from the hydrogen whisper. The best ideas get tested against the team's own Bayesian pipeline.

Listening from the quiet side

Go back to that small craft gliding into the Moon's shadow. Each pass buys it about 40 minutes of near silence [2]. Over two years, those quiet slices are meant to add up to roughly 1,000 hours of listening [2].

If CosmoCube flies and its calibration holds, it could open the last unseen chapter of cosmic history using hardware that fits in an overhead bin. If it hears nothing, that silence will still rule out ideas, as SARAS 3 did for EDGES [8]. Either way, the lesson is the same: sometimes the best telescope site is the one that hides you from your own noise.
