Can a Neural Network Find the Enzyme That Eats Your Sneakers?

An algorithm and a compost heap are both hunting for enzymes that unmake polyurethane foam. Their results look worlds apart for a reason.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

AI & DataBiotechEnergy

The sneaker in the compost heap

Start with a running shoe that has done its last mile. Its springy sole is polyurethane foam, a plastic built to bounce back for years. Now picture a slice of that sole sitting in a warm flask with a pinch of protein borrowed from a bacterium that lives in compost. Three days later, a small share of the foam has quietly come apart [1].

That is the scene a team at Aarhus University in Denmark, working with the University of Porto, described in September 2026. Their enzyme came from a microbe found in Danish compost. Its sharpened version broke down about 1.4% of the polyurethane in shoe-sole foam in three days, with no pretreatment [1][2].

One and a half percent sounds tiny. It is. But almost a year earlier, a team in Beijing reported something that sounds like the opposite: an enzyme that enabled near-complete breakdown of commercial polyurethane at kilogram scale within eight hours [4]. The Beijing enzyme was not found by digging through compost. It was picked out by a neural network.

So which is the future of foam recycling: the patient microbe or the algorithm? The surprise is that they are solving different problems. The gap between them says a lot about what AI can and cannot do in biology.

The plastic that refuses to let go

Polyurethane is everywhere you sit, sleep and walk: mattress foam, sofa cushions, shoe soles, insulation panels. The world makes roughly 22 million tons of it a year, more than 5% of all plastics reaching the market [1]. Foams, flexible and rigid, make up an estimated 60 to 70% of that market [7].

Most of it has nowhere good to go. Polyurethane waste piles up in landfills [1]. One review describes its natural biodegradability as extremely low, with fragments accumulating in soil and water [7].

The chemistry explains why. Many polyurethanes are thermosets: their molecular chains are cross-linked into a single tangled network, like a fishing net knotted at every crossing [4]. You cannot simply melt a thermoset and remold it the way you can a soda bottle. The links holding the chains together, called urethane bonds, are also unusually stable [4].

Recyclers do have chemical options. One of the best known is glycolysis, which soaks the plastic in a glycol solvent so the polymer breaks into smaller molecules. But chemical and physical recycling routes carry costs of their own; the same review notes high energy use and secondary pollution as drawbacks [7].

That is where enzymes come in. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up one particular chemical reaction, like a key cut for one lock. Enzymes that cut urethane bonds, called urethanases, already exist. The problem is that the known ones work poorly under industrial glycolysis conditions [4]. They are keys that bend in the lock.

Finding a better one is a search problem. Nature holds an astronomical number of protein sequences. Testing them one by one in a lab is far too slow. That is exactly the kind of haystack where machine learning promises a magnet.

Two searches, two results

The recent results come from two very different strategies. Here is what has been reported.

  • The algorithm's pick. A team from Beijing University of Chemical Technology, with Chinese Academy of Sciences institutes and China Agricultural University, built a tool it calls GRASE: a graph neural network that recommends enzymes likely to be both active and stable [4]. A graph neural network treats a protein as a web of connected parts rather than a flat string of letters, which suits molecules well. The tool combines self-supervised learning (finding patterns in unlabeled data) with supervised learning (training on measured examples) [4].
  • A tough enzyme for a harsh bath. Its top pick, named AbPURase, showed activity two orders of magnitude higher than previously known urethanases in 6-molar diethylene glycol, a glycolysis solvent. The team reports near-complete depolymerization of commercial polyurethane at kilogram scale within eight hours [4]. The paper appeared in Science on October 30, 2025 [4]. The authors suggest a tightly packed water-repelling core and a stiffened lid loop help the enzyme survive the solvent [4].
  • The compost route. The Danish-Portuguese team took a different road. Its enzyme, CCPUR1, comes from the bacterium Chelatococcus composti. Guided by molecular-dynamics simulations, which model how a protein jiggles and flexes atom by atom, the researchers swapped a few amino acids for larger ones [1]. The best version degraded about 1.4% of shoe-sole polyurethane in three days under mild conditions [1][2]. The work appeared in Chem Catalysis on September 11, 2026, according to the Cell Press release [2].
  • How the compost hunt worked. Coverage of the team's screening describes 29 bacteria that attacked polyurethane or nylon to some degree, yielding 12 working enzymes: 10 active on polyurethane and two on nylon. A cell sorter could screen up to 10,000 bacteria a second [3]. Unmodified CCPUR1 had earlier broken down just under 1% of powdered mattress foam in three days [3].
  • A third approach. A team at Nanjing Tech University reported a laccase system (an oxidizing bacterial enzyme paired with a small helper molecule) that removed about 21% of the weight of a lab-made polyester foam over ten days. On commercial sofa-cushion foams, weight loss was only about 3 to 4% [7].

Eight hours versus three days is the wrong race

Put the two headline numbers side by side and the algorithm seems to win by a landslide: near-complete breakdown in eight hours against 1.4% in three days [1][4]. That comparison is misleading.

The Beijing enzyme works inside a chemical recycling bath. Its job is to survive and speed up glycolysis, a process that already uses a concentrated solvent to attack the plastic [4]. The Danish enzyme works on bare foam in mild conditions, without pretreatment [1]. One is a turbocharger bolted onto an existing chemical engine. The other is trying to be the engine itself. They are not competing for the same seat.

That distinction also clarifies what the neural network actually did. It did not invent new chemistry. It ranked candidate enzymes by how likely they were to be both active and stable in a harsh solvent [4]. That is valuable, the kind of triage that can spare a lab a great deal of trial and error. But the hard physical work still happens in a reactor.

There are honest gaps in what we can confirm. The Science abstract does not say how many candidates the network screened, what share of the plastic was recovered as usable chemicals or whether those chemicals went back into new foam [4]. Secondary coverage disagrees on details: one trade magazine reports eight hours [5], while a news summary of another outlet's report describes a dozen hours [6]. For the Danish work, one outlet names a different journal from the one in the official Cell Press release [2][3]. We follow the primary records here.

Real foams are also tougher than lab samples. In the Nanjing study, commercial cushion foams lost far less weight than the team's own foam, which the authors link to cross-linking and additives [7].

Finally, a caution on energy. Enzymes work at gentler conditions than many chemical routes, which is part of their appeal [1]. Whether that translates into lower energy use at industrial scale has not yet been shown.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built on the research described above. None of them describes an existing product, plant or commitment.

The enzyme-assisted glycolysis line. Picture a mattress recycling plant that already runs a glycolysis reactor. Instead of replacing its equipment, it adds a solvent-tough enzyme to the bath, the way a brewer adds a yeast strain. The same tanks process more foam per shift. The plant's managers judge success not by how fast the foam disappears but by how clean the recovered chemicals are when they leave.

Compost as a library. Every heap of hot compost holds microbes that have spent generations chewing on whatever we throw away. Picture regional teams sampling compost, landfills and insect guts, then feeding the enzymes they find into a shared model. The model gets better at predicting which proteins tolerate heat or solvent. Each new sample makes the next search shorter, the way every new map tile makes a navigation app a little smarter.

The sorting line that reads foam. Recycling yards struggle when they do not know which chemistry a cushion contains. Picture a sorting line where a camera and a spectrometer read each piece of foam while software routes it to the enzyme bath best matched to its type. Polyester foams go one way; polyether foams go another. Data on the way in decides which biology does the work on the way out.

Algorithms that learn from failure. Most enzyme candidates that a model suggests turn out to be duds. Imagine labs routinely publishing those failures alongside their successes, so the next network learns what does not work as well as what does. A shared record of dead ends would be dull to read. It might also be the most useful data set in the field. The haystack shrinks a little with every negative result.

Designing foam to be unmade. The deepest fix may sit at the start of the product's life. Picture chemists designing a new foam alongside the enzyme that will one day take it apart, testing both together before the first mattress ships. A shoe brand could do the same for its soles, knowing exactly which formulation it needs to unmake. The product would carry its own recycling key from day one, like a building designed with its own demolition plan.

Back to the compost heap

Return to that slice of shoe sole in its warm flask. After three days, most of it is still there. The compost enzyme is slow; its makers call the work "still in its infancy" [1]. Meanwhile, an enzyme chosen by a neural network has shown what happens when biology is asked to help a chemical process rather than replace it [4].

The takeaway is not that AI beat nature. It is that AI is becoming a very good scout. It can point to the rare protein that survives a harsh solvent. People still have to build the reactors, check what comes out and prove the loop closes.

We have watched this tension before. In Waiting on the Plastic-Eating Factory, we followed a bottle-eating enzyme born in compost on its slow road to an industrial plant. That article even imagined a future enzyme tuned for foam cushions. In The Enzyme That Needed a Leash, AI-designed gene editors ran into the limits of what labs can yet measure. Foam is the next test of both lessons.

Next time, we follow microbes into another hard recycling job: bacteria that could help recover metals from spent lithium-ion batteries.
