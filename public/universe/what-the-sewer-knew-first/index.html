What the Sewer Knew First

A city's sewage can warn of an outbreak days or even weeks before its clinics do. Here is how that hidden sensor works, what it cannot see and why its future is being decided right now.

1 November 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart CitiesBiotechAI & Data

The sample that spoke first

In the first week of August 2025, a routine sample of sewage left a treatment plant in Mesa County, Colorado. The plant serves about 90,000 people in and around Grand Junction. Since testing began there in May, the samples had been clean [1].

The sample collected on August 6 was not. It held about 944,000 copies of measles genetic material per liter. Genetic sequencing later confirmed a measles genotype called D8 [1].

At that moment, no doctor had reported a suspected case to the county's health agency. That report arrived on August 13, a week after the sewage sample was taken [1].

Then the next two samples were lost. A wildfire delayed their delivery to the lab, so they warmed beyond the temperature range that keeps results valid [1]. By the time the outbreak was traced, investigators had followed up 225 contacts and confirmed seven cases [1].

The story holds the whole promise of wastewater surveillance in a few lines. A city's plumbing can notice a disease before its clinics do. It can also go quiet for the most ordinary reasons, from a wildfire to a lapsed contract.

A national sensor on a short lease

Wastewater surveillance means testing sewage for traces of pathogens that people shed when they are infected, often before they feel sick and whether or not they see a doctor. One sample from a treatment plant speaks for everyone connected to it, a catchment engineers call a sewershed.

In the United States, this sensor network grew out of the COVID-19 pandemic. As of mid-2026 the national program counted about 1,300 sampling sites covering roughly 144 million people [2]. It was built on emergency funding from 2020 and 2021 rather than a dedicated, permanent budget line [2].

That made autumn 2026 a test. On September 28 the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) awarded a new national wastewater testing contract to Verily Health. The agency's own pages warn of a brief gap in data affecting about 200 sites while testing moves over [3]. A similar handover in 2023 left hundreds of sites without data for several weeks [4].

The money question is also open. The administration's budget proposal would cut the program from about $125 million to about $25 million a year [5]. Advocates have asked Congress for at least $120 million in the fiscal year that began in October. A proposed bill would set aside $150 million a year through 2030 [2]. Several states, including Colorado, Massachusetts, North Carolina, Ohio, Utah and Washington, have budgeted to keep testing into 2027 or beyond [2].

How this resolves is a policy decision. What the science shows is the value of the signal itself.

What's real today

The evidence that sewers can serve as early-warning sensors has grown quickly.

  • A two-month head start on measles. In Oregon, scientists retested archived sewage after a 2024 outbreak. They found measles in a sample from April 3, about ten weeks before the first known patient fell ill on June 11. Twenty of 94 samples came back positive during an outbreak of 30 cases [6].
  • Polio with no patient. In 2022, poliovirus turned up repeatedly at the Beckton sewage works, which serves close to four million people in London. No clinical case was ever found. By moving sampling upstream, officials narrowed the vaccination response to eight local areas. England now samples 28 sites a month instead of two [7].
  • A panel of 16 targets. WastewaterSCAN, an academic program run by Stanford, Emory and Verily, samples the settled solids from plants serving more than 10,000 people. Its labs measure targets including COVID-19, flu, RSV, norovirus, hepatitis A, measles, mpox, West Nile virus and a drug-resistant fungus [8]. The CDC dashboard now tracks influenza A, COVID-19, RSV, measles, H5 avian influenza and mpox, updating every Friday [9].
  • Europe makes it the norm. The European Union's revised urban wastewater directive asks each member state to set up coordination between health and water authorities on what to monitor, where and how often. It also adds monitoring of antimicrobial resistance (germs that survive the drugs meant to kill them) at large treatment plants [10]. Spain opened a public consultation in June 2026 on a national system with a shared data platform [11].
  • A planet-wide sampling day. Researchers have announced Global Wastewater Day for March 31, 2027, with more than 31 countries signed up to sample on the same date. Targets include resistance genes, pathogens, medicines and chemicals [12].

The lab chemistry is less exotic than it sounds. Most programs use a method called digital PCR, which splits a sample into thousands of tiny droplets and counts the ones that light up when a target gene is present [8]. To correct for how diluted each sample is, labs compare the result with a harmless plant virus from peppers that people eat and shed in large amounts [8]. Think of it as checking the volume of one voice against the steady hum of the whole room.

A newer approach, called metagenomics, reads all the genetic material in a sample rather than searching for a short list. One study of sewage from 62 cities on six continents found more than 2,500 viral species [13]. Sorting through data at that scale is where data science enters the pipe, with machine learning increasingly part of the toolkit.

What the sewer cannot tell you

For all its promise, a sewer is a blurry sensor. It is excellent at saying "something is spreading here" and poor at saying how many people are sick.

The reason is biology. People shed very different amounts of virus depending on the pathogen, the stage of illness and the person, so the CDC cautions that wastewater cannot easily estimate case counts [14]. Weather muddies it further. A storm can dilute samples, especially in older systems where rainwater and sewage share pipes. A national expert panel found there is still no consensus on the best way to correct for dilution [15].

Not every signal is human, either. Avian influenza in sewage can come from birds, farm animals or milk poured down a drain. A positive result does not reveal the subtype [3]. Rural homes on septic tanks are poorly covered by the network [14].

Then there is privacy. The method works because it pools thousands of people. Shrink the sewershed to one building and that anonymity thins. The CDC dashboard limits its public data to plants serving more than 3,000 people for that reason [15]. Policy researchers have pointed to a case where a rare COVID-19 strain was traced to a small workplace, perhaps a single person [16].

The fairest reading is that wastewater is a smoke detector, not a diagnosis. It tells a health department where to look. It works only if someone keeps the batteries in.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

These scenarios are speculation by IMAGINE, not announced plans by any agency, city or company.

The neighborhood health forecast. A city could publish sewer readings beside its weather report, giving residents a weekly sense of which infections are rising so clinics can plan staffing ahead of a surge. Aggregated to large sewersheds, it would inform without singling anyone out.

Treatment plants as data centers for health. Plants already measure flow, temperature and chemistry around the clock. Adding automated samplers and on-site genetic testing could turn a weekly snapshot into a daily pulse, with results streamed to public health teams before a courier van would have reached the lab.

A global watch that never sleeps. If coordinated sampling days become routine, the world could compare the same targets across dozens of countries on the same morning. A new pathogen appearing in several port cities at once would stand out against that shared baseline.

Resistance maps for hospitals. Monitoring antimicrobial resistance genes in sewage could help hospitals see which drug-resistant germs are circulating in their region. That could inform infection-control planning long before resistant cases fill a ward.

Machine learning listens for the unknown. Metagenomic data could be scanned by algorithms trained to flag genetic sequences that do not match anything familiar. The goal would not be to replace scientists but to point them toward the one strange signal hidden among thousands of ordinary ones.

Back to Grand Junction

Return to the sample that left the Mesa County plant in August 2025. It carried a warning a week ahead of the first suspected case. Then a wildfire interrupted the chain. The sensor fell silent at the worst moment.

That pairing is the lesson. The science of reading a city's health from its sewage now works well enough to change how outbreaks are found. What decides its value is everything around the science: steady sampling, reliable couriers and labs, careful privacy rules and funding that outlasts an emergency.

Earlier in the series, The Germs That Know Where the Pipes Will Fail showed how microbes in sewers can reveal the health of the pipes themselves. This article turned that lens on the people upstream.

Next, we board a plane. The CDC already samples wastewater from arriving aircraft at several major international airports [17]. One modeling study suggests this kind of sampling could spot a new respiratory threat weeks earlier [18]. We will ask what flying toilets can teach the world about the next outbreak.
