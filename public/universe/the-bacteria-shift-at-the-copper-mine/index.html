The Bacteria Shift at the Copper Mine

In the Arizona desert, acid-loving microbes are being sprinkled onto mountains of crushed rock to win back copper that machines left behind.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

BiotechEnergyAI & Data

Barrels from Denver

In the Arizona desert, a copper mine is running out of time.

The Carlota mine, owned by the Polish copper producer KGHM, now turns out roughly a third of what it did at its peak [1]. Unless something changes, it is scheduled to close by the end of the decade [1]. Its leftover ore sits in heaps of crushed rock, some as large as ten sports stadiums [1].

So the miners have called in an unusual crew. Barrel-sized containers of microbes arrive from a laboratory in Denver run by a young company called Endolith [1]. On site, the microbes multiply in tanks called bioreactors. Then they are mixed with dilute sulfuric acid and sprinkled over the rock through drip lines, like a garden irrigation system for stone [1].

The bacteria eat the rock, in a manner of speaking. Months later, the liquid that drains out of the bottom of the heap will show whether they have freed enough copper to give the old mine a longer life.

The surprising part is not that microbes can mine metal. They already do. The surprise is that companies are now breeding microbe teams to order, then steering them with DNA sequencing and artificial intelligence.

The metal behind every wire

Copper is the quiet workhorse of the energy transition. It carries current through power lines, motors, transformers and chargers. Electric vehicles need three to four times as much copper as cars with combustion engines, according to BloombergNEF figures cited by Bloomberg [1].

Data centers are adding their own pull. In a January 2026 study, S&P Global projected world copper demand of 42 million metric tons a year by 2040, up about 50% [4]. It expects demand linked to AI and data centers to roughly triple over that period [4]. Yet it sees mine production peaking at 33 million metric tons in 2030, leaving a shortfall of about 10 million metric tons by 2040 [4].

Opening new mines is slow. S&P Global puts the average time from discovery to production at about 17 years [4].

That makes old mines interesting again. Many sit on rock with too little copper to process the usual way, which involves grinding the ore, concentrating it and shipping it to a smelter. A separate study in Science found that ore at US metal mines also holds many other critical minerals that are rarely recovered [5]. Squeezing more value out of rock that has already been dug up is suddenly worth real effort.

Microbes offer one way to do it. They work slowly, but they work at the scale of a mountain, without grinding every rock to powder.

Microbes on the payroll

Here is the state of play, with company claims flagged.

  • Bioleaching is already big. An estimated 20% of the world's mined copper already comes from bioleaching, according to an MIT source cited by Bloomberg [1]. Early versions go back roughly 2,500 years in China. Rock-eating bacteria were identified in the 1940s [1].
  • The chemistry is simple, the biology is not. One classic species, Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans, gains energy by changing iron from one chemical form to another [9]. The iron it produces attacks copper-bearing minerals, while the microbe's processing of sulfur generates acid [9]. It grows best in very acidic conditions near 30 °C [9]. It also works slowly [9].
  • Teams, not soloists. Endolith uses communities of several to dozens of microbial strains rather than a single species [1]. The company says it trains them to survive heat, strong acid and toxic metals [1]. That training happens in glass tubes packed with ground ore, where communities compete for months before the tubes are cut open and analyzed [1].
  • AI speeds up the lab. The company reports that DNA sequencing, cloud computing and AI now return results in about 24 hours instead of months [1]. Endolith's general goal is at least 10% more copper from low-grade ore, though it gives no estimate for any single project and describes the work as early-stage [1].
  • A field trial is coming. A demonstration on a commercial-scale heap at Carlota was expected to begin within months of October 2026 [1]. The mining giant BHP has validated Endolith's laboratory results, according to the company's 2025 funding announcement [8].

Meanwhile, a different route to the same goal has already produced metal. Nuton, a heap-leaching technology from Rio Tinto, made its first copper at the Johnson Camp mine in Arizona in November 2025 [2]. The partners report 99.99% pure copper cathode straight from the mine site, with no smelter in between [2]. Amazon Web Services signed on as Nuton's first customer in January 2026, for copper used in US data center components [3].

From heap to cathode

Slow, wet and hard to steer

Microbial mining has a reputation problem that is partly deserved. It is slow, it is messy and it is hard to control.

A heap is not a lab tube. Once a mountain of rock is stacked and wetted, changing what happens deep inside is very difficult. A researcher at the Colorado School of Mines told Bloomberg that controlling a heap is "almost impossible" [1]. A Cornell researcher said next-generation bioleaching still has a long way to go [1].

The economics are open too. Endolith claims its approach can pay for itself in under a year, while Bloomberg's reporting notes that the economics remain unproven at scale [1]. Nuton's performance claims, including up to 85% recovery and up to 80% less water than conventional processing, are still awaiting multi-year testing and third-party verification [2].

Even the carbon numbers need care. Rio Tinto reports a full footprint for Nuton copper of 2.82 kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilogram of copper, counting supply-chain emissions [3]. A narrower "mine-to-metal" figure is 0.82 kilograms [2]. Both compare with a global range of roughly 1.5 to 8.0 kilograms for newly mined copper [3]. Which number you quote changes the story.

There is also a difference between "engineered" and "trained." A funding announcement described Endolith's microbes as engineered [8]. Bloomberg describes communities adapted under stress [1]. Nothing we found confirms that genetically modified microbes are being used in the field.

Finally, biology cannot fetch everything. A UK government roundtable heard that microbial methods suit copper, nickel, zinc and some rare earths better than lithium or graphite [6]. Barriers include scale-up, thin margins and regulation [6]. Cornell researchers noted in 2024 that no known microbes were yet suitable for commercial mining of magnet metals such as neodymium [7].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are imagined by IMAGINE. They are not products, projects or announcements by Endolith, KGHM, Rio Tinto, BHP or Amazon Web Services.

A microbe recipe for every mountain. Each ore body has its own minerals, temperature and water chemistry. A future lab could test a sample of rock against a library of microbe communities, then ship a custom blend to the mine, the way a winemaker picks yeast for a particular grape.

Heaps with a nervous system. Sensors buried in a heap could track temperature, acidity and dissolved metal at many depths. A machine learning model could then suggest where to add more acid, more water or a different microbe blend, turning a black box into something closer to a managed field.

Data centers built on rescued copper. If the copper in server halls came from ore that was already dug up, a data center could point to a shorter, lower-impact supply chain. That link between computing and microbial mining could become a selling point for buyers who track their materials.

A second harvest from old waste. Many old mining sites hold tailings, the fine waste left after processing. Microbial communities tuned to those materials could recover copper along with other critical minerals that were once thrown away, while also helping to stabilize the waste.

Biology meets battery recycling. Batteries that fail inspection go to recycling. Microbes trained on crushed battery material might one day help pull out copper, nickel and cobalt at lower temperatures than furnaces need.

Back at the heap

Picture the Carlota heap again. The drip lines tick. Underground, countless cells are working through the rock, changing iron from one form to another, making acid, loosening copper.

Nobody yet knows how much more metal this mine will give up [1]. That uncertainty is the real headline. Biology already supplies a fifth of the world's mined copper [1]. The open question is whether designed microbe teams, guided by sequencing and AI, can push that share higher without new pits.
