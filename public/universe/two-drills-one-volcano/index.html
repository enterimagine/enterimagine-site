Two Drills, One Volcano

Two companies are drilling into the same Oregon volcano, racing toward the temperature where water stops behaving like water.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

InnovationEnergy

Two rigs, one target

Picture a drilling rig on the pine-covered flank of a volcano in central Oregon. Its bit is grinding through rock about two miles down, where the stone is hotter than a kitchen oven at full blast [3].

On the same volcano, a second company is drilling a well of its own. It wants to go deeper still [6].

The volcano is Newberry, south of Bend. The companies are Mazama Energy and Quaise Energy. They use different tools, but both are chasing the same number: about 374 degrees Celsius, or roughly 705 degrees Fahrenheit [11].

Above that temperature, at enough pressure, water stops behaving like water. It is no longer a liquid. It is not quite steam either. It becomes a supercritical fluid, a strange in-between state that flows like a gas while carrying heat like a liquid.

That in-between state could change what a single geothermal well is worth. Advocates argue that one well tapping rock this hot could deliver several times the energy of a conventional well [4].

Nobody has yet run a power plant on it. This year, the race to be first moved from slide decks to drill floors.

Why hotter rock matters

Grids running on sun and wind still need power that shows up at 3 a.m. on a windless winter night. Engineers call that firm power. Geothermal energy, heat drawn from inside the earth, is one of the few clean sources that can run around the clock [4][5].

The trouble is geography. Conventional geothermal plants tap fluids at roughly 150 to 200 degrees Celsius [4]. That mostly limits them to places where hot water sits close to the surface, such as Iceland or parts of the American West.

Superhot rock flips the approach. Instead of hunting for natural hot springs, you drill deep enough to reach rock above about 400 degrees Celsius, the threshold the Clean Air Task Force uses for the term [4]. At those temperatures, water pumped down can return in the supercritical state.

The prize is energy density. The Clean Air Task Force, a nonprofit that advocates for the technology, projects that a superhot well could produce five to ten times the energy of a typical commercial well [4]. Mazama makes a similar claim: it says a 400-degree well could deliver up to ten times the power of a conventional 200-degree well [2][3]. These are projections, not measured results.

Why would hotter be so much better? Hotter fluid carries more heat per kilogram. It also turns that heat into electricity more efficiently. Fewer wells for the same power would mean less drilling, with rigs that cost more than $100,000 a day to run [1][2].

The idea is drawing official attention. According to the Clean Air Task Force, the International Energy Agency featured superhot rock in its State of Energy Innovation 2026 report, released in March [5].

What's happening on the volcano

Here is what has actually been drilled, measured or announced. Most of the newest figures come from the companies themselves, so they are flagged as such.

  • A record, by the company's count. In October 2025, Mazama reported a bottom-hole temperature of 331 degrees Celsius at Newberry, about 10,200 feet down [3]. It built an enhanced geothermal system there: water is pumped down one well, through cracks opened in hot rock, then back up a second well. The company calls it the hottest system of its kind so far [3].
  • Faster wells, bigger plans. Mazama reports that its second well reached the same depth about 80% faster than its first [1][2]. Its next step, Project Ceres, uses horizontal wells and could yield about 15 megawatts, the company says, with drilling due in late 2026 and testing in early 2027 [2]. A later phase aims below 15,000 feet for hotter rock [1].
  • A rival with a different drill. Quaise is developing a drill that uses millimeter-wave energy, a cousin of microwaves, to vaporize rock instead of grinding it. In July 2025, its hybrid rig bored 118 meters into granite at a Texas quarry, the company reports [7].
  • A confirmation well at Newberry. In July 2026, Quaise began drilling a confirmation well for its Project Obsidian, aiming for about 15,000 feet and roughly 400 degrees Celsius [6]. Its first phase plans seven wells and more than 50 megawatts by 2030. The US Department of Energy selected the project for up to $25 million in support [6].
  • Iceland has already been there. In January 2017, the Iceland Deep Drilling Project's IDDP-2 well at Reykjanes reached 4,659 meters along its slanted path. It measured 426 degrees Celsius at the bottom with fluid pressure of 34 megapascals [8]. That is a peer-reviewed result.

Iceland has an even wilder data point. In 2009 its IDDP-1 well at Krafla drilled straight into magma. During flow tests it proved about ten times more powerful than an average well in that field, before corrosion ended its run [9].

Governments are paying attention too. The IEA report also highlighted a US ARPA-E program on superhot rock plus a cooperation agreement between New Zealand and Iceland, according to the Clean Air Task Force [5].

Iceland has measured rock above the supercritical line, but the hottest enhanced system reported so far sits just under it [3][8][11].

The heat is the easy part

Start with the scoreboard. Mazama's 331 degrees is impressive, but it is still below the 374-degree critical point of water [3][11]. No commercial superhot rock power plant operates anywhere yet [10]. Canary Media sums up the field as "emerging and largely unproven" [2].

Then there is the plumbing. Superhot fluids can be brutally corrosive. Iceland's magma well showed how fast ordinary equipment can fail in those conditions [9]. The Clean Air Task Force lists cements, drill bits and downhole tools that survive extreme heat among the unsolved problems [4].

Depth is another wall. Away from volcanic regions, the group estimates that reaching superhot rock may require drilling to around 15 kilometers. Today's methods top out around 7 kilometers [4]. Quaise's millimeter-wave drill is meant to close that gap, but by the end of 2025 its field tests had reached about 100 meters [7].

Earthquakes deserve a straight answer. Enhanced geothermal systems work by opening cracks in rock [3]. In 2017, a magnitude 5.4 earthquake struck Pohang, South Korea. It is believed to have been linked to a nearby geothermal project [10]. That does not mean every project carries the same risk. It does mean careful monitoring is part of the job.

Finally, the timelines. Every megawatt figure and every date between 2027 and 2030 in this story is a company projection [1][2][6]. The rock will decide which of them hold.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below imagine where superhot rock geothermal could lead. None of them describes an existing product, project or installation.

The coal plant that keeps its turbines. A retired coal plant still has its turbine hall, its grid connection and its skilled crew. Instead of burning coal, it takes in steam from superhot wells drilled nearby. The smokestack comes down; the switchyard stays busy.

A data center on the volcano's doorstep. A cluster of servers sits beside a superhot field, drawing round-the-clock power without long transmission lines. Its waste heat warms greenhouses in the valley below.

The drill floor runs itself. Robotic pipe handlers do the heavy lifting on the rig. Software reads temperature and vibration sensors deep in the hole, then adjusts the drilling in real time. Human crews supervise from a control room instead of standing next to spinning steel.

A map of hidden heat. Satellite measurements of ground deformation, combined with old oil-well temperature logs, feed models that flag where superhot rock sits closest to the surface. Developers drill where the map glows brightest.

Geothermal by the kit. After a few dozen wells, superhot drilling becomes a repeatable product rather than a one-off science project. Standard well designs, standard casings and standard turbines make each new site faster than the last.

Back on the volcano

Those two rigs on Newberry's flank are running the same experiment from different directions. One is pushing a familiar drilling method hotter and deeper [1][3]. The other is betting on a new way to cut rock altogether [6][7].

The target is fixed by physics: 374 degrees, the line where water changes character [11]. Iceland proved that wells can cross it [8]. What nobody has proved yet is that a well can cross it, survive the conditions and pay its way as a power plant. The companies' own timelines point to first answers between 2027 and 2030 [1][2][6].
