The Pig Kidney That Held a Place in Line

A gene-edited pig kidney kept a man off dialysis for 271 days, then stepped aside for a human one. The first bridge between species changes what success looks like for animal-to-human transplants.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

BiotechInnovation

Three kidneys, one summer on the pond

This past summer, a man in his late sixties spent his days at a cabin by a pond in New England, swimming, kayaking and riding his bike [4]. For someone whose own kidneys had failed, that is already a good story. What makes it remarkable is the route he took to get there.

His kidneys were destroyed by diabetes and high blood pressure. He needed frequent dialysis [4], the machine treatment that filters the blood when the kidneys no longer can. He also had a rare blood type, which made a matching human donor unlikely to arrive in time [4].

In January 2025, surgeons at Mass General Brigham in Boston gave him a kidney grown in a pig whose genes had been edited to make the organ more acceptable to a human body [2][4]. It worked at once [2]. For 271 days he lived without dialysis, the longest stretch yet recorded for a pig kidney in a living person [2].

Then the pig kidney began to fail and was removed. In January 2026 he received a kidney from a deceased human donor, which also worked immediately [2].

The surprise is in that sequence. The pig organ did not have to last forever to succeed. It had to hold his place in line.

This article explains published research. It is not medical advice.

The line that never gets shorter

The waiting list is the heart of this story. In the United States, 109,817 people were on the national organ transplant waiting list as of July 30, 2026 [7]. Kidneys are the organ most in demand [7]. About 90,000 people are waiting for a kidney alone, according to figures from the United Network for Organ Sharing cited by the Associated Press [4]. Every day, about 12 people die waiting for an organ of any kind [7].

More than 49,000 transplants were performed across the country in 2025 [7]. That sounds like a lot until it is set against the list. So far, human donors have not closed the gap. Some patients, like those with rare blood types, face especially long odds [4].

For them, the alternative is dialysis, which keeps people alive but is hard on the body and on daily life. The Boston team's lead author, the kidney specialist Leonardo Riella, put the message he hears from patients bluntly: life on dialysis "is no life" [4].

This is where xenotransplantation comes in. The word means transplanting an organ from one species into another. The donor animals in today's studies are pigs [3][6]. The obstacle is the human immune system, which treats a pig organ as an invader.

Why now? In September 2026, the Boston team published the first report of a patient moving from a pig kidney to a human one, in The Lancet [1][2]. The company that supplied the kidney released updated results the same day [3]. Formal clinical trials are underway or about to start [3][6].

What the editing changed

Making a pig kidney survive in a person is mostly a problem of disguise. The kidneys used in Boston come from eGenesis, a company based in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Its kidney, called EGEN-2784, carries three kinds of genetic changes, the company reports [3]:

  • Three sugar tags removed. Pig cells carry sugar molecules on their surface that human antibodies recognize instantly, like a foreign flag. Deleting the genes for three of these tags is meant to prevent hyperacute rejection, the fast and violent attack that destroys an unmodified pig organ [3].
  • Seven human genes added. These help calm the immune response, reduce inflammation, keep blood clotting in step with human biology and restrain the complement system, a part of immunity that punches holes in foreign cells [3].
  • Old viruses switched off. The pig genome carries fragments of ancient viruses, known as porcine endogenous retroviruses. These are inactivated to reduce the risk of passing anything to the patient [3]. No pig pathogen transmission was detected in the Boston patient [2].

Here is what has happened so far, according to the published report and the company:

  • Five patients since March 2024. All received EGEN-2784 kidneys at Mass General Brigham under the FDA's expanded access pathway, which allows experimental treatment for individual patients outside a formal trial [3][4].
  • Months without dialysis. Three of the five lived more than eight months without dialysis [3]. One has passed nine months and remains on the study, the company reports [3].
  • Two bridges. Two patients have since moved on to human donor kidneys, the first reported transitions of this kind [3]. The Boston patient received his human kidney 82 days after the pig kidney was removed, the company says [3].
  • An early loss. The first pig kidney transplant into a living person, done at the same hospital in 2024, ended when the patient died after 52 days of heart problems unrelated to the transplant [2][5].
  • Trials ahead. The FDA has cleared eGenesis to run a combined phase 1/2/3 trial in 33 patients aged 50 to 70 who are on the waiting list, expected to start in early 2027 [3]. A rival company, United Therapeutics, announced the first transplant in its own trial, EXPAND, at NYU Langone Health in November 2025. Its pig kidney carries ten gene edits: six human genes added and four pig genes removed [6].

What a failure can teach

It would be easy to tell this as a story of triumph. The truth is more interesting. The pig kidney did eventually fail. How it failed matters.

About six months after the transplant, doctors reduced the patient's immune-suppressing drugs because of an infection [2]. The kidney later developed injury and inflammation in its smallest blood vessels, which progressed until it stopped working [2]. News reports also describe blood clots forming in its vessels [4]. That trade-off, between suppressing the immune system enough to protect the organ and not so much that infections take hold, is a familiar problem in transplant medicine. Pig organs do not escape it.

The bigger worry going in was sensitization. A failed transplant can leave the immune system primed against foreign tissue, which can make finding a compatible human kidney later even harder. If pig kidneys did that, using them as a bridge could backfire. So far, the evidence is reassuring but thin. The Boston team found no sign of sensitization after the human transplant [2]. eGenesis says a second patient's experience points the same way [4].

Two cases are not a rule. Everything here rests on a handful of patients. Long-term safety is not established [2][4]. Riella himself describes pig kidneys today as a potential bridge, with a true replacement for human organs perhaps 10 to 15 years away [4].

There is also a quieter point. A bridge only helps if a human kidney waits on the other side. On its own, it does not shorten the line. It keeps people healthier while they stand in it. Ending the shortage still requires pig organs that last as long as human ones, which is the stated long-term goal [4].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE thought experiments. No hospital, company or regulator has announced them. None describes a treatment available today.

The transplant you can put in a calendar. A kidney from a deceased donor arrives whenever a donor dies, often with a phone call in the middle of the night. A pig kidney could be scheduled like a knee replacement. In this future, a patient on dialysis picks a date for a bridge transplant, keeps a place on the human waiting list and goes home weeks later without the machine. The waiting list stops being a lottery of timing and becomes a plan with two stages.

Bridges for the hardest to match. Some patients wait longest because of rare blood types or immune systems primed by earlier transplants or pregnancies. Transplant centers begin offering them a pig kidney as a planned first step, buying months or years of better health while the search for a human match goes on.

Herds as hospital infrastructure. Donor pigs are raised in sealed facilities near major transplant centers, tested and documented like a pharmaceutical production line. Each animal's genome, health record and organ journey can be traced from birth to the operating room. Inspectors audit the herds the way they audit sterile drug factories today.

Other organs take the bridge. A heart patient too sick to wait receives a gene-edited pig heart to keep going until a human heart is available. A patient with sudden liver failure has their blood filtered outside the body through a pig liver for a few days while their own liver recovers. Each organ brings its own immune puzzle, so each bridge is developed separately.

Editing for the recipient. Instead of one standard set of changes, donor pigs come in several genetic versions. Doctors choose the version whose edits best fit a particular recipient's immune profile, much as they now compare tissue types between human donors and patients.

Holding the place

Go back to the pond. The man kayaking across it carries a human kidney now. The pig kidney that came before it is gone. Yet it did its job. It gave him the better part of a year off dialysis and delivered him, apparently no worse for the experience, to the transplant he had been waiting for [2].

That reframes the goal. For decades, the dream of xenotransplantation was a pig organ that could last a lifetime. That dream is still the destination. In the meantime, a pig kidney that lasts long enough may already be worth having, if the trials starting now confirm what a handful of patients have shown [3][6].

Readers of Medicine for One will recognize the tool at the center of this story: the same gene-editing methods that can fix a single patient's mutation can also redesign an animal's organ to suit a human body.
