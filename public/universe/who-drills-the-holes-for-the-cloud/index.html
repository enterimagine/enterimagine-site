Who Drills the Holes for the Cloud?

AI's appetite for data centers has collided with a shortage of skilled hands and the first robots on the job are doing the least glamorous work there is.

30 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

RoboticsAI & DataInnovation

A floor full of tiny holes

Picture a hall the size of several football fields, its concrete slab freshly cured and completely bare. Before a single server arrives, someone has to drill into that floor thousands of times. The holes anchor the stops that keep server cabinets in place and the legs that hold up overhead power trays, cooling pipes and cable runs [1]. Every one must land in the right spot, at the right depth, exactly where the building plans say.

For decades that job meant crews on their knees with chalk lines, tape measures and hammer drills, working across the slab one mark at a time. It is loud, dusty, repetitive work. It also sits at the very start of the schedule: until the floor is drilled, the electricians and pipefitters cannot move in.

On some of the largest data-center sites today, a low wheeled robot rolls across the slab instead. It reads the hole positions from the digital drawings and drills them by itself [2][3].

Here is the surprise. The first robots helping to build the machinery of artificial intelligence are not humanoids assembling supercomputers. They are drills and cable-pluggers, doing the jobs nobody puts on a recruiting poster.

Too many halls, too few hands

The AI boom is, physically, a construction boom. By late 2025 more than 400 data centers were in development in the United States and contractors taking them on faced backlogs of close to a year, according to reporting summarized by the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation [4]. The same summary put the construction industry's worker shortage at roughly 439,000 people, mostly in skilled trades such as electricians and pipe layers [4].

Electricians are the tightest pinch. Electrical work makes up 45% to 70% of the cost of building a data center, according to the electricians' union IBEW as cited by Fortune [5]. A single large site can need up to 1,500 workers at the peak of construction [5]. Meanwhile nearly 30% of union electricians are between 50 and 70 years old and about 20,000 retire every year [5]. The contractors' group Associated Builders and Contractors estimates that US construction needs about 349,000 net new workers in 2026 alone [5].

Training is ramping up. Applications for commercial electrical apprenticeships climbed from about 70,000 in 2022 to 120,000 in 2024 [5]. But an apprentice takes years to become a seasoned electrician and the data centers are wanted now.

So the industry is asking a blunt question. Which parts of the job could a machine take over, so that scarce skilled people spend their hours on work only they can do?

From exhibition halls to server halls

The robots arriving on data-center sites today are narrow specialists. Each does one job, in one phase of a building's life. Here is what has been shown publicly so far.

  • A drill that reads the drawings. In January 2026 the tool maker DEWALT unveiled an autonomous floor-drilling robot built with August Robotics, a mobile-robot company [1]. The robot drills downward into concrete for rack stops and for the supports of overhead mechanical, electrical and plumbing systems. It takes hole positions from the building's computer drawings and finds its own way across the floor [3]. In July the company opened commercial orders for the machine, now called DALE [2].
  • A pilot with big claims. The numbers so far come from the companies themselves. DEWALT reports that a year-long pilot with an unnamed large tech company cut 190 weeks from project timelines across 26 construction phases, at 99.97% accuracy over more than 230,000 holes [2]. In an interview with The Robot Report, the team described four robots shrinking floor preparation from eight or nine weeks to about seven to nine days and cost per hole falling from more than $60 to about $20 [3]. None of this has been independently verified.
  • An unlikely ancestor. The platform did not start in construction. August Robotics launched its first robot, Lionel, in 2019 to mark out booth layouts on exhibition floors and it has since marked more than 300 million square feet [3]. A robot that learned to find exact spots on trade-show carpet turned out to be well suited to finding exact spots on concrete.
  • Robots in the live aisles. Inside finished halls, Meta has been testing robots for routine technician chores, according to reporting by WIRED summarized by ForkLog [6]. Two-armed robots from Watney Robotics have handled network cables at its Altoona, Iowa campus since mid-2025. A Kinova robotic arm has been tried for power-cycling servers. Wheeled ABB machines with a lift and a six-axis arm have reseated hardware at the Prometheus campus in New Albany, Ohio [6]. Meta declined to comment on specific trials [6].
  • Bigger bets, still unconfirmed. SoftBank is reportedly setting up a business called Roze AI to make US data-center construction more efficient, partly with autonomous robots, according to the Financial Times and The Wall Street Journal [8]. SoftBank had not confirmed the plans in that coverage [8].

Put together, the pattern is clear. Robots are entering a data center's life at its two most repetitive moments: when the empty slab is prepared and when the finished aisles need constant small fixes.

Slower than a person and still worth it?

The honest picture inside the halls is less dramatic than the headlines. Meta's test robots work under human supervision and are slower than the technicians they assist, according to the WIRED reporting [6]. They sit idle while they recharge. Their cameras see in grayscale, which makes red and green status lights hard to tell apart. They struggle with tight turns and with cables lying across the floor and they need people to move them between buildings [6]. Staff still open cage doors and check camera feeds before a task counts as done [7].

There are hard limits too. Meta concluded that current systems are not suited to laying the very large number of cables its newest Nvidia-based supercomputers need [6]. A widely shared claim that robots could take over 80% of some technicians' work came from a single worker, describing what might happen if one cable robot works as hoped [6][7]. It is a guess, not a measured result.

So why bother? Because speed per task may be the wrong yardstick. A robot that drills through the night, swaps its battery and starts again changes the schedule even if each hole takes longer. In the drilling pilot, the team said the system freed about 21,000 labor hours in six months for skilled workers and apprentices to spend elsewhere [3]. That is company-reported, but it points at the real prize: moving people from the most repetitive steps to the ones that need judgment.

The workforce question stays open. Employees have voiced worries about job cuts, while a Meta spokesperson told reporters the company needs "more workers, not fewer" [6]. In 2026 Meta also launched free training in electrical, mechanical and plumbing trades, with guaranteed jobs for graduates in several states [6]. Nobody knows yet how that balance will settle.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities grounded in today's trends. They are not announced plans by any company named in this article.

The night-shift slab. A small fleet of drilling robots works a new hall overnight. When the crews arrive in the morning, the floor is drilled, vacuumed and logged and the first task of the day is a short walk-through of the robots' quality report rather than a week of layout.

The floor that remembers. Every hole a robot drills is recorded with its exact position and depth. That log becomes part of the building's digital twin, so years later a technician planning a new cooling loop can see precisely what lies under each tile before cutting anything.

The apprentice as fleet supervisor. Trade schools add a module on running construction robots: setting up a fleet, checking its work against the drawings and stepping in when it gets stuck. Young electricians learn to direct machines on the dull steps while they master the skilled ones.

The aisle medic. In a live hall, a cable robot handles routine overnight reseats and swaps, flagging anything unusual. Human technicians start the day with a list of the tricky cases the robot set aside, instead of a queue of identical chores.

Beyond the server hall. The same drilling robots move on to other buildings packed with overhead services: hospitals, chip plants, battery factories. Wherever thousands of anchors must land in exactly the right places, a drill that reads the drawings becomes a normal tool on site.

Back on the slab

Return to that bare concrete hall. The machine crossing it is not glamorous. It drills a hole, checks it, rolls a few feet and drills again. Yet that plain loop says something important about how automation really arrives: not as a humanoid doing everything, but as a specialist taking the most repetitive step off a crowded schedule.

The takeaway is a balance, not a replacement. The drilling numbers are promising but company-reported, the aisle robots are still slow and supervised and the people who wire these buildings remain the scarcest ingredient of all.
