When the Robot Picks Up the Surgeon's Tools

A general-purpose humanoid robot has removed gallbladders from living pigs using the same handheld instruments a human surgeon would. Here is what that proves, what it does not yet prove and where borrowed hands might one day reach.

2 November 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

RoboticsBiotechSpace

A stranger at the bedside

The setting was familiar to any surgical team. An anesthetized pig lay on the table. A laparoscopic camera glowed on the monitor. Long, slender instruments slipped through small ports in the abdomen, the standard toolkit of keyhole surgery.

The surgeon was familiar too: seated at a control console, hands resting in the master controllers.

The thing holding the instruments was not. At the bedside stood a two-legged humanoid robot, gripping ordinary laparoscopic tools in its hands. It pulled tissue aside. It dissected. It placed clips. Then it lifted the gallbladder free of the liver.

In July 2026, engineers and surgeons at UC San Diego reported in Nature that humanoid robots had completed two gallbladder removals in live pigs this way [1][2]. In one procedure a single robot led while a surgeon assisted at the table. In the other, two robots shared the work [3]. One case had minor bleeding, which was quickly stopped [2].

Robots have helped surgeons for years. What is new here is the body. Today's surgical robots are purpose-built machines with their own arms and their own instruments. This one was a general-purpose humanoid, a commercially sold robot meant for many jobs, that was simply handed the surgeon's tools.

That raises a question worth taking seriously. If a robot can hold what a person holds, does the operating room still need to be built around the robot?

Too few hands, too few rooms

The pressure behind this experiment is not a shortage of clever machines. It is a shortage of surgery.

A landmark Lancet commission on global surgery estimated that about 5 billion people cannot get safe, affordable surgery and anesthesia when they need it [7]. Of the roughly 313 million operations performed worldwide each year, only 6% take place in the poorest countries, which the commission calculated would need 144 million more procedures a year to close the gap [7].

Wealthy countries feel a version of the same squeeze. The Association of American Medical Colleges projects that the United States could be short 10,100 to 19,900 surgical specialists by 2036 [8]. One maker of miniature surgical robots says about a third of US counties have no local surgeon [9]; that is a company figure, but it matches the direction of the national projections.

Robotic surgery has not solved this, because most of today's systems are large, specialized installations. One developer of a miniature system says its two-pound device is about 1,000 times lighter than existing technologies [9], a company comparison that shows how heavy the incumbents are. A dedicated surgical robot is designed around its own arms, its own instruments and a room prepared to hold it. That makes sense in a large hospital. It makes much less sense on a ship, in a rural clinic or anywhere the room has to serve many purposes.

The UC San Diego team's bet runs the other way. Hospitals are already full of tools shaped for human hands. A robot shaped like a human could, in principle, pick those tools up. The team's surgeons note the humanoids are smaller than many hospital surgical robots [3] and easier to move around [2].

If that idea holds, the expensive part of robotic surgery could shift from custom hardware toward software, training and the network that links a surgeon to a distant pair of mechanical hands.

What the pigs proved

Strip away the headlines and the verified record is short but solid. Here is what the researchers have shown so far.

  • A peer-reviewed first. The Nature paper, published online on 8 July 2026, describes a teleoperation framework in which a surgeon at a console drives a humanoid that holds general-purpose laparoscopic instruments [1]. The team tested it in three stages: benchtop measurements of reach and tracking, dry-lab studies with surgeons of varied experience, then gallbladder removal in live pigs [1].
  • Two complete operations. Both procedures in pigs were finished successfully, covering port setup, tissue retraction, dissection, clipping and removal of the organ [1][2]. A second robot joined briefly in one case [2]. The project went from idea to first surgery in nine months, according to one of the surgeons [2].
  • A familiar feel. One surgeon said operating through the humanoid felt much like using commercial surgical robots [2]. That matters, because surgeons would not need to learn an alien interface.
  • Accuracy close to a research platform, but slower. In an earlier preprint, the same group had a humanoid hold off-the-shelf wristed instruments while users drove it through the master controllers of the da Vinci Research Kit, an open research version of a commercial surgical system [4]. On a standard peg-transfer exercise with 14 participants, the humanoid's error scores were close to the research robot's. Novices, however, needed about 89 seconds on the humanoid versus about 45 on the dedicated system [4].
  • An off-the-shelf body. The preprint used a Unitree G1 [4]. Press coverage reports the same model in the pig study [6]. The manufacturer lists the G1 at about 1.3 meters tall and 35 kilograms, folding down to 69 centimeters for transport [5].

One point is easy to miss: none of this was autonomous. Every movement came from a surgeon's hands at the console [1][2]. The robot was a new kind of body, not a new kind of surgeon.

The lag in the loop

Here is the honest caveat: the experiment shows that a humanoid can do this, not that it should yet.

The surgeons themselves listed the problems. One described instrument collisions, signal delay and a need for constant repositioning, the same growing pains early surgical robots went through [2]. Another mentioned latency and repeated recalibration during the cases [2]. The earlier user study found the humanoid roughly twice as slow as a dedicated research robot for novices [4]. The Nature abstract itself flags technical challenges that must be solved before any clinical use [1].

There is also a design argument against the whole idea. A human body is a compromise shaped by evolution for walking, carrying and grasping. Purpose-built surgical arms are designed for one job: holding an instrument perfectly still at a fixed pivot point in the body wall. Every extra joint in a humanoid is another place for small errors to creep in. Think of the difference between a camera on a tripod and a camera held by someone standing on one leg. Both can take the picture, but one has far less to correct.

Then there is the staffing paradox. These robots did not replace the surgeon; a surgeon drove every motion, with another doctor at the bedside [2]. A humanoid could let a scarce surgeon reach farther, or let one surgeon work with fewer assistants. It does not, by itself, create more surgeons. And reaching farther means operating over long-distance networks, where delay is harder to control than in a single room.

Finally, the animals were pigs, chosen because their anatomy is a reasonable stand-in, not a perfect one. Human trials would require new rounds of safety testing and regulatory review. The team calls this a proof of concept [3]. That is exactly what it is.

None of this makes the result small. It makes it early.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation zone. The scenarios below are imagined by IMAGINE. They are not announced plans of the UC San Diego team, any robot maker or any hospital. They carry no forecasts or invented figures.

The surgeon in the cargo hold. A research vessel spends months far from port. Folded in a padded locker below deck is a humanoid robot, charged and calibrated. When a crew member needs an urgent abdominal procedure, the ship's medic preps the patient while a surgeon ashore takes the controls over a satellite link. The researchers have already named ships as a possible use [3]. The hard part would be the link itself: delay and dropouts would decide which procedures are safe to attempt.

The extra pair of hands on the night shift. In a busy hospital, the humanoid never touches the scalpel. Instead it holds the camera steady, keeps a retractor in place or swaps instruments on request, the tasks that usually tie up a second trained person. The lead surgeon stays fully in charge, while the robot frees a colleague to help elsewhere.

One surgeon, two bodies. The second pig operation used two robots [3]. Picture a teaching hospital where a senior surgeon moves between two humanoid stations, guiding a trainee through the routine steps at one table while handling the delicate moment at the other. Every motion is logged, giving trainees a frame-by-frame record of how an expert worked.

The operating corner in orbit. Space stations have little room for dedicated medical machines, which is why a miniature surgical robot has already been flown to the International Space Station for remote-control tests [9]. A general-purpose humanoid already on board for maintenance could, in principle, pick up a surgical kit when needed. Weightlessness would turn every loose instrument into a floating hazard, so the robot's first job might simply be holding things down.

Learning one stitch at a time. Years of teleoperated cases could become a training library. Small, well-defined subtasks, such as holding tension on tissue while the surgeon cuts, might gradually become assisted motions that the robot suggests and the surgeon approves. Autonomy would creep in at the edges, one supervised task at a time, never as a sudden leap.

Back to the bedside

Return to that operating table in San Diego. The pig, the ports, the glowing monitor: all of it was ordinary. The only unusual thing in the room was a robot built for no job in particular, standing where an assistant would stand and holding what an assistant would hold.

That is the real lesson. For decades, robotic surgery meant building a special machine and then building the room around it. This experiment hints at the reverse: keep the room, keep the tools and change only the hands. It is slow, it lags and it is a long way from a human patient. But it reframes the question from "Can we afford a surgical robot here?" to "Can we reach this patient with the robot we already have?"
