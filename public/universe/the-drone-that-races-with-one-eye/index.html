The Drone That Races With One Eye

A racing drone from Delft beat human world champions with nothing but one camera, a motion sensor and a very small brain. What it gave up may matter more than how fast it flew.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

RoboticsAI & DataSmart Mobility

Eleven gates, no pilot

The track in Abu Dhabi is a slalom of square gates hung in the air. A drone about the size of a dinner plate screams through them at roughly 100 kilometers per hour [2][3]. It banks hard, threads a gate, drops, threads another.

Nobody is holding a controller. There is no GPS signal guiding it and no ring of cameras on the walls tracking its every move. The drone sees the course through a single forward-facing camera [2][3].

In 2025, this machine, called MonoRace, won the autonomous drone racing contest of the Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing League. It beat every other AI team. Then, in a knockout tournament, it beat three former world champion human pilots [1][2].

Its best run took two laps through eleven gates in 16.56 seconds [2][3]. The research behind it, from the Micro Air Vehicle Lab at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands, was published in the journal Science Robotics on October 14, 2026 [1].

Here is the surprise. The winning drone was not built to carry the most sensors. It was built to need as few as possible. Its creators set out to see how much intelligence they could squeeze from very limited resources [4]. The answer turned out to be: enough to outfly former world champions.

Why seeing less is harder

Flying fast is not the hard part for a drone. Knowing where you are while flying fast is.

Outdoors, many drones lean on GPS. Indoors, in tunnels or between tall buildings, that signal fades or vanishes. In research labs, fast drones often get help from a room full of motion-capture cameras that track them from the outside, like a stage lit by spotlights. Earlier learned drone controllers relied on that kind of outside tracking while they flew [2].

The best-known previous milestone came from the University of Zurich, working with Intel. Its drone, Swift, beat three champion human pilots in races held in June 2022 in a hangar near Zurich. Its fastest lap was half a second quicker than the best human lap [5].

Swift was a landmark. It also carried more sensing than MonoRace, using a stereo camera system to help track its own motion [2]. Its team noted that it struggled when conditions drifted from what it had trained on, such as too much light in the room [5].

That weakness sits at the heart of a much bigger debate. Robots that sense, decide and move in the real world, sometimes called physical AI, keep impressing in demos. They are far less impressive at doing the same thing reliably, every time. A recent survey of the field quotes a veteran roboticist who warns that raising a success rate from 50 to 70 percent is still not good enough for practical use [6].

For small flying robots, every extra sensor also costs weight, power and money. A drone that can race on one camera hints at a cheaper, lighter route to autonomy. The question is whether that route holds up outside a carefully built race.

Inside the one-eyed racer

The Delft team has described MonoRace in a peer-reviewed paper plus a detailed preprint. Here is what the drone actually does.

  • It wins on a track it did not build. The race organizers designed the course and ran the drone hardware, not the Delft team [1]. MonoRace set the fastest lap of the individual time trial, topped out at about 28 meters per second (roughly 100 kilometers per hour) and won the AI-versus-human knockout [2].
  • It sees with one camera. A single wide-angle camera captures 90 frames per second. An inertial measurement unit, a chip that senses acceleration and rotation much like the balance organ in your inner ear, fills in between frames. There is no GPS, no stereo camera and no preloaded map of the course [2][3].
  • It finds the gates with a neural network. One network picks out the gates in each camera image. The drone combines those sightings with a physics model of how it moves to estimate where it is and how fast it is going [1][2].
  • A tiny network flies it. A second, very small network takes that estimate and sends commands straight to the four motors, 500 times every second [1]. It has three layers of 64 artificial neurons and runs on the drone's flight controller chip [2]. Normally, a stack of separate control programs sits between "where am I" and "spin this motor faster." Here one learned function replaces the whole stack [1].
  • It learned to fly from scratch. The control network was trained by reinforcement learning, a method of trial and error with rewards, inside a physics simulator [1][3]. During training, the team varied the drone's weight, battery voltage, drag, sensor noise and propeller condition. That way, the network could not rely on any single idealized drone [3].

Think of it as a skilled driver who has never seen a map. All they have is a windshield, a sense of their own body and endless practice in a driving simulator built to be slightly wrong in many different ways.

Fast is not the same as safe

The Delft paper is unusually candid about what MonoRace cannot do. That honesty is the most useful part of the story.

Start with the race it did not win. In the multi-drone event, where several machines fly the course at once, MonoRace finished third. It had no way to detect or dodge other drones, so it crashed into a rival [2].

Then there are the gates. The vision system was trained to spot rectangular gates. Give it a differently shaped target and it would need retraining [2].

Reliability is good but not perfect. One of the control networks the team used in competition completed 43 flights with an 88.4 percent success rate [2]. That is excellent for a race. For a drone delivering medicine over a city, roughly one failure in nine flights would be unacceptable.

The simulator also has blind spots. It does not model mistakes in the vision system, so crash rates in simulation were a poor guide to crash rates in real flight [2]. Before the team fixed them, a saturated motion sensor and electrical interference reaching the camera both caused crashes [2].

Finally, a note on bragging rights. The team says MonoRace is the first AI-piloted drone to beat human pilots in an international racing competition [4]. Swift had already beaten champions in 2022, but on its own team's purpose-built track [5]. Both claims can be true. The difference is the venue: MonoRace won on a course and hardware it did not control [1].

None of this diminishes the result. It frames it. Racing rewards one narrow skill, speed through known gates. The real world asks for many skills at once, often with no gates at all.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built on the techniques described above. None is a product or a project anyone has announced.

The tunnel inspector. Inside a long rail or road tunnel, GPS is useless and the walls all look alike. A small drone that navigates by one camera and its own sense of motion could fly the length of the tunnel at night, checking lights and linings. Instead of square gates, it would learn to recognize the tunnel's own landmarks.

Racing for battery life, not lap time. The Delft researchers point out that the same approach could chase efficiency or safety instead of raw speed [1]. Picture a delivery drone whose tiny controller has been trained to save every possible watt. It would fly a smooth, unhurried path across a city, stretching each charge a little further.

A sparring partner for human pilots. The team suggests MonoRace could help train pilots, much as strong chess programs now coach human players [3]. A trainee could race against an AI ghost that flies a perfect line, then study exactly where they lost time.

The same small brain, on the ground. The researchers mention vacuum cleaning robots and ship-to-shore cranes as places where this style of control could apply [1]. Imagine a port crane that moves containers with the same smooth, learned precision, trained in simulation on every kind of gust and load.

A search team that shares the sky. MonoRace's crash in the multi-drone race shows what is missing [2]. A future swarm, each drone carrying one camera, would need to see its neighbors as well as its goal. Searching a collapsed building together, such drones could cover far more ground than one acting alone.

Back on the track

Picture the Abu Dhabi course again, eleven gates hanging in the air. The drone that won was the one carrying the least. One eye, one inner ear, one very small brain that talks directly to its motors.

That is the real lesson of MonoRace. Robots do not always get smarter by piling on sensors and processors. Sometimes they get smarter by learning to make more of very little. A lighter machine is cheaper, flies longer and fits into places a heavier one cannot.

The honest caveat stays in view, though. A racetrack is a known world with fixed gates and a single job. The open world has gusts, crowds and other drones. Speed was the easy part to prove.
