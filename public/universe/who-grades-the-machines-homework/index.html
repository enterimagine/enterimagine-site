Who Grades the Machine's Homework?

Two AI labs say their models knocked down a 27-year-old pillar of computer science on the same day. The bigger story is who gets to check the work.

31 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

AI & DataInnovation

Two envelopes on one Monday

Picture a computer scientist opening her laptop on Monday, October 26, 2026. In her feed sits a claim that would once have taken a career to make: a famous assumption in her field is false. A few hours later a second claim arrives from a different lab, reaching the same conclusion by its own route [1].

Neither result came from a human team grinding through a proof for years. Both came from AI models. OpenAI said an internal model found its argument on October 16 [2]. Anthropic said its Claude model found a similar result that same day, after producing about 14 million output tokens [4]. Each lab posted its write-up along with a machine-checkable version of the proof [2][4].

The assumption in question is called the Exponential Time Hypothesis. Hundreds of published results lean on it the way a house leans on a load-bearing wall [2].

So here is the surprise. The hardest part of this story is no longer finding the proof. It is deciding whether anyone should believe it. Who has the time and skill to say so?

The wall under 800 papers

Start with a puzzle called 3-SAT. You get a long list of rules, each mentioning up to three yes-or-no switches. Your job is to find a setting of all the switches that satisfies every rule at once [6]. With a handful of switches you can try every combination. With a few hundred, the number of combinations outgrows anything a computer could ever test.

In 1999 Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi proposed that this blowup is unavoidable [6]. Their Exponential Time Hypothesis says no algorithm can solve 3-SAT in what theorists call subexponential time. Put simply, the work must keep doubling at a steady clip as switches are added, with no clever shortcut that tames the growth [6]. A formal version followed in 2001, worked out with Francis Zane [1].

Nobody ever proved it. Researchers simply found it useful. If you assume it, you can show that many other problems are hard too: coloring maps, finding tight-knit groups in networks, planning database queries [6][2]. Anthropic's write-up says its model's literature search turned up more than 800 papers whose conclusions rest on this assumption or its stronger cousin [4]. That count is the company's own and has not been independently checked.

That is why a disproof matters. It does not crash anyone's software tomorrow. It quietly removes the foundation from a large body of theory.

It also lands on a profession that runs on volunteer referees. A typical proof gets read by a few experts over months. Now machines can produce long, dense arguments in days, faster than any human community can read them. The pressure point in 2026 is not discovery. It is verification.

What the labs actually posted

Everything below is what the companies report. As of this writing, no independent group has confirmed the central claim.

  • OpenAI's algorithm. The company says its model found a deterministic method that counts the solutions of these rule-lists faster than the hypothesis allows, while using only modest memory [5]. Its public notes state the running time precisely, with an exponent that grows a little slower than the number of switches [5]. OpenAI says it checked the argument in Lean, a programming language that verifies proofs line by line, one day after the discovery [2].
  • Anthropic's parallel paper. Titled "A 2^o(n) Time Algorithm for 3-SAT", it lists its author as Claude [4]. The company reports a Lean development of 1,050 modules, checked both by Lean's own core and by nanoda, a separate checker written by other developers [4]. Its paper says the result also topples the stronger version of the hypothesis [4]. A footnote acknowledges OpenAI's concurrent claim [4].
  • A review team that refuses to rubber-stamp. Impagliazzo and Paturi of UC San Diego, joined by Ryan Williams of MIT, agreed to examine OpenAI's proof without pay or nondisclosure agreements [2]. Their open letter stresses that taking part "should not be taken as certification" [2]. They asked for all raw data plus funding for a community conference. Both sides say OpenAI agreed. The company also gave $5 million to the Simons Foundation for workshops on AI-produced results [2].
  • A bigger batch of claims. The same day OpenAI also said its models proved the local smoothing conjecture, a 1991 question about how waves spread out, in every dimension [3]. If correct, that would settle two other long-standing problems as well [2]. A physics group led by Nobel laureate David Gross received separate AI-produced material on quantum field theory and chose to write up its own analysis before releasing anything [2].

The reaction from experts has been excited but careful. Harmonic analyst Alex Iosevich called the smoothing result a historic development, while saying the models only appeared to have cracked it [8]. Williams said his team was still verifying the complexity proof and did not expect near-term practical uses [8].

That restraint is well founded. By Anthropic's own estimate, its algorithm only beats brute force on typical puzzles once they have roughly 10^10,000 switches [4]. Brute force already becomes hopeless at around 100 [4]. Neither lab claims to have settled the even bigger question of P versus NP [3][4].

A perfect checker for the wrong question

Here is the part that sounds reassuring. Both proofs were checked by Lean. Lean does not get tired, flattered or rushed. If a step does not follow, the code will not compile.

The catch is subtle. Lean confirms that the formal statement in the code follows from its axioms. It cannot tell you whether that formal statement is the same thing the paper claims in English. Think of a spell-checker that guarantees every word in your essay is spelled correctly. It says nothing about whether you answered the exam question.

Lean can certify step 2 on its own. Step 3 still needs a person who understands the field.

This is not a hypothetical worry. On October 6, mathematicians Alexander Bastounis, Fabian Circelli and Anders Hansen posted an analysis of OpenAI's earlier claimed result on the Navier-Stokes equations of fluid flow [7]. They argue that the Lean proof released with it does not match the argument in the accompanying paper [7]. More broadly, they make the case that translating everyday mathematical language into airtight code involves resolving ambiguities no algorithm can be guaranteed to settle [7].

Anthropic's own paper is candid on the same point. Its appendix says the Lean code was written by AI models and reviewed by other instances of those models [4]. At the time of writing, it adds, no person was known to have checked it [4].

So the bottleneck has moved. The scarce resource is now a qualified human who can read a formal statement and say: yes, this is the claim we care about. Notice who that person is. It is exactly the kind of researcher whose careful, unglamorous checking earns the least credit. The ETH review team made this point themselves, warning that credit may flow to whoever publishes first or owns the most powerful tools [2].

There is a fair counter-argument. Machine checking still shrinks the problem enormously. Instead of auditing hundreds of pages of reasoning, a reviewer only needs to audit the definitions and the final statement. That is a far smaller target. It is also one reason both labs published their code openly [2][4].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments. They are not announced plans by any lab, journal or university.

The referee's desk becomes a control room. A journal receives a machine-written proof along with its formal code. Instead of reading hundreds of pages, two human referees spend a week on the dozen lines that define the problem and state the result. Software highlights every definition that differs from the field's standard library, so the humans know exactly where to look. Reviewing becomes less like proofreading a novel and more like inspecting the foundations of a building.

Credit for the checkers. Universities start counting verification as real research output. A young mathematician builds a career not by proving new theorems but by certifying that machine-made ones say what they claim. Her name sits on a public registry beside each result she has vetted, the way an engineer's stamp sits on a bridge drawing.

Two machines, one argument. Because two labs reached a similar result independently, reviewers in this future routinely ask a second AI system to attack a claimed proof before any human looks. Disagreement between machines becomes a signal: when two models built by different teams cannot agree on a formal statement, a person steps in. Agreement does not count as proof, but it decides where scarce human attention goes first.

Rebuilding the wall. If the disproof holds, hundreds of papers need new foundations. Picture a public map of theoretical computer science in which every result is colored by what it depends on. Results that leaned on the old hypothesis glow amber. Graduate students pick them off one by one, finding new justifications or flagging the ones that no longer stand.

Puzzles for the real world. The new algorithm only wins on astronomically large inputs today. Still, the ideas inside a proof often outlive its headline. Engineers who schedule factory shifts or route delivery fleets solve close relatives of 3-SAT every day. Some trick buried in the machine's argument could, years from now, shave time off those everyday solvers, even if the asymptotic result itself never does.

Back to the inbox

Return to that computer scientist on her Monday morning. A year ago, a message claiming to overturn a pillar of her field would have come from a colleague she could phone. This time it came from two machines, with machine-checkable code attached and an invitation to check.

Maybe the proofs will survive. The reviewers say they are still working. They have been careful not to promise an answer [2][8]. Either way, something has already changed. Producing a bold mathematical claim has become cheap. Earning trust for it has not.

That is the real lesson of the week. The future of AI in science may depend less on how clever the models become and more on whether we build the human systems that can keep up with them: referees, registries, open code and the patience to read it.

We have seen this pattern before in the IMAGINE universe. In The Lab That Never Sleeps, we followed machines that run experiments around the clock. Once discovery speeds up, trust becomes the slow step. Mathematics has now reached that same crossroads.

Next time, we leave the blackboard for the hospital ward, where a sliver of diamond is learning to hear the heart without touching the skin.
