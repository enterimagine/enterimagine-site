The Voice on the Radio Isn't in the Plane

Pilotless planes can already taxi, take off and land on their own. The harder test is the conversation with air traffic control. Two companies are betting on very different answers.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart MobilityAI & DataRoboticsSpace

A voice from 700 miles away

Albuquerque's airport is a busy place to be a beginner. Its airspace is shared by airliners, private planes and military jets. It averages 427 aircraft operations a day [1].

In October a single-engine Cessna Caravan joined that traffic. It taxied, took off and landed by itself [1]. When the tower called it, the answer did not come from the cockpit. It came from a remote pilot sitting at the company's headquarters in Mountain View, California, more than 700 miles away [1][2].

A safety pilot sat aboard [2][3]. Still, the radio exchange was the strange part. The controller was speaking to an airplane while the person answering sat in an office in another state.

Two months earlier, on the other side of the Pacific, a regulator had signed off on a different idea. In New Zealand the aviation authority accepted the test evidence for software designed to listen to controllers and answer them itself [4][5].

Same aircraft type. Same airspace rules. Two opposite answers to one question that sounds almost trivial: when there is no pilot on board, who talks to the tower?

It turns out that this, more than the flying, may decide how quickly pilotless aircraft arrive.

The party line in the sky

Air traffic control still runs largely on voice. A controller speaks on a shared radio frequency, every aircraft on it hears the call and the pilot it was meant for reads the instruction back. Heading, altitude, speed, "cleared to land." It is an old, human system that works because both ends are people who understand context, accents and the occasional garbled word.

An autopilot can hold a heading perfectly. What it cannot do on its own is hear "turn left heading two-seven-zero, descend and maintain four thousand" through static, work out that the call was meant for it and say so in a form the controller trusts.

That gap matters because controllers will not rebuild their job around robots. Any pilotless plane that wants to fly where airliners fly has to fit the existing conversation.

Regulators are now trying to collect the evidence they need to write rules for this. In March 2026 the FAA picked eight projects for its electric and advanced air mobility pilot program out of more than 30 proposals [6]. The program's stated purpose is to gather real-world operating data that will shape future regulations [6]. Albuquerque was the only New Mexico project and the only one built around early autonomous operations [6].

The FAA has also asked industry for research on fitting uncrewed aircraft into controlled airspace. Its call lists links to and from air traffic controllers among ten focus areas, with roughly $6 million per project over 2025 to 2028 [7].

Why now? Because between August and October 2026 both rival approaches reached public milestones within weeks of each other.

Two answers, both flying

Here is what has actually happened so far.

  • The AI that reads back. On August 6, 2026, Merlin reached what regulators call Stage of Involvement 3 with New Zealand's Civil Aviation Authority for two parts of its system: the flight control computer and an Automated Communication System [4][5]. At this stage the regulator has reviewed and accepted the evidence that the software was tested against its requirements. It is one step short of final compliance [4][8]. The FAA follows the work through a bilateral safety agreement with New Zealand, which could later support approval in the United States [4][8].
  • What the software is meant to do. According to trade reporting, the communication system interprets spoken ATC instructions, composes its own replies and converts headings, altitudes and speeds into commands for the flight computer [4]. The company reports it has also closed an "issue paper" on its machine-learning speech processing, an agreement with the regulator on what evidence will show the AI is safe [5]. Its test aircraft is a Cessna 208B Grand Caravan that has flown in New Zealand since 2024 [8].
  • A public demonstration. On September 10, 2026, at Alliance Airport in Texas, the company reports it showed autonomous takeoff and landing, in-flight replanning and AI-powered communication with controllers at the launch of the state's project in the federal program [9].
  • The human on the ground. The rival approach, from Reliable Robotics, keeps a human pilot in the loop but moves that person to a control station. In Albuquerque that remote pilot supervised the aircraft and handled the radio [1][2]. The company's data link, described in April, combines Viasat's geostationary satellites, Iridium's low-orbit network and ground radios for low-delay voice near airports. The company reports it is built to the industry standard for uncrewed command links [10]. In that design the remote pilot, not the software, is responsible for acting on clearances [10].
  • Machines that only listen. A third line of work leaves pilots alone and watches the radio instead. An August preprint describes a monitor that checks controller and pilot exchanges against international procedures. It scored 0.85 on a standard accuracy measure on real traffic and flagged the same deviations investigators had found in two reconstructed accidents [11]. In May, at a trade show in Lisbon, Rohde & Schwarz and the German Aerospace Center demonstrated a system that turns radio speech into data on controllers' screens. The company gave no accuracy figures [12].

Reliable's next announced step is daily cargo flights from Albuquerque to Santa Fe and Durango, with no start date yet [1][2].

In one design a remote pilot speaks for the plane over satellite and ground links. In the other, software on board hears the controller and replies itself.

The radio has no password

It is tempting to read these milestones as a race the AI is winning. The evidence is thinner than that.

Stage of Involvement 3 is not certification. Trade reporting says so plainly [4]. The evidence package behind Merlin's speech system has not been published [4][5]. The Texas demonstration came with a company release, not a technical report [9]. Albuquerque's flights also came without published dates, results or radio transcripts. Every flight carried a safety pilot [1][2][3]. Leeham News reports that Reliable is aiming for a certificate in 2028, a target from the trade press rather than a company document [8].

There is a deeper issue too. Aviation's voice channel has no built-in way to prove who is speaking. A George Washington University study cloned controller voices with text-to-speech software. The fakes got the words almost exactly right, with a word error rate of 3.9%, though their rhythm and pitch were off [13]. The authors point out that the radio offers no cryptographic authentication [13].

That study did not test AI pilots. But it frames the trade-off. A human remote pilot brings judgment about odd calls, at the cost of depending on a satellite link that must never drop, which is why Reliable layers two satellite networks with ground radios [10]. Software on board avoids the link problem but must decide, alone, whether a voice is real and whether an instruction makes sense.

Nobody yet knows which risk regulators will find easier to accept. Both may end up flying for different jobs.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below imagine where today's trends could lead. None describes a product, schedule or decision that any company or regulator has announced.

The bilingual tower. A regional tower keeps its voice frequency for crewed traffic while pilotless cargo planes receive the same clearances as digital messages. A speech-to-data system like the one shown in Lisbon keeps both versions in step on the controller's screen, so a human never has to type what they just said.

The night-shift remote pilot. A control room in one city looks after several small cargo planes, each on a quiet regional route. A remote pilot takes the radio for whichever aircraft is entering busy airspace, while software handles the long, uneventful cruise. Staffing works like an air traffic center in reverse.

The readback referee. Every exchange on a busy frequency is checked in real time by a monitor that flags a wrong altitude in a readback before anyone acts on it. It helps human pilots as much as machines. Accident reports start citing the alerts it gave.

Signed voices. Controllers' radios attach a short digital signature to each transmission that only equipped aircraft can verify. Human pilots hear nothing different, but onboard software can refuse instructions from a voice that cannot prove where it came from.

The tower that asks twice. An onboard AI that is unsure of an instruction does what good pilots already do: it asks the controller to say again. Its rules for when to ask become part of the certification evidence, judged as carefully as its rules for flying.

Back in Albuquerque

Picture the tower again. A controller looks at a radar screen dotted with airliners, private planes and military jets and calls a cargo Caravan. Today a person in California answers. Merlin is working toward the day when the answer comes from software in the airplane itself while regulators in two countries are weighing the evidence.

The takeaway is that pilotless flight is no longer mainly a flying problem. It is a communication problem: about trust, about readbacks and about knowing who is on the other end of a crackling radio.
