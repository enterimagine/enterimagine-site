How Cold Does a Quantum Computer Need to Be?

The quantum computers in the headlines run colder than deep space, on helium that is suddenly scarce. Others work with lasers, vacuum and much warmer hardware. Which designs lean least on the planet's rarest resources? And what do they need instead?

31 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe6 min read

Quantum ComputingEnergyInnovation

Two machines, two climates

Picture two quantum computers in the same building. The first hangs inside a tall metal cylinder, a stack of shields that steps down from room temperature to a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero. Its coldest stage runs on a mixture of helium gases, one of them so rare that most of the supply comes from a nuclear stockpile.

The second sits in a rack that looks like ordinary server hardware. Inside, a handful of charged atoms float in a vacuum, held in place by electric fields and chilled by laser light rather than by a refrigerator. The room around it is at office temperature.

In Running Out of Cold we followed the 2026 helium shock and what it means for machines like the first one. This article asks the follow-up question. If some quantum computers barely need the deep freeze, do they escape the resource problem altogether?

Here is the surprise. The answer is no, but for a reason that has little to do with physics and a lot to do with engineering choices. Even the "warm" machines often borrow some cold. Every design trades one scarce input for another.

A resource bill that depends on the hardware

Quantum computing is not one technology. Companies build qubits, the basic units of a quantum computer, from superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, particles of light and other systems. A 2026 analysis led by researchers at Oak Ridge National Laboratory lists these platforms and notes that it is not yet clear which ones will be deployed at scale [1].

That choice shapes the resource bill. Superconducting qubits need cooling close to absolute zero. The same analysis flags the supply of helium-3, the rare isotope that makes that cooling possible, as a concern. Its preliminary estimate for large, error-corrected superconducting machines is 1 to 10 watts per qubit for cooling and control. A useful machine may need a million qubits or more, which adds up to megawatts [1].

Notably, that paper gives no comparable power figures for the other platforms [1]. The question of which design is gentlest on resources is wide open. It deserves a closer look.

What the warmer machines actually need

Here is how the main alternatives stand, based on what companies and researchers have published.

  • Trapped ions: no dilution fridge, but often some cold. Trapped-ion computers hold charged atoms in electromagnetic traps inside a vacuum and cool them with lasers [2]. They avoid the dilution refrigerators that superconducting machines need [2]. Yet IonQ says its Aria and Forte systems use closed-loop cryogenics to improve the vacuum and qubit performance, while its first commercial system, Harmony, used a warm vacuum chamber [3]. In research setups, such cryocoolers chill the trap to around 4 kelvin, colder than liquid nitrogen but far warmer than a dilution fridge, using a helium compressor [4].
  • Trapped ions at room temperature. Some companies already run ion traps warm. The Austrian company AQT says its PINE system operates at room temperature in a standard 19-inch rack. eleQtron reports up to 30 room-temperature ion qubits [2]. In February 2025 IonQ said it had built a prototype vacuum package intended for room-temperature systems "without any cryogenically enhanced vacuum" [5]. That is a roadmap goal, not a shipping product.
  • Neutral atoms: lasers and glass. Neutral-atom machines trap uncharged atoms, typically rubidium or strontium, with focused laser beams in an ultra-high-vacuum cell [6]. QuEra says its systems run at room temperature. One analysis puts a typical system's draw at about 3 kilowatts [6][7]. QuEra also says its planned 2028 fault-tolerant machine should draw under 40 kilowatts, a company projection [7].
  • Photonics: cold, but not that cold. PsiQuantum builds qubits from particles of light on silicon chips, but its detectors still need cold. In 2025 Linde Engineering agreed to build one of the largest cryogenic plants ever made for a quantum computer, cooling tens of thousands of chips to 4 kelvin at PsiQuantum's site in Brisbane, Australia [8].

Put together, the picture is a ladder rather than a split between cold and warm machines. Superconducting qubits sit at the bottom near absolute zero. Photonic detectors and many ion traps sit around 4 kelvin. Neutral atoms and some ion traps work at room temperature.

Escaping helium means depending on something else

Climbing the ladder does remove the most fragile input. Cryocoolers that stop around 4 kelvin can run on a sealed charge of ordinary helium-4 that circulates in a closed loop, with no need for helium-3 [4]. Room-temperature machines need no cryogenic helium at all. In a year when a third of the world's helium supply was cut off, that matters.

But the warmer machines carry their own bill. Many trapped-ion computers use ytterbium, which one industry review calls the backbone of leading trapped-ion machines [9]. Ytterbium is a rare-earth element. The same review estimates that China holds about 90% of global rare-earth refining capacity [9]. A trap holds only dozens of atoms at a time, so the quantity is tiny, but the supply chain is narrow. Interestingly, the newest machines are shifting: Quantinuum's 98-qubit Helios uses barium ions as qubits, with ytterbium only as a helper for cooling [10]. IonQ's Tempo also uses barium [2].

Lasers are the other cost. Ion and atom machines need many precisely tuned beams. An industry review names laser addressing as a scaling constraint for trapped ions [2]. That is one reason IonQ bought Oxford Ionics in 2025, a company that controls ions with electronics on chips made in conventional semiconductor fabs instead of with many lasers [11][2]. Vacuum is the third. Ultra-high vacuum cells need careful baking and pumping. Cold surfaces help, which is exactly why many ion systems still use cryocoolers [3][6].

The honest conclusion is that no platform is resource-free. Superconducting machines depend on helium-3 and deep cold. Warmer machines depend on lasers, vacuum and specific elements, some of them refined in only a few places. Which design ends up using less energy and fewer critical materials per useful calculation has not yet been measured on any machine large enough to matter.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built from today's trends. They are not announced plans by IonQ, Quantinuum, QuEra, PsiQuantum, AQT or any other company or laboratory.

The resource label. Quantum computers ship with a resource label, like the energy label on a fridge. It lists the power drawn per useful operation, the helium-3 and helium-4 inside and the critical elements used. Buyers compare platforms on the planet's terms as well as on speed.

A data center without a cryoplant. A hospital or university installs a room-temperature quantum machine in an ordinary server room, next to its AI clusters. There is no helium contract and no special cooling building, just racks, vacuum cells and power.

Ions from a common element. Trapped-ion makers standardize on barium or other widely available elements for qubits, keeping rare earths out of the critical path. A shortage in one country no longer threatens a whole class of computers.

Cold only where it pays. Hybrid machines put each part on its own rung of the ladder. Qubits that need deep cold get it, while control electronics, light sources and memory sit in warmer stages or at room temperature, shrinking the scarcest part of the cooling bill.

The fair comparison. An independent lab runs the same problem on superconducting, ion, atom and photonic machines and publishes the full bill: energy, cooling, materials and error rates. For the first time, readers can see which design really does more with less.

Back to the two machines

Return to the building with two quantum computers. The one in the tall cylinder needs helium that the world is struggling to supply. The one in the rack needs no deep freeze, yet it may still lean on a cryocooler, a bank of lasers and a rare-earth metal refined mostly in one country.

The takeaway is that the real question is not cold versus warm. It is which scarce resources each design relies on and how well each can swap them out as it scales. The warmer platforms have a genuine head start on helium. Whether they win on energy and materials overall is a question the industry has not yet answered with data.
