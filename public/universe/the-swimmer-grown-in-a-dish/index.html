The Swimmer Grown in a Dish

Engineers are building robots whose motors are living muscle, grown from cells and steered by flashes of light. This fall, two labs showed how far the idea has come. They also showed how much it still depends on a dish of nutrients.

The fin that twitches on cue

In a petri dish at MIT, a sliver of gel about the size of a stick of gum floats in liquid. It has two fins. Along each fin runs a layer of muscle thinner than a human hair [1].

A researcher holds a light over the dish and flashes it at the left fin. The muscle twitches. The fin pulls. The little swimmer turns. Another flash, this time on the right, straightens it out. Flash by flash, it is guided around the corners of a tiny water maze [1][2].

There is no motor, no battery and no wire. The power comes from living cells, genetically engineered to contract when light hits them [1]. They were even "exercised" with flashes of light before the robot was assembled, much like an athlete training before a race [1].

In late September 2026, the team described how this paper-thin robot reached about four body lengths per minute, steered only by light [1]. Three weeks later, a group at ETH Zurich showed muscle actuators that could swim, walk, jump and grasp, designed with the help of a computer algorithm [3].

The robots are slow and fragile. But they raise a strange question: what happens when a machine's engine is alive?

Why build a motor out of meat?

Electric motors are superb at many things. They are not good at being small, soft and gentle at the same time.

Muscle is different. It turns chemical energy into motion right where the force is needed. Researchers point out that living muscle can sense its surroundings, grow stronger with exercise and even heal after damage [11][10]. Engineered tissue could also open the door to robots that break down safely when their job is done [4].

That combination suits places where hard metal machines are unwelcome. Researchers mention water quality monitoring, delicate reefs and wetlands as well as environments too fragile or unpredictable for conventional hardware [1][4].

There is also a scientific payoff. A muscle-powered swimmer is a living test bench. When a lab at Harvard built a fish-shaped robot from human heart muscle cells in 2022, the goal was partly to study how heart rhythms work and fail [7].

For years, though, these devices were craft projects. Each one was a bulky chunk of tissue that needed millions of cells [1]. They were hard to reproduce, hard to control and weak. The latest results suggest the field is starting to treat muscle as an engineered part, one that can be shaped, trained and optimized.

From one-off creatures to designed parts

Here is the recent track record, from the newest result back.

  • Thin films instead of chunks. MIT's fall 2026 swimmer uses a half-millimeter gel skeleton with grooves cut into it. Square-bottomed grooves lined up the muscle cells better than curved ones, so the cells fused into stronger fibers [1]. An earlier, softer gel shriveled under the pull of the muscle; the stiffer one held [1]. Thinner designs could also be cheaper to build [2].
  • Muscle designed by algorithm. ETH Zurich's team grew mouse muscle tissue on a soft printed scaffold, using a computer to co-optimize where the muscle goes and how the scaffold is shaped [3]. Its 1.5-centimeter actuator can jump about 5 millimeters in a single contraction, a third of its length [3]. The institution reports a range of motion up to 11 times greater than earlier systems with similar amounts of muscle [3]. Each scaffold prints in about a minute [3].
  • A speed record that trains itself. In March 2026, researchers at the National University of Singapore reported a boxfish-inspired swimmer that reached 467 millimeters per minute, which they describe as the fastest skeletal-muscle biohybrid robot reported [4]. Its muscle trained itself: two muscle rings were coupled so that each contraction stretched the other [4]. A sound-triggered system lets the robot start and stop at a clap [5].
  • Nerves in the loop. In 2025, a team reported a soft swimmer in which motor neurons growing next to the muscle made it contract four times harder [6]. A 2024 study controlled a fish-like robot wirelessly by stimulating neurons on its left or right side [9].
  • The long-distance record. Harvard's 2022 heart-cell fish swam on its own rhythm for more than 100 days, getting faster as its cells matured [7]. Over that time its muscle beat about 38 million times [8].

Each step solves a different piece of the puzzle: structure, design, strength, control and endurance. No single robot yet has them all.

A pulse of light or electricity makes the muscle contract; the scaffold turns that pull into a fin stroke. Every step only works while the cells sit in their nutrient liquid [1][3].

Alive, but on life support

Every one of these robots shares a hidden dependency: it is only alive while it sits in the right liquid.

The ETH team states it plainly. Its muscle needs a nutrient solution and cannot yet operate on its own over long periods [3]. Larger systems would need something like blood vessels to bring in oxygen and nutrients and to carry away waste [3]. Without that, thick tissue suffocates from the inside. A Japanese team that built a muscle-driven robotic hand in 2025 rolled thin strands of tissue into bundles for exactly that reason [13]. The finished hand also has to work suspended in liquid [13].

Muscle also tires. In one MIT study, performance dropped sharply after 30 minutes of rapid contractions [11]. The Japanese hand lost force after about 10 minutes of stimulation and needed an hour to recover [13].

Living parts are also not identical. Cultured tissue varies from one batch to the next, so two robots built from the same design may not behave the same way [3][10]. Biological actuators also produce small forces, which makes them hard to control [10].

Then come the questions that are not about engineering. A 2024 analysis by ethicists and roboticists called for tailored rules, risk assessments and public debate [12]. It asked what moral status a robot made of living tissue might have. It also asked how a released biohybrid device could disturb a natural food chain [12].

Even the medical promise needs patience. When the heart-cell fish was published, an outside expert said a lab-grown artificial heart could be years away [8]. These robots are research tools first.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built on the research described above. No lab or company has announced them.

The reef inspector that dissolves. A swarm of small muscle-powered swimmers drifts along a coral reef, checking water quality. When their tissue gives out, their bodies are designed to break down, leaving no plastic behind. Their makers would first need to show they cannot disturb the reef's own food web.

The drug test that swims. A pharmaceutical lab grows a small swimmer from heart-like muscle cells. Instead of watching cells beat in a flat dish, researchers watch how a candidate drug changes the way the robot swims, a vivid readout of muscle health.

The algorithm that grows its own engine. Software in the style of ETH's co-design proposes a muscle layout; a printer builds the scaffold in a minute; the lab measures the result and feeds it back. Over many rounds, the computer learns what living muscle can do, much as engineers once learned the limits of steel.

The robot with a circulatory system. A future biohybrid carries thin channels that pump nutrients through its tissue, like a simple bloodstream. It could leave the dish for hours instead of minutes, opening the way to larger, stronger devices.

The classroom kit. Students grow muscle rings that train each other, then race palm-sized swimmers across a tank by clapping. The lesson is not just robotics, but how life and machines start to blur.

The flash in the dish

Back at MIT, the swimmer finishes the maze, nudged around each corner by a hand-held light. It is slow, simple in shape and entirely dependent on its liquid [1][3].

Yet its fins are moved by cells that were grown, trained and shaped by engineers. A few years ago, such robots were one-off creatures. Now labs are designing muscle the way they design circuits, measuring it, printing it and improving it with algorithms.

The robots in this series have learned to pick up a surgeon's tools and to work in the next aisle. This one has to learn something harder: how to stay alive. Next in the series: when an algorithm starts designing living tissue, who decides what it is allowed to build?
