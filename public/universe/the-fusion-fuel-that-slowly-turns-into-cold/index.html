The Fusion Fuel That Slowly Turns Into Cold

Fusion reactors will store tritium that slowly turns into helium-3, the rare gas quantum computers need to stay cold. The catch is that fusion would rather burn it first.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

EnergyQuantum Computing

Fifty-two steel beds

In southern France, inside a concrete building at the ITER fusion project, engineers are preparing a row of 52 steel containers. Each holds a powdered metal that soaks up hydrogen the way a sponge soaks up water. Their job will be to store tritium, the rare radioactive form of hydrogen that fusion reactors burn as fuel [1].

Tritium does not sit still. With a half-life of about 12.3 years [2], a stored supply loses roughly 5.5% of itself every year [3]. Each decaying atom turns into helium-3, a light, stable form of helium.

ITER's plans say the helium-3 will be pulled out of those beds and collected for use [1].

Now travel to a quantum computing lab almost anywhere in the world. At its center hangs a gold-colored chandelier of plates and tubes: a dilution refrigerator, the machine that chills superconducting qubits to a whisker above absolute zero. Its working fluid is a mixture that depends on helium-3.

So here is the curious link. The fuel of tomorrow's power plants slowly turns into the coolant of today's quantum computers. Could fusion become a helium-3 supplier? The physics says yes, a little. The engineering says that fusion would rather not keep tritium around long enough to find out.

A gas you cannot simply dig up

Ordinary helium comes out of natural gas wells. Helium-3 barely does. The US Department of Energy says its own helium-3 comes solely from the decay of tritium held by the nuclear weapons program, separated during processing at the Savannah River Site [4]. It makes "thousands of liters" available each year [4].

That supply has been squeezed before. Demand peaked at about 70,000 liters a year in 2008 [4]. The US stockpile fell from about 235,000 liters in 2001 to about 50,000 in 2010, which led to rationing [3]. Today the DOE projects federal demand at under 6,000 liters a year, thanks to recycling and substitutes [4].

Quantum computing adds a new kind of customer. A government audit found that the DOE isotope office made 583 helium-3 shipments between fiscal years 2020 and 2025, the third most of any isotope, with quantum science among the uses [5]. One leading fridge maker reports that it has delivered more than 1,500 dilution refrigerators. It has also agreed to buy up to 10,000 liters a year of helium-3 mined on the Moon, if that supply materializes, from 2028 to 2037 [6].

Why now? On October 27, 2026, the DOE added helium to its Critical Material List, naming quantum computing among the uses that depend on it [7]. The notice treats helium in general. It does not single out helium-3 [7]. Still, it signals that the cold behind quantum machines has become a matter of national supply planning.

Meanwhile fusion is moving from physics experiments toward fuel factories. And fuel factories for fusion are, by necessity, tritium factories.

Where the tritium will live

A fusion reactor of the most common design fuses deuterium with tritium. A one-gigawatt fusion power plant would burn nearly 55 kilograms of tritium a year [8]. The world's entire civilian stock is only a few dozen kilograms at most. ITER puts it at around twenty kilos [9]. Fusion plants therefore have to make their own, by hitting lithium with the neutrons the reaction releases. Here is where that work stands:

  • ITER's tritium plant. Korea signed the last procurement agreement for ITER's storage and delivery system in March 2025. The 52 hydride beds must be ready for commissioning by 2030. The decay helium-3 will be extracted and collected [1]. At any moment, less than a gram of fuel sits inside the reactor vessel. Only about 1% of the injected fuel burns. The rest is pumped out, cleaned and recycled [9].
  • A fuel loop in Ontario. UNITY-2, a joint venture of Kyoto Fusioneering and Canadian Nuclear Laboratories at Chalk River, is built to circulate up to 30 grams of tritium in each 24-hour cycle. The company says commissioning is scheduled for late 2026 [10]. After raising about 25.7 billion yen in August 2026, it said tritium testing was set to begin within the year [11].
  • Breeding blankets in Britain. The UK Atomic Energy Authority's LIBRTI program, worth about £220 million from 2023 to 2030, is building a test bed at Culham. It aims to show that prototype lithium blankets really produce tritium [12]. In July 2026 Commonwealth Fusion Systems became its first international partner [13].
  • Canada's head start. Canada's heavy-water reactors already make tritium as a byproduct. In 2021 an Ontario Power Generation subsidiary announced plans to extract helium-3 from tritium stored at its Darlington station. It described this as the first civilian source of the isotope [14]. A 2025 review estimated that a 700-megawatt reactor of this type could recover about 100 to 700 liters of helium-3 a year [15].
  • A record year for the weapons pipeline. The US National Nuclear Security Administration completed 13 tritium extractions in nine months, beating its previous record of eight in a year [2].

The pieces of a tritium economy are taking shape. Helium-3 rides along as a side effect.

Only tritium sitting in storage turns into helium-3. The faster a plant burns and recycles its fuel, the less helium-3 it makes.

The fuel that fusion does not want to keep

Here is the catch. Every liter of helium-3 from a fusion plant comes from tritium that was not burned. To a power plant operator, that is lost fuel.

Fusion designers work hard to keep tritium inventories small. A peer-reviewed analysis of the fuel cycle found that ITER-like operation burns only about 0.36% of the tritium per pass. A self-sufficient plant needs that figure, multiplied by its fueling efficiency, to exceed roughly 2% [16]. The same study estimated that a 3-gigawatt plant might need about 11 kilograms of tritium to start up. With better burn rates and faster processing that falls below 5 kilograms [16]. Smaller stores mean less radioactive material on site and less fuel lost to decay.

Do the rough arithmetic. A plant holding 5 kilograms in storage would see about 275 grams decay each year at the 5.5% rate [3]. By our own estimate, that is on the order of 2,000 liters of helium-3 gas. It is useful, but only a fraction of a single fridge maker's planned lunar contract [6]. And the tritium that actually burns becomes ordinary helium-4, which does nothing for quantum fridges.

So fusion is unlikely to flood the market with helium-3. It could become one steady stream among several. It would join Canada's reactors, the weapons program's stockpile [4][14] and newer approaches. One company reports that it has produced 99% pure helium-3 from domestic natural helium using a cryogenic process [17]. The defense research agency DARPA has also asked for ideas on sub-kelvin coolers that need no helium-3 at all [18].

There is also a timing problem. ITER's tritium systems will not be commissioned before 2030 [1]. Commonwealth Fusion Systems targets its first power plant for the early 2030s [13]. Helium-3 builds up only slowly from stored tritium. Half of a stock's eventual output takes 12.3 years to appear [3].

The honest picture: fusion and quantum computing are linked by an isotope, but not yet by a supply chain.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities, not announced plans of any company, agency or project named in this article.

Fusion's other export. A fusion plant in the 2040s runs a small separation unit next to its tritium storage beds, much as ITER plans to. Under a long-term contract, the collected helium-3 travels a few hundred miles to a quantum data center. It goes into new refrigerators and tops up old ones.

The fuel bank dilemma. A utility must decide how much tritium to keep in reserve so it can restart its reactor after maintenance. A bigger reserve means more security and a steady trickle of helium-3, but more fuel lost to decay every year. Engineers and isotope buyers end up at the same table.

Fridges that need none of it. Helium-3-free coolers reach the temperatures qubits need. Quantum labs stop competing for the isotope. Fusion's helium-3 flows instead to neutron detectors and lung imaging. The link between the two fields fades almost as soon as it forms.

A closed loop for the coldest gas. Quantum data centers recover nearly every molecule of helium-3 when fridges are serviced or retired. Fusion plants supply only the small amount lost along the way. The isotope behaves less like a fuel and more like a lease.

One lab, two needs. A fusion research campus hosts its own quantum computing facility. The tritium plant's helium-3 never leaves the site. In return, the quantum team helps model the plasma.

Back to the beds

Picture the 52 steel beds again, waiting for their first fill. Inside, tritium will do what it has always done, quietly turning into helium-3 a few atoms at a time. Whether that gas ends up cooling a qubit depends less on physics than on choices: how much fuel plants hold, how fast fusion arrives and whether quantum machines find other ways to stay cold.

For the backstory of why quantum computers are so hungry for this isotope, read Running Out of Cold.

Next in the series: the refrigerators trying to reach millikelvin temperatures without helium-3 at all. If they succeed, the race for this rare gas could end before fusion joins it [18].
