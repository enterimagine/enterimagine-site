The Ferry That Had to Hitchhike

The world's biggest battery ship now crosses the River Plate on electricity alone, but first it had to be carried halfway around the planet.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart MobilityEnergyInnovation

Stranded on the Derwent

Through the southern winter of 2026, the largest electric ship ever built was sitting on the River Derwent in Hobart, going nowhere.

It was 130 meters of aluminum catamaran [1]. Inside its hulls sat more than 250 metric tons of batteries [1]. It had been finished, tested and named China Zorrilla [1]. Its future job was waiting on the far side of the Pacific, on the broad brown estuary between Buenos Aires and Uruguay.

There was one small problem. The ship could not get there by itself.

Its batteries hold enough energy for roughly an hour and a half of sailing [5][9]. That is plenty for its working route. It is nowhere near enough for an ocean. So the plan was to load the ferry onto another ship and carry it across. In April 2026 that carrier was itself held up in the Strait of Hormuz amid regional shipping disruption, so the ferry waited in Hobart [9].

In August, a semi-submersible heavy-lift ship finally arrived, sank its own deck beneath the ferry and lifted it clean out of the water [3][10]. Forty days later the ferry reached the River Plate [10]. On October 22 it carried its first paying passengers [1][2].

That is the central puzzle of this story. The ship that may show the world how far battery ferries can go first had to prove, very publicly, how far they cannot.

Why the short hop goes electric first

Shipping is one of the hardest parts of transport to clean up. Ships are heavy, they travel far and they often refuel in places with no spare grid capacity. For long ocean routes, the debate is mostly about new liquid fuels.

Short, busy ferry crossings are different. They behave more like a bus line than a voyage. The distance is fixed. The ship returns to the same two docks again and again. It spends time tied up while cars and people roll on and off, which is exactly when a battery can be refilled.

The Buenos Aires to Colonia del Sacramento crossing fits that pattern. It is about 55 kilometers across the Río de la Plata, the wide estuary that separates Argentina from Uruguay [2][3]. The operator, Buquebus, runs it as a regular shuttle for tourists, commuters and cars.

Uruguay's side of the story matters too. Its grid draws almost all its electricity from renewable sources, mostly hydropower and wind [8]. A ferry that charges there is close to running on rivers and breezes.

There is also money pressure in a quieter form. Lenders need evidence that an electric ship can be financed like any other vessel, not treated as a science project. In 2024 the International Finance Corporation (IFC), the private-sector arm of the World Bank Group, backed a loan for this ferry [6]. The World Bank later described the deal as the first financing for an electric ferry anywhere [7].

So the question in late 2026 is concrete. Can a full-size passenger and car ferry carry thousands of people on battery power, day after day, on a real commercial timetable?

What crossed the river on October 22

Here is what has been confirmed so far, along with who is reporting it.

  • It is in service. Buquebus held a launch ceremony in Buenos Aires on October 21, 2026, then ran the first commercial crossing the next day [1][2]. The route takes about 90 minutes [4].
  • It is big. The catamaran was built by Incat Tasmania in Hobart. It is 130 meters long and 32 meters wide [1][4]. It can carry up to 2,100 passengers along with 225 vehicles [1][2].
  • The battery is enormous. Reports put its storage above 40 megawatt-hours (MWh) [1][2]. A megawatt-hour is a thousand kilowatt-hours, the unit printed on a household power bill. The battery supplier, Corvus Energy, reports 41 MWh spread over 5,016 modules in four separate battery rooms [5]. The company also claims the system is about four times larger than any earlier battery in commercial maritime service [5].
  • It was not meant to be electric. The ship was ordered with engines burning liquefied natural gas or diesel. The design switched to fully battery-electric during construction [1][2][8]. Incat reports that the change saved enough weight to allow smaller waterjets [2].
  • It drinks from the shore. Dedicated high-power chargers sit at both terminals [1][2]. Published charging times vary: one trade outlet reports about 40 minutes per crossing [2], while an Argentine business magazine describes 45 to 80 minutes to take the battery from 20% to 80% charge [8].

Electric motors drive Wärtsilä waterjets, which pull in river water and shoot it out astern to push the ship forward at about 25 knots [3][4]. Sources disagree on how many motors there are, so treat that detail as unsettled.

The financing is equally concrete. An IFC guarantee of US$67 million supports a US$107 million loan from Banco Santander Uruguay [6]. Buquebus committed a further US$14 million to the two charging stations along with their grid connections [6]. On the Uruguayan side, the state utility UTE built new high-capacity cabling in Colonia worth about US$20 million [8].

One charge, one crossing

The battery that cannot leave home

The hitchhiking voyage is not just a funny footnote. It is the most revealing data point in the story.

Batteries are heavy for the energy they hold. Corvus reports 41 MWh [5]. The battery weighs more than 250 metric tons [1]. A tank of diesel holding the same energy would weigh only a small fraction of that. Electric motors waste far less energy than engines, which narrows the gap without closing it.

That is why the ferry's range is measured in minutes rather than days [5][9]. It works because its route is short and its docks have chargers. Take it away from those two plugs and it is stranded, as Hobart showed.

So this ship does not show that oceans are about to go electric. It shows something narrower: for crossings of an hour or two with good shore power, batteries are now big enough for very large vessels.

There are other caveats worth naming.

The climate benefit depends on where the electricity comes from. Uruguay's grid is close to fully renewable, while Argentina's has a much smaller renewable share [8]. The IFC estimates savings of about 37,500 metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent a year [6]. The World Bank cites an 84% cut in the route's transport emissions [7]. The Argentine coverage notes that no detailed method has been published for these figures [8].

Shore infrastructure is expensive. Two countries, two utilities, two ports and their regulators all had to line up [8]. Service began in October, later than first planned [10].

Finally, the ship is new. Charging reliability, battery wear and passenger demand will only be known after months of service [8]. Even the published timetable differs between sources [3][8]. Most of the performance claims come from the companies that built or supplied the ship [5][8].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are imagined by IMAGINE. They are not products, projects or announcements by Buquebus, Incat, Corvus Energy or anyone else.

The estuary as a power bank. A big ferry battery sits idle at the dock for part of every night. In a future where ports and utilities agree on rules, a fleet of such ships could soak up surplus wind power after midnight and hand some back at the evening peak, acting like a floating battery for the coastal city.

Ferries that sail on a schedule set by the weather. Charging when renewable power is cheapest could shape the timetable itself. A software planner might shift a crossing by a few minutes, or slow a ship slightly, to match the hours when the grid is greenest, much as some data centers already move flexible work.

Plug-in harbors. The expensive part of this project was not only the ship but the cables, substations and chargers at each end. A harbor that builds that once could serve tugboats, pilot boats, port cranes and electric trucks from the same connection. The ferry becomes the anchor customer that makes the plug worth building.

Retirement planning for a ship battery. Marine batteries will eventually wear out. Modules that still pass careful inspection might be rated for lighter shore duty, while any pack that fails inspection goes straight to recycling, where its metals feed new cells. A port that plans for both paths from day one avoids an expensive surprise a decade later.

Hybrids that hop further. For routes too long for batteries alone, designers could pair a smaller battery with a clean liquid fuel. The battery handles harbor maneuvering, where noise and exhaust hurt most, while the fuel carries the open-water stretch. Each new route would choose its own split.

The ride home

Picture the China Zorrilla again, this time leaving Buenos Aires on a weekday morning. No funnel plume trails behind it. The deck hums rather than throbs. Ninety minutes later it noses into Colonia, where a charger is waiting [2][4].

Then remember that same ship riding on another ship's back for 40 days [10]. Both pictures are true. Together they mark the frontier with unusual clarity: battery ships have grown to full size, but only on routes short enough to come home to a plug every crossing.

For IMAGINE, this continues the battery thread of Batteries in Waiting. It also echoes The Last Rehearsal Before the First Fare: every new kind of transport faces a nervous first day with paying passengers.

Next, we leave the water for the road. If ships can charge at every dock, could trucks and cars one day charge through the pavement while they drive?
