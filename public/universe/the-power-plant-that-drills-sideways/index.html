The Power Plant That Drills Sideways

In Utah, a geothermal plant borrowed the oil field's sideways drills to pull steady power out of hot granite. Its first block is now selling electricity, with a data center buyer waiting in line.

3 November 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

EnergyInnovationAI & Data

Heat under the sagebrush

Drive through Beaver County in southwest Utah and you see the usual high-desert picture: sagebrush, wind, a long horizon. On September 24, 2026, something unusual happened beneath that scenery. A power plant called Cape Station began feeding electricity into the grid from rock thousands of feet down [1].

Six days later its owner, Fervo Energy, declared commercial operation, one day ahead of its contract deadline [2]. The first unit, which the company calls a GeoBlock, is producing 33 megawatts of net power [2]. That is enough to matter on a regional grid. It is also running around the clock, whatever the weather.

The surprising part is how the heat gets to the surface. Cape Station does not sit on a natural hot spring or a field of steam vents. Its wells turn sideways deep underground, using a drilling style borrowed from the oil and gas industry [1]. Water travels down cold through one well, threads its way through cracked hot rock and comes back up another well hot enough to drive a turbine [1].

Even the customers tell a story. Weeks before first power, Google agreed to buy 396 megawatts from the site to supply a planned data center in Utah [3]. Oil-field tools are now helping to feed artificial intelligence.

The always-on gap

Wind and solar power come and go with the weather. Grids still need power that runs steadily day and night. Engineers call this firm power. Data centers, which train and run AI models around the clock, need exactly this kind of steady supply.

Geothermal energy is firm by nature. The heat inside the Earth does not set at dusk or drop when the wind stops. The catch has always been location. Conventional geothermal plants only work where hot rock reservoirs already exist in nature, which is why the United States has only about 3.7 gigawatts of geothermal capacity today [6].

Enhanced geothermal systems, or EGS, try to manufacture what nature left out. Engineers drill into hot rock that has heat but little natural plumbing. Then they pump fluid down at pressure to open a network of fine cracks. The result works like a radiator built inside a mountain. Water flows through the cracks, picks up heat and returns to the surface.

The US Department of Energy estimated in 2024 that next-generation geothermal could economically supply up to 90 gigawatts by 2050 [6]. That would be roughly 10% of the extra clean firm power the department expects the country to need [6]. Fervo describes Cape Station as the first enhanced geothermal development built from scratch to reach commercial operation [2]. It is the first big test of whether that promise holds up.

What the drills have proven

Here is what the record shows so far. Much of it comes from the company, so it is flagged as company-reported where no independent check exists.

  • First power, then commercial operation. Cape Station's first 33-megawatt GeoBlock synchronized with the grid on September 24, 2026. It began commercial operation on September 30 [2]. Fervo reports that it built and commissioned the block in 23 months [2]. Two more blocks are due by January 1, 2027, bringing the first phase to about 100 megawatts. A further 400 megawatts is under construction for 2028 [1][2].
  • Sideways wells, many from one spot. Fervo drills several wells from a single pad, then turns them horizontal through the hot rock. The technique comes straight from oil and gas [1]. In 2024 the company reported that its fastest Cape well took 21 days to drill, 70% less time than its first horizontal well in Nevada [5]. It credited diamond-studded drill bits from shale basins plus coolers that keep drilling mud from overheating [5]. Average cost per well across its first four horizontal Cape wells fell by roughly half, the company reports [5].
  • Deeper and hotter. In 2025 Fervo drilled an appraisal well to 15,765 feet in 16 drilling days, with a projected bottom temperature of 520 °F [4]. An independent engineering firm estimated that the Cape area could support more than 5 gigawatts of development, according to the company [4].
  • Wells wired with light. At its earlier Nevada pilot, Fervo and the oilfield services firm SLB ran fiber-optic cable through a horizontal well [7]. The fiber worked like a long chain of thermometers and microphones along its length. From the way temperature pulses moved, the team calculated where injected water was leaving the well. Their results closely matched a conventional logging tool [7]. That kind of data tells operators which cracks are taking water and which are not.
  • Buyers lining up. Fervo's Nevada pilot has supplied the grid since 2023, including power for Google data centers in that state [1]. The new Google agreement covers 396 megawatts, with an option for about 600 more through June 2030 [3]. Southern California Edison plus Shell Energy North America are also customers [1].
  • A public research site next door. The Department of Energy runs a geothermal field laboratory called FORGE near Milford, in the same county. Its research focuses on how to start and sustain crack networks in deep rock [8].

Water pumped down the injection well spreads through the cracks, picks up heat from the granite and rises through the production well to drive the plant [1][7].

When the ground pushes back

Opening cracks in deep rock has a known risk: it can disturb faults. The clearest warning comes from Pohang in South Korea. In November 2017, a magnitude 5.4 earthquake struck the city near an experimental geothermal plant [9]. A government-commissioned panel of Korean and foreign experts later concluded that water injected at the plant had activated a previously unknown fault [9]. The plant was shut down for good. Dozens of residents were injured in the quake and its aftershocks [9].

Pohang does not mean every EGS project will shake the ground. It does mean that monitoring the subsurface is not optional. Fiber-optic sensing and the crack-network research at FORGE are part of the answer, but no developer can see every fault in advance.

There are other open questions. A geothermal reservoir must keep delivering heat for decades. Cape Station has produced commercial power for about a month, so its long-term performance is still a forecast. Fervo cites an independent estimate that its reservoirs can recover 50 to 60% of the heat in the rock. The company says that triples the useful heat compared with conventional geothermal [4]. Time will test that number.

Geography matters too. Researchers estimate that southwest Utah alone holds more than 10 gigawatts of high-quality geothermal resources [1]. Bringing EGS to cooler regions means drilling deeper, which is exactly why the 15,765-foot appraisal well matters [4].

Finally, much of the speed and cost data comes from the company itself [4][5]. The milestones are public. The full engineering picture behind them is not yet.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities grounded in today's trends. They are not announced plans by Fervo Energy, Google or the Department of Energy.

The data center that sits on its own heat. Picture a server campus built beside a geothermal field. The plant supplies steady power for the computers. Leftover warm water heats greenhouses on site, so the same underground heat feeds both chips and tomatoes.

Wells that listen. Every well carries a fiber-optic line. AI software reads the stream of temperature and sound data, spots a crack that is taking too much water or a tremor starting to build, then adjusts pumping within minutes. The reservoir is managed more like a living system than a fixed machine.

A second career for the drilling rig. Oil and gas crews already know how to drill sideways through hard rock. A future where those rigs and skills move steadily into geothermal would turn one industry's know-how into another's foundation.

A battery made of rock. An EGS reservoir holds water under pressure. Operators might pump extra water in when solar power is plentiful at midday, then let it flow back faster in the evening peak. The crack network would act like a slow, natural battery.

Heat beyond the West. As wells reach deeper and hotter rock, regions far from Utah's hot spots could tap the same resource. Firm, clean power would no longer depend on living near a volcano or a geyser.

Back to the desert

Return to that stretch of sagebrush in Beaver County. Nothing about the view hints at what is happening miles below, where water loops through cracked granite and comes back carrying the planet's own heat.

The lesson of Cape Station is not that geothermal has won. It is that a trick learned in the oil field has crossed into clean energy and passed its first real exam. The next exams are harder: decades of steady heat, careful handling of faults plus costs low enough to compete everywhere.
