The Whisper Behind the Milky Way

The oldest hydrogen in the universe is still humming. To hear it, astronomers are teaching algorithms to subtract a whole galaxy, then checking that the algorithms are not hearing things.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

AI & DataSpace

A siren over the outback

Night falls over the red scrub of Western Australia. There are no streetlights here, no cell towers and almost no people. What there is, in growing numbers, is metal: antennas shaped like stiff little Christmas trees, planted in clusters across the desert for the SKA-Low telescope. By early 2026 crews had installed more than 15,000 of the 131,072 antennas the design calls for [1].

They are not listening for stars. They are listening for the time before most stars existed, when the universe was a dark fog of hydrogen gas a few hundred million years old [2].

That gas is still sending us a message. It is just very hard to hear.

The message arrives buried under the radio roar of our own galaxy. The faint hydrogen signal is measured in thousandths of a degree. The glare in front of it reaches tens of thousands of degrees [3]. One description compares the task to hearing someone breathe while a siren wails beside you [4].

So astronomers are doing something that sounds almost reckless. They are asking algorithms to subtract an entire galaxy from the sky, then trusting what is left over. The hard part is not teaching a machine to find a whisper. It is proving the machine did not make one up.

The hum that every hydrogen atom knows

Hydrogen has a party trick. Its single proton and single electron each spin like tiny magnets. Now and then the pair flips from lined up to opposed (or back again). When it does, the atom absorbs or releases a radio wave 21 centimeters long. Any single atom does this only about once in ten million years, yet the early universe held so much hydrogen that the combined glow is detectable [4].

Picture a single pitch that all hydrogen shares, like a tuning fork the size of an atom. Whether the gas rings louder or softer than the cosmic background tells you whether it was colder or warmer than the light around it [4]. Chart that pitch across the sky and across time to get a movie of the first stars switching on and heating their surroundings.

There is a catch. As the universe expanded it stretched those waves to meters long, right into the band that FM radio uses here on Earth [4]. Older, more stretched signals arrive at lower frequencies. The very oldest, from the cosmic "dark ages," fall below about 50 megahertz, where Earth's upper atmosphere scrambles them before they reach the ground [5].

And in front of all of it sits the Milky Way. Electrons spiraling through our galaxy's magnetic field fill the same frequencies with radio light. Depending on the instrument and the method, estimates put this foreground at thousands to hundreds of thousands of times brighter than the hydrogen signal [3][6].

Why does this matter now? Because the hardware is finally arriving. SKA-Low is growing station by station [1]. In August 2026 a team published plans for a suitcase-sized lunar satellite built to hear the dark ages [5]. That same month a pioneering experiment opened up its entire analysis pipeline and raw data for anyone to test [7]. The bottleneck is shifting from collecting radio waves to cleaning them.

What the cleaners can already do

The Milky Way's glare has one weakness. It is smooth. Its brightness changes gently and predictably from one frequency to the next, while the hydrogen signal wiggles. In principle you can fit the smooth part and subtract it.

In practice the telescope itself spoils the smoothness. Every antenna sees the sky slightly differently at different frequencies, which stamps fake wiggles onto the foreground [3]. Cables reflect signals. The ionosphere smears sources into halos. Satellites leak radio noise [3]. The cleaning job has turned into one of the busiest corners of machine learning in astronomy.

  • A learned model of the signal. The LOFAR telescope in Europe separates foreground from signal using Gaussian process regression, a statistical method that describes each component by how it varies across frequency. Its description of the hydrogen signal was learned by a neural network trained on simulations. From about 200 hours of data the team set upper limits two to four times tighter than its previous ones [6]. It has not detected the signal yet, only narrowed where it can hide.
  • Deeper limits from South Africa. The HERA array of dishes in the Karoo published first results from its upgraded Phase II in November 2025. It covered 50 to 250 megahertz in eight bands, reaching back to a time when the universe was a tiny fraction of its current age [8]. The team found that signals bouncing between neighboring dishes are now its biggest source of contamination [8].
  • A contest of algorithms. In an SKA data challenge, 17 teams tried to recover a hidden signal from simulated SKA-Low data, using methods ranging from Gaussian processes to U-Nets and Transformers, the neural network designs behind image tools and chatbots. Seven submissions came close to the true answer, yet most teams underestimated their own uncertainties. The best error bars captured only about 60% of the true errors [3].
  • Speed for the skeptics. A Cambridge team running graphics processors reports completing 1,052 full foreground fits in under two GPU-days. It estimates the same work would take about 100 CPU core-years on ordinary processors [9]. The test used mock data, though. Fast checks make it practical to try many models instead of trusting one.
  • A satellite that hides from Earth. The proposed CosmoCube would orbit the Moon and listen between 10 and 50 megahertz. For about 40 minutes of every two-hour orbit, the Moon would block Earth's radio noise. The team plans to rely on advanced Bayesian statistics to pull the signal from the galaxy's glare, with a cost target under €50 million [5].

None of these is a detection. Each is a tighter net.

Every layer above the hydrogen signal must be modeled and removed, which is why each cleaning method is now tested on data with no signal before its results are trusted.

The dip that would not go away

There is one famous claim of a detection. It shows why the cleaning step is so delicate.

In 2018 the EDGES experiment, a small antenna in the Australian outback, reported a dip in the sky's average radio brightness centered at 78 megahertz [10]. The timing matched the era of the first stars. The trouble was the depth. The dip was more than twice as strong as the largest theoretical predictions [10]. Explaining it would require gas colder than expected or a hidden extra radio glow, perhaps from dark matter interacting with ordinary matter [10].

Four years later, India's SARAS 3 experiment looked at the same frequency range and saw nothing. Its analysis rejected the EDGES profile at 95.3% confidence [11]. The dispute is not settled. In its 2026 pipeline paper, the EDGES team calls the feature hard to reconcile with theory and acknowledges warnings about instrument effects [7]. It also shows how much judgment sits inside any analysis: one quality cut removes 76 of its 138 days of data [7].

That is the twist at the heart of this story. A foreground cleaner is a model of what the sky should look like. If the model is wrong, it can erase a real signal or carve a fake one out of leftover noise.

Recent work shows both failure modes are real:

  • In a test on simulated data containing no hydrogen signal at all, three of five Gaussian process model variants still found evidence for one. The authors described it as "a spurious increase in model evidence" [12].
  • On simulated SKA data, both avoiding foregrounds and removing them could skew conclusions about the early universe. Under strict assumptions about modeling errors the skew from removal grew large enough to mislead [13].
  • A team building neural network tools warns that such models are highly problem-specific and can stumble when real data stray from their training simulations [14].

The honest answer, for now, is that nobody knows yet what the cosmic dawn signal looks like. The field's response is to make algorithms argue with one another. Teams run null tests on data with no signal, cross-check results between instruments and publish their pipelines so outsiders can try to break them [7][13].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities, not announced plans or predictions by any team named in this article.

The consensus of doubters. A full SKA-Low data cube arrives at a processing center. Instead of one cleaner, a panel of rival methods works on it: Gaussian processes, U-Nets, diffusion models. Each is first fed versions of the data with the signal deliberately removed. A feature is announced only if every method finds it in the real data and none finds it in the empty copies.

Two listeners behind the Moon. A lander on the far side and a small orbiter overhead record the same patch of low-frequency sky. An algorithm that has learned each antenna's quirks from in-flight calibration compares the two. A dip that appears in one instrument but not the other is flagged as hardware. A dip that appears in both earns a second look.

The replay festival. With raw data and analysis code public, students around the world rerun an old observation with thousands of different reasonable choices. The result is no longer a yes-or-no answer about a disputed dip but a map of which choices make it appear. Disagreement becomes something you can measure.

A sky model that never stops learning. Every new observation updates a shared, open model of the Milky Way's radio glare at low frequencies. Telescopes on different continents subtract the same galaxy, so their leftovers can be compared directly.

The quiet-sky alarm. Machine learning trained to spot the fingerprints of satellite leakage runs alongside the foreground cleaner. When a new spacecraft adds noise in a protected band, the system catches it within hours, giving regulators and operators evidence to act on.

Back to the scrub

Return to those antennas in the outback. Every night they record a sky that is almost entirely our own galaxy, with a whisper of ancient hydrogen tucked underneath. The whisper is real physics. Whether we have already heard it is still an open question.

What has changed is the method. The search is no longer just a contest of bigger antennas. It is a contest of better subtraction, done by algorithms that are themselves checked, challenged and replayed in public.

If that sounds familiar, it is the same problem the CosmoCube team faces in orbit, which we explored in The Satellite That Hides Behind the Moon. Distance from Earth helps. Honest math helps more.
