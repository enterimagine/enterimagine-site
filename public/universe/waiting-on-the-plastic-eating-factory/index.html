Waiting on the Plastic-Eating Factory

Enzymes can now turn old polyester clothes back into new T-shirts. The first full-scale factory is still waiting for its money. The reasons reveal where plastic recycling really gets hard.

1 November 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe6 min read

BiotechInnovationEnergyAI & Data

Batch number 100

In the summer of 2026, at a demonstration plant in Clermont-Ferrand in central France, a tank finished its hundredth run. Inside, shredded and dyed polyester from old clothes had soaked for a day in warm water laced with an enzyme. When the batch was done, more than 95% of that fabric had been broken back into the basic chemical building blocks it was made from [1].

A few weeks later, those building blocks had become T-shirts. More than a metric ton of the recycled material was turned back into polyester in Portugal, spun into yarn in Spain, then knitted, dyed and sewn in France, in a project with Patagonia, PUMA and Salomon [2]. The companies report it matched new polyester for strength and for how well it takes dye [2].

Meanwhile, in Longlaville in France's Grand Est region, the world's first commercial plant built for this process sat waiting. Its groundbreaking was held in April 2024 [3]. In August 2026 the company behind it, Carbios, said its financing would not close on schedule [4].

The enzyme works. The factory waits. That gap is the most interesting thing about plastic recycling right now.

A bottle that cannot become a bottle

Most plastic recycling today is mechanical. Bottles are washed, shredded, melted and remolded. It is cheap, but every cycle shortens the plastic's long molecular chains, a bit like photocopying a photocopy. Colored bottles, mixed fabrics and dyed clothing are especially hard to bring back to high quality.

Enzymatic recycling takes a different route. PET, the plastic in drink bottles and polyester clothing, is a long chain of two small molecules joined by chemical links called ester bonds. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up one specific chemical reaction. The right enzyme can snip those ester bonds, releasing terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, which can be purified and joined into brand-new PET [5].

The promise is a loop rather than a slide downhill. The catch is scale. Carbios designed its Longlaville plant to handle 50,000 metric tons of prepared PET waste a year [3]. The company estimates construction at €230 million, plus start-up costs, with bank loans backed by public export-credit agencies [6].

On August 3, 2026, Carbios said its September 30 target for closing that financing would not be met, because some parties were still reviewing what it called a first-of-its-kind project [4]. Production, previously expected in the first half of 2028, was pushed back with no new date [4]. In late September the company reported that a majority of lenders had approved and that a deal with a major beverage company should lift advance sales to 60% of the plant's capacity [7].

So the question is no longer whether an enzyme can digest a bottle. It is whether that trick can earn its place in a real industry.

What's real today

The science behind this moment has been building for years.

  • An enzyme from a compost heap. The best-known PET enzyme, called LCC-ICCG, began as a protein found in leaf-branch compost, where it helps break down the waxy coating on leaves [8]. Researchers engineered it to depolymerize, or unchain, at least 90% of used PET within 10 hours at 72°C [8]. The best variants cut PET bonds roughly 10,000 times more efficiently than the original enzyme [5].
  • Machine learning picks the mutations. A University of Texas at Austin team used a structure-based machine-learning model to choose which changes to make to a PET-eating enzyme. Their FAST-PETase works below 50°C and nearly fully broke down untreated plastic from 51 different products within a week [9].
  • Beyond bottles. In a paper in Science, a Beijing University of Chemical Technology team used a graph neural network, an AI model that reasons over networks of connected data, to find an enzyme that breaks down polyurethane, the stuff of foam mattresses. It converted 98.6% of its test material in eight hours [10].
  • A second company, a second plastic. Samsara Eco opened its first plant at Jerrabomberra in New South Wales, Australia, in September 2025. The company says it uses AI-crafted enzymes to break down nylon 6,6 and polyester [11].
  • Clothes back to clothes. The Clermont-Ferrand demonstration plant has now run 100 batches on packaging and textile waste [1]. The T-shirt trial then took recycled fiber all the way through industrial production lines [2].

The heat before the bite

Here is the twist. The enzyme step is the elegant part of the story. The energy bill sits mostly in the steps around it.

Enzymes struggle to attack plastic in which the molecular chains are packed tightly in orderly, crystal-like regions. Textiles in particular are built this way [12]. Before an enzyme can bite, the plastic usually has to be melted and rapidly cooled into a disordered, glassy state, then ground into fine particles [8]. Researchers at the University of Portsmouth report that these pretreatments account for a significant share of the whole process's electricity use [12]. Their attempt to swap melting for a solvent-based shortcut weakened the enzyme itself [12].

There is also a temperature ceiling. If the reaction runs hotter than about 75°C, the plastic starts to recrystallize. Conversion then falls [8]. That makes heat-tolerant enzymes valuable, yet it also means the reactor must stay in a narrow window.

The climate case is promising but not settled. Carbios has been reported as claiming about 57% lower carbon emissions than making new PET, without a named life-cycle study behind the figure in that report [13]. An independent model by the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory found 69% to 83% less supply-chain energy and 17% to 43% less greenhouse gas for a hypothetical plant. It also found that cost parity with new plastic was possible only at scale [14].

Finally, enzymes are picky. Today's methods work on plastics held together by ester or urethane bonds. Polyethylene, used in many bags and films, has no such weak point. A University of Konstanz study published in October 2026 found it showed virtually no breakdown in soil, while bioplastics broke down [15].

Enzymes will not recycle everything. They may recycle the right things very well. Dyed, blended clothing is exactly where melting and remolding struggle most. A process that thrives in that corner does not need to handle every plastic to find a lasting niche. It only needs to do one hard job better than anything else on offer.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

These scenarios are speculation by IMAGINE, not announced plans by any company, city or research group.

Closets that close the loop. If fiber-to-fiber recycling scales, a worn-out polyester jacket could return to the same factories that made it. Collection bins in clothing stores would feed regional plants instead of landfills or incineration.

An enzyme for every plastic. AI models that search vast libraries of protein sequences could produce a toolkit of specialist enzymes, one tuned for nylon carpets, another for foam cushions, another for mixed fabrics. Recycling plants might swap enzymes the way a print shop swaps inks.

Recycling plants powered by waste heat. Because the reactions run warm rather than scorching hot, enzyme plants could sit beside factories, data centers or power stations with spare heat. Our earlier article The Server Farm That Warms Your Living Room explored how such leftover warmth can be put to use.

Gentler pretreatment. The next breakthrough may come from enzymes that tolerate partly crystalline plastic, cutting the melting step that dominates electricity use. That would shift the energy balance more than any improvement in the enzyme's speed.

Cities that sort for chemistry. If enzyme plants become common, municipal sorting might separate plastics by the bonds that hold them together rather than by shape or color, steering each stream toward the process that can actually unmake it.

The factory and the bank

Return to the hundredth batch in Clermont-Ferrand. Old clothes went in. Fresh building blocks came out. On a technical level, that is a remarkable result for an idea that began with a protein from rotting leaves.

But a demonstration plant is a laboratory with plumbing. Longlaville is a bet that the process can run every day, at 50,000 metric tons a year, at a cost that buyers will accept. The bankers studying that bet are asking the same questions as the engineers: how much energy, how reliable, how cheap.

Innovation often stalls at this exact point, between the prototype that works and the first plant that pays. Our article The Reaction That Chose a Hand followed another piece of chemistry on that same journey from bench to industry.
