The Robots That Walk on What They Build

A lab at MIT has small robots that crawl across a lattice of snap-together blocks to build it. Could that become the fastest way to put roofs over people after a disaster?

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

RoboticsSmart CitiesSpace

A robot that uses its own work as a road

Picture an open lattice of identical cubes, each one a small cage of struts. A machine shaped like an inchworm grips the top face of one cube. It lifts its back end, swings it forward and plants it again. In its grip it carries one more cube.

At the edge of the lattice it lowers its load. The new piece settles into alignment with its neighbors. Then the robot steps onto the block it just placed.

That is the whole trick. There is no crane, no gantry and no scaffold. The structure is the robot's road, so every block it adds becomes floor for the next trip [1].

Researchers at MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms call these building blocks voxels. The word comes from 3D graphics, where it means a pixel with volume: a tiny cube that, stacked with millions of others, makes a solid shape. The lab's robots, nicknamed MILAbots, are built to crawl over voxel lattices and fasten new pieces in place [1].

The surprise is not the robot. It is the arithmetic. When the team modeled a one-story building made this way, the result emitted far less carbon than three familiar methods of construction. With enough robots, it could also go up faster [1].

That combination is rare in construction. It is also exactly what a town needs after a flood or a fire.

Roofs, hands and carbon

Shelter after a disaster is a race against the calendar. The Internal Displacement Monitoring Center's 2026 global report counted 13.6 million people still displaced by disasters at the end of 2025, according to a summary by Down To Earth [8]. In Los Angeles alone, the 2025 wildfires destroyed more than 16,000 structures, by one industry account [5].

Rebuilding runs into two walls. The first is people. A US contractors' association estimates the industry must attract about 349,000 net new workers in 2026 just to keep pace with demand [7]. That figure comes from an industry model, not a head count, but it points to a real squeeze on skilled crews.

The second wall is carbon. The UN Environment Program and its global buildings alliance report that buildings and construction account for about 37% of global CO2 emissions. The sector also takes nearly half of all the material extracted from the earth [6].

Much of that carbon is embodied carbon: the emissions released while making materials such as cement and steel, then hauling and assembling them, before anyone turns on a light. It is spent the day a building goes up.

The same report notes that half of the buildings that will stand in 2050 have not yet been built or renovated [6]. Whatever methods we choose now will be locked into walls for decades.

So the question is pointed. Can we build faster with fewer hands while also cutting the carbon locked into floors and walls? Robot-assembled voxels are one attempt to avoid trading one goal for the other.

What the lab has actually shown

The newest evidence is a peer-reviewed comparison, published in the journal Automation in Construction and described by MIT in April 2026 [1]. Here is what it and related projects establish.

  • A head-to-head test of block designs. The MIT team, with a co-author from the Swiss institute EPFL, compared eight existing voxel designs. It then added three new self-aligning designs based on the octet lattice, a pattern of triangles that resists bending the way a bicycle frame does [1].
  • Much lower embodied carbon, in a model. For a modeled one-story building, voxels cut embodied carbon by up to 82% compared with 3D-printed concrete, precast modular concrete or steel framing. Plywood voxels came in at about 17% of the carbon of printed concrete. Steel voxels landed at about 36%. Plastic voxels performed poorly [1].
  • Faster assembly, if you bring a fleet. Modeled on-site assembly took about 99 hours for steel or wood voxels, against 155 hours for existing methods. That figure assumes roughly 20 robots working in parallel [1].
  • NASA's version of the idea. At NASA's Ames Research Center, three robots built a shed-sized structure from hundreds of composite voxels. Two placed blocks while a third bolted them together from inside. The job took a little over 100 hours of robot operation. The machines corrected their own placement errors without vision systems [4]. NASA's goal is large structures in space rather than homes.
  • A field test on the calendar. In August 2025, Bhutan's state holding company, Druk Holding and Investments, signed a two-year program with the MIT lab [2][3]. It aims to develop robotic construction for the planned Gelephu Mindfulness City. Temporary housing and pavilions made from local materials such as wood, rammed earth and steel are among the planned uses [3]. The MIT team names a larger testbed in Bhutan as its next step [1].

Not every construction robot looks like an inchworm. In Pacific Palisades, the company Cosmic Buildings runs a mobile microfactory where ABB robot arms assemble wall panels for homes lost in the fires. The companies report that it serves more than 35 families. They also claim builds up to 70% faster than usual [5].

That is prefabrication rather than voxels. Still, it shows that robots are already working inside a real disaster recovery, not just in a lab.

Each voxel the robot places becomes the floor it stands on for the next trip. That is why the system needs no crane or scaffold [1].

The fine print on a snap-together house

Here is the honest catch. Nobody has lived in a voxel building yet. The carbon and speed figures come from a model of a one-story structure, not from a finished house standing through a winter [1].

The open questions are serious ones. The lab's own release lists insulation, weatherproofing, sheathing and the routing of plumbing and wiring as future work [1]. Performance under earthquakes, strong side loads from wind and fire has not been explored at building scale [1]. Those are exactly the forces a disaster shelter must survive.

The speed advantage comes with a condition. The 99-hour figure depends on about 20 robots running at once [1]. A single robot would take far longer. So the hard part after a disaster may be logistics: shipping, charging and coordinating a robot crew in a damaged town.

Cost is another blank. The release describes voxel construction as competitive but publishes no dollar figures [1]. Building codes are a further gap. None of the sources reviewed for this article describes a code approval or permit pathway for voxel homes. A new structural system has to prove itself through testing before it earns one.

The NASA results deserve care too. They show that robots can build strong, light lattices, but they were designed for space trusses, not for kitchens [4].

None of this makes the idea weak. It makes it early. The step from a lab model to a roof over a family is where most construction ideas succeed or quietly fade.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below imagine where robot-assembled voxels could lead. None of them describes an existing product, program or installation.

The shelter that arrives in crates. After a river floods, a cargo plane lands with pallets of plywood voxels plus a case of crawling robots. Within days, rows of one-room shelters rise on a school playing field. When families return home, the robots walk the shelters apart block by block. The parts go back into crates for the next emergency, leaving no rubble behind.

The field clinic that grows a wing. A temporary clinic opens as a single room. As the need grows, robots add a corridor overnight, then a second room, without closing the first. Staff wake up to more space instead of a construction site.

A city's block library. A coastal city stores voxels the way it stores sandbags. In ordinary years, the same blocks become bus shelters, market stalls or festival stages. That keeps the robots maintained and the operators practiced for the day the blocks are needed as homes.

Local material, shared machines. In a mountain valley, voxels are cut from local timber or pressed from earth. The robots and their software are shared between regions. Heavy material never travels far; only the know-how does.

From orbit back to the roadside. Lattice tricks first refined for space trusses find their way into light emergency bridges. A robot crew assembles a footbridge across a washed-out road while engineers check each joint from a tablet.

Back to the inchworm

The little robot stepping onto its own work makes a neat picture. The real promise is the system around it: identical blocks, many small machines and a building that doubles as a road. The lab's model says that system could sharply cut construction carbon. With a large enough robot crew it could also save time [1].

What it has not yet shown is a house that keeps out a storm. That is why the Bhutan program matters [2]. If field tests hold up, disaster relief gains a tool that is fast to deploy and light on carbon. If they fall short, the lessons will still feed robotic factories like the one rebuilding homes in Los Angeles [5].
