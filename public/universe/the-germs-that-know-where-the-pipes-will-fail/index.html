The Germs That Know Where the Pipes Will Fail

Bacteria quietly eat away at the concrete sewers beneath our cities. Now researchers in Japan are reading those microbes' DNA to predict which pipes are most likely to crumble next.

29 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart CitiesBiotechAI & Data

The morning the road opened

On a winter morning in January 2025, the middle of an intersection in Yashio, a city just north of Tokyo, gave way beneath a truck [5]. Over the following days the hole kept growing, from about 10 meters across to roughly 40 [4]. Wastewater service for about 1.2 million residents was disrupted and officials warned that rebuilding would take at least two to three years [4].

The culprit lay deep underground: a reinforced concrete sewer almost 5 meters wide, laid in 1983, that carried sewage from 12 municipalities and had corroded through at a bend [4]. In February 2026, an expert committee confirmed pipe corrosion as the cause. It also found that a camera survey of the pipe in 2021 had failed to capture usable images, so the risk had been underestimated [5].

Here is the strange part. The thing that ate the pipe was alive and it was leaving traces in every liter of sewage flowing past.

In October 2026, researchers at Tohoku University in Japan reported a way to read those traces. By measuring the DNA of certain bacteria in wastewater and feeding it to a machine-learning model, they could point to the pipes most likely to be badly corroded [1]. The sewer, it turns out, can tell on itself. And the idea reaches well beyond one country, because every city with old concrete pipes is fighting the same invisible enemy.

A city's hidden skeleton

Every modern city rests on a skeleton nobody sees. Japan alone has about 490,000 kilometers of sewer pipe [4]. Today about 7 percent is more than 50 years old, but that share is expected to reach 19 percent within a decade and 40 percent within two [4]. Road collapses linked to sewers still numbered around 2,600 in 2022, most of them less than a meter deep [4]. That figure had already fallen after a 2015 revision of Japan's Sewerage Act required more frequent inspections [4], a sign that looking more often works, if you can afford to look.

After Yashio, Japan's infrastructure ministry ordered a nationwide check of large, older pipes. Of 4,692 kilometers inspected, 748 kilometers, about 16 percent, were flagged as needing action [6]. The pressure is not unique to Japan. In the United States, wastewater infrastructure earned a D+ from the American Society of Civil Engineers in 2025 and the society estimates a funding gap of $69 billion a year for wastewater and stormwater systems [8].

The usual tool is a camera, pushed or driven through the pipe on closed-circuit television. It works, but it takes time, equipment and trained crews [3]. Japan's wastewater workforce fell from 47,000 people in 1997 to 26,900 in 2021 and crews typically reach a given pipe no more than once every five years [4].

Worse, age alone is a poor guide. Pipes laid in the same year can corrode at very different rates [3]. So the real question for a city engineer is not "how old is this pipe?" It is "where should my few cameras go first?"

Reading the sewer's microbes

The new approach starts with a fact sewer engineers have known for decades: much of the damage is biological.

  • Bacteria that make acid. In the slimy biofilm that coats a sewer, sulfate-reducing bacteria produce hydrogen sulfide, the gas that smells of rotten eggs. It escapes into the air above the flow, where other microbes turn it into corrosive compounds. A study of biofilms in a corroded sewer found the damaged surface at a pH of around 3, consistent with sulfuric acid attack [9]. The gas tends to collect where the flow is disturbed, such as at bends [4], exactly where the Yashio pipe failed.
  • Sampling the sewage, not the pipe. The Tohoku team collected wastewater from 40 manholes across a residential sewer network and measured DNA from sulfate-reducing bacteria in it [2]. Every organism sheds genetic traces, so a bottle of sewage carries a census of what lives upstream.
  • Mapping habitats like ecologists. The researchers used species distribution modeling, the technique ecologists use to estimate where an animal is likely to live from a handful of sightings. Here the "landscape" was the sewer: pipe slope and the area of buildings draining into each pipe [2].
  • Letting a forest of decisions vote. Those habitat estimates went into a random forest, a machine-learning method that builds many simple decision trees and lets them vote, alongside pipe age, diameter and slope [2]. Checked against inspection records for 3,498 pipe sections, the model picked out severely corroded pipes better when the microbial data were included [2].
  • One microbe stood out. A sulfate-reducing genus called Desulfobulbus was the strongest biological predictor of damage, outranked only by pipe age [2]. The work appeared in the Journal of Environmental Management on October 1, 2026 [1].

In plain terms: the model turns an invisible biological signal into a map with hot spots, which is exactly what a city needs to decide where to send its cameras.

A clue, not a camera

It would be easy to oversell this. The study covered a single residential network and the public release gives no accuracy figures, only that the microbial data improved the model [3]. The authors frame it as a screening tool to prioritize inspections, not a replacement for looking inside the pipe [3]. Nobody has yet worked out how often samples would need to be taken, or how much money the method would save [3].

The Yashio investigation is a sober reminder, too. Its committee concluded that rising corrosion risk could have been anticipated with closer attention, but that the collapse itself was not necessarily predictable [5]. Better maps shrink the odds of a surprise. They do not abolish it.

There is also a timing problem that no sensor can fix. Trenchless repairs such as pipe lining avoid digging up roads, but they last only around ten years and cannot be used on severely deteriorated pipes [4]. A warning only helps if it arrives while a cheap fix is still possible.

Now the twist. Cities already pour effort into sampling sewage, just for a different reason. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention runs a national wastewater surveillance system that tracks influenza, COVID-19, RSV, measles and other viruses in sewage, with data updated weekly [7]. The same bottles that warn of a flu wave might one day also warn of a crumbling pipe. A city's waste is turning into one of its richest data streams.

From a bottle of sewage to a risk map

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities grounded in today's research. They are not announced plans of any city, utility or company.

The manhole that samples itself. Small automatic samplers could sit in key manholes, collecting wastewater on a schedule and sending it to a lab, or one day to a portable DNA reader. The risk map would refresh with the seasons instead of once every few years, catching a hot spot as it forms rather than after it has done its damage.

One bottle, two diagnoses. A single sample could be split: one half checked for viruses for the health department, the other for corrosion microbes for the public works department. Two city services would share one sampling network and one data platform and each new sample would make both of their maps a little sharper.

Robots sent by microbes. Pipe-crawling inspection robots could be dispatched only where the microbial map glows red. Crews and machines would spend their hours where damage is most likely, not where the schedule happens to point.

A sewer layer for the city twin. A city's digital twin could gain an underground layer that blends pipe records, inspection footage and microbial data. Planners could see the street and the sewer beneath it at once and avoid repaving a road just months before digging it up to replace the pipe below.

Pipes designed against their tenants. Because slope and drainage area shape where corrosive bacteria thrive, engineers might lay new pipes with those habitats in mind, with smoother bends and better airflow, making them less welcoming from day one.

Back to the intersection

Return to that intersection in Yashio. The pipe beneath it had been surveyed, but the camera failed to capture it clearly [5]. The bacteria eating it, meanwhile, were sending a steady signal downstream that nobody was equipped to read.

The takeaway is that a city's infrastructure is not just concrete and steel. It is also an ecosystem and ecosystems leave evidence. Learning to read that evidence will not stop pipes from aging, but it could help cities fix the right ones first.

In The Planet in the Mirror, satellites and digital twins helped cities see their surface. This story looks the other way, down the manhole, at the part of the city no satellite can see.
