Who Frames the House After the Fire?

In fire-hit Los Angeles, robot arms now frame houses beside the lots they belong to, while the city learns that permits were never the only bottleneck.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart CitiesRoboticsAI & Data

A factory parked on the lot

On a burned lot in Pacific Palisades, a factory has moved in next door to the foundation.

It does not look like much from the street: a portable, roughly container-sized enclosure [5]. Inside, robot arms pick up lengths of lumber, cut them, drill them and nail them into wall panels [1][5]. A few steps away, a finished backyard cottage serves as the builder's local headquarters [3].

This is one of the stranger sights in Los Angeles after the January 2025 wildfires. The fires destroyed somewhere between 15,000 and 16,000 structures across the Palisades and Altadena [1][5]. Rebuilding a neighborhood normally means hundreds of framing crews, lumber trucks and months of noise on every street.

Here the idea is different. Bring the factory to the neighborhood rather than shipping houses from a factory far away. Let robots make the skeleton of the home, then let people do the rest.

The surprise is where the bottleneck has moved. For most of 2025, the story was permits. By fall 2026, permits are flowing, but finished houses are not.

From permits to people with hammers

A rebuild has stages. First comes the paperwork: plans, fire codes, utility sign-offs, a building permit. Then comes the slow physical work of framing, roofing, wiring and finishing.

At the end of 2025, the first stage was the choke point. By mid-December, permits had been issued for only about 16% of destroyed homes in Altadena and about 14% in the Palisades [10]. The average wait from application to permit was 131 days in the county and 79 days in the city [10].

Ten months later, the numbers look very different. By October 8, 2026, the Palisades had 1,317 building permits issued and 1,126 main structures under construction [8]. Yet only 50 homes had received a certificate of occupancy, the official sign-off that lets a family move in [8]. A compilation of City of Los Angeles permit data for the Palisades ZIP code, updated November 2, counts 70 completed homes [9].

In other words, more than a thousand houses are now somewhere between a bare slab and a front door.

That shifts the pressure to labor. Builders interviewed by the Los Angeles Times blamed shortages of workers, with many projects competing for the same crews at once [10]. They also cited utility backlogs and the redesign work needed to switch to non-combustible materials [10].

This is where robots enter the story. Framing is repetitive, measurable work. If a machine can do it quickly and precisely, the scarce human crews can move on to the parts only people can do.

What the robots are actually doing

Here is what is on the ground, with company claims flagged as such.

  • A mobile microfactory in the burn zone. In August 2025, the builder Cosmic and the industrial robot maker ABB announced a mobile robotic microfactory working in the Palisades. It uses ABB IRB 6710 robot arms along with ABB's RobotStudio software, which rehearses every robot move in a digital twin before the real cut [1]. A digital twin is a virtual copy of a machine or building used to test changes safely.
  • Robots frame, people finish. The robots cut, drill and assemble wall, roof and floor panels using conventional wood framing [3][5]. Crews then set the panels on the foundation and handle the finishes, such as cabinets, appliances and solar [3][5]. Cosmic's chief executive has described roughly a third of a home as robotic, rising to about half if factory-made plug-in components are counted [4].
  • Software does the paperwork. Cosmic reports that its AI model covers design, permitting, procurement, robotic fabrication and assembly, with computer vision checking quality on site [1]. That is a company claim.
  • Built for fire. The company says its homes use Class A fire-rated materials, avoid overhanging eaves or exposed vents [3] and include mineral wool insulation, metal roofs, sprinklers and tempered dual-pane glass [5]. They are designed to meet or exceed California's rules for the wildland-urban interface, the zone where homes meet wild vegetation [5][6].
  • A second robotic builder arrives. On August 31, 2026, Reframe Systems reported it was finishing a wildfire-resilient bungalow plus a backyard unit in Altadena [7]. Its model differs: a fixed regional factory makes components that are trucked to the site [7]. The company reports 10 homes completed nationally, 8 of them occupied [7].

Scale is still small. In October 2025, Cosmic had completed two backyard units in Los Angeles and had more than 40 rebuild clients, mostly in design or permitting [3]. In January 2026 it said it was building 20 to 25 homes there [4]. We found no public record yet of a completed, occupied single-family home it robot-framed in the burn zone.

How a rebuild runs through a robot cell

Only one of the six steps belongs to the robots. Two run on software, while three still depend on people, which is why a faster frame does not automatically mean a faster move-in date.

The numbers that do not line up

Speed and cost are the whole pitch, which makes them the place to be careful.

Almost every figure comes from the companies. Cosmic describes framing a 2,000 square foot home in about 10 days, then finishing in four to six months [5]. Elsewhere, ABB's announcement speaks of delivery in about 12 weeks [1]. Those two timelines do not match, so treat the faster one with caution. Neither CBS nor Metropolis found an independent benchmark [4][5].

Cost is even slipperier. Cosmic quotes roughly $550 to $700 per square foot [1][2]. ABB's announcement compares that with typical Los Angeles rebuild costs of $800 to $1,000 or more [1]. But a resident-run database of Palisades builder quotes found ranges of $650 to $850 [3]. A UCLA handbook cites some Altadena builders quoting up to $500 per square foot before soft costs, such as design or permit fees [6]. Whether robots save money depends heavily on the baseline chosen.

UCLA's review, as summarized by a local rebuild group, finds little evidence that off-site construction is reliably cheaper once foundations and site work are counted [6]. Its real advantages may be speed and price certainty. The same source stresses that factory-built homes are not automatically fire-safe; materials and design decide that [6].

There is a human question too. Robots that save labor could help when crews are scarce, but they could also shrink some trade jobs over time. Some residents also worry about neighborhoods looking uniform, a concern Cosmic disputes [3].

Finally, robots address only one bottleneck. Insurance gaps, utility approvals and the redesign work for fire codes stall many rebuilds no matter who frames the walls [10].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are imagined by IMAGINE. They are not products, projects or announcements by Cosmic, ABB, Reframe Systems or any city.

The disaster kit that ships before the smoke clears. A region prone to wildfires or floods could keep robotic microfactories in storage, the way it keeps generators and water tanks. After a disaster, the cells would roll out to staging yards within weeks, with pre-approved designs ready to cut.

Permits written in the same language as the robots. If a city accepted a verified digital building model as part of a permit, the same file could drive the robot cell, the inspector's checklist and the fire-code review. Fewer drawings would be redrawn by hand. Inspectors could also compare the panel on site with its digital twin.

Neighborhood-scale fire design. A single fire-hardened house helps; a whole street of them, with clear gaps, non-combustible fences and coordinated landscaping, could change how a fire moves. Microfactories building many homes on one block make that kind of planning easier to coordinate.

Variety by software. Robots do not care whether every panel is identical. A design system could deliberately vary rooflines, window patterns and porches from house to house, answering the fear of uniform streets while keeping the frames efficient.

Crews that train beside the cell. Instead of replacing framers, a microfactory could double as a classroom, where apprentices learn to program, supervise and repair robot cells alongside traditional carpentry.

The house that waits for people

Go back to that lot in the Palisades. The robot arms can turn out the bones of a house in days [5]. The slab is ready. The panels are stacked.

What happens next still depends on people: the electrician, the inspector, the insurer, the utility crew that connects the meter. That is the plain lesson of the robot microfactory. It can speed up the part of a rebuild that machines are good at, but a neighborhood comes back only when every link in the chain moves.
