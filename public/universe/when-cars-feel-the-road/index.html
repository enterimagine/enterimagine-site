When Cars Feel the Road

Millions of cars already feel every bump they drive over. A few now report potholes to the city. What if every brand shared that knowledge in one open format, so road crews could fix the crack before it becomes a pothole?

30 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe6 min read

Smart MobilitySmart CitiesAI & Data

A bump that nobody hears about

It is a wet evening in late winter. A bus pulls away from a stop and its front wheel drops into a shallow dip in the asphalt. The passengers feel a small jolt. A cyclist following the bus swerves around the same spot a few seconds later.

Behind them comes a car with an active suspension. Its computer notices the dip a fraction of a second after the wheel meets it and pushes the wheel back up to smooth the ride. The driver feels almost nothing. The car, in a sense, knows exactly where the road is starting to fail.

Then it forgets. The measurement is used for one smooth moment and thrown away. The road department will hear about this dip only months later, when frost has turned it into a pothole and someone files a complaint.

Here is the surprise. Cars are becoming some of the best road sensors ever built, yet almost none of what they feel reaches the people who fix the roads.

Roads fail slowly, then all at once

Potholes rarely appear out of nowhere. Water seeps into small cracks and into the layer beneath the surface. In winter it freezes and expands, pulling in more water. When the ice melts, the soaked base can no longer hold the surface up. Passing traffic then breaks it open [1].

That slow story matters for budgets. In a six-year study by the Washington State Department of Transportation, sealing cracks was the cheapest treatment on the list, at about a dollar per lane-foot. Digging out and patching a damaged section cost more than ten times as much [2]. Catch the crack early and the repair is small. Miss it and the bill grows.

The problem is seeing those cracks in time. Many cities survey their streets only every few years, with inspection vans or crews on foot. They rely on residents to report the worst damage. In between, the road wears out unseen. Meanwhile thousands of connected cars cross every street every day, feeling each change in the surface.

Cars are already learning to report

The pieces of a different system already exist, scattered across brands, countries and rulebooks.

  • Cars that feel the road. NIO's ET9 and ES9 use a fully active suspension from ClearMotion that calculates the force for each wheel in real time and can adjust up to 40 times a second. About 20,000 units had been built by September 2026 [3].
  • Cars that report damage. Since April 2026, Waymo robotaxis have flagged potholes to road departments in five US metro areas, including Phoenix, through the free Waze for Cities tool. About 500 potholes were found at launch. Cities receive the location only, not the size or depth [4].
  • Carmakers that share safety warnings. In Europe, the Data for Road Safety group lets carmakers, service providers and road authorities exchange hazard warnings generated by cars. When Ford joined in 2021, it began sharing anonymized signals such as emergency braking and fog-light use with partners including BMW, Mercedes-Benz and Volvo [5]. In September 2025 the group published its first impact report, showing data sharing in six countries, among them Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany [6].
  • A common language for sensor data. SENSORIS, an industry specification coordinated by the European transport body ERTICO, first released in 2018, describes a standard way for cars to send sensor data to the cloud, covering items such as weather, road infrastructure and traffic events [7].
  • A legal right to the data. Since 12 September 2025, the EU Data Act gives users of connected products, including cars, rights to the data those products generate. The European Commission's guidance names sensor signals among the data in scope [8].

Put together, the direction is clear. Cars can feel the road, some already report what they feel and Europe has shown that rival carmakers can share data when safety is at stake.

Hazards are shared, wear is not

Look closer and a gap appears. The European hazard exchange is built around sudden danger. Its eight categories, set out in EU rules from 2013, cover events such as slippery roads, obstacles, broken-down vehicles, reduced visibility and wrong-way drivers [9]. A warning lives for minutes or hours and then expires.

Road wear is a different kind of signal. A crack that widens a little each week is not a hazard today. It becomes useful only when thousands of passes are added up over months, so that a road department can see which stretches are getting rougher and plan repairs before winter. Today that knowledge stays with each carmaker, in its own format, if it is kept at all.

There are honest obstacles to changing that. Location traces can reveal where people live and travel, so any shared road map would need strong anonymization, with data merged from many cars before anyone sees it. Carmakers may see road data as a valuable asset rather than a public good. Sensors differ too: a jolt measured by a heavy SUV with active suspension is not the same as one measured by a small hatchback, so the readings would need careful calibration. And data alone fills no potholes. Road departments still need the budget and crews to act on what the map shows.

None of these problems look impossible. The hazard exchange already solved similar questions about trust and privacy for safety warnings. The open question is whether the same spirit can stretch from sudden danger to slow decay.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built from today's trends. They are not announced plans by NIO, ClearMotion, Waymo, Waze, ERTICO or any other company or authority.

One road map, every brand. Carmakers agree on an open road-surface message, perhaps as an extension of today's shared formats. Every connected car, whatever its badge, sends anonymous reports of bumps, cracks and potholes to a common map run under public oversight. No single brand owns the picture. No driver can be traced.

Fix the crack before the pothole. Each autumn, the road department opens the map and sees which streets grew rougher over the summer. Crews seal those cracks before the first frost. Fewer potholes appear in spring. Complaints drop because the repairs came first.

Bus routes, bike lanes and school streets first. The city decides that the shared map serves the most vulnerable road users first. Bus routes, cycle lanes and the streets around schools are checked weekly, so that a cyclist never has to swerve around the hole the bus felt yesterday.

The car that knows what is coming. The same map flows back into the cars. A vehicle approaching a rough stretch slows a little or prepares its suspension, even on a road its owner has never driven. Every car becomes both a road inspector and a beneficiary of all the others.

A report card for every street. Residents open a public version of the map and see how their neighborhood's roads are aging compared with the rest of the city. Road budgets become easier to explain, because everyone can see where the money went and why.

Back to the bump

Return to that bus, the cyclist and the car that smoothed the dip without anyone noticing. Each of them felt the same small warning. Today that warning disappears. In the future IMAGINE describes, it would join thousands of others on one shared map, long enough to reach a road crew before winter does its work.

The takeaway is that the hard parts already exist. Cars can sense the road, a few can report it and Europe has shown that carmakers will share data for safety. What is missing is one open standard for the slow wear that road departments need to plan repairs. That standard is the IMAGINE part. It is a choice, not a breakthrough.

In The City That Parks Itself we imagined streets freed from parked cars. In The Planet in the Mirror we saw cities studying themselves through satellites and digital twins. Here the mirror is the road itself, read by every car that drives over it. What if every car helped fix the road?
