The Brain Scanner That Lets You Fidget

Brain scanners built on quantum sensors no longer need a deep freeze or a perfectly still patient. That could open a window into the brains of children and of anyone who cannot lie motionless.

29 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe6 min read

BiotechQuantum Computing

"Now, try to keep perfectly still"

Imagine being seven years old and asked to sit motionless with your head pushed up into a giant, helmet-shaped machine. The helmet was designed around an adult male head, so yours rattles around inside it [6]. Its sensors sit behind a thick layer of insulation, because they must be kept at minus 269 degrees Celsius [3][4]. Move your head by half a centimeter and the recording can become useless [4].

That is what a conventional brain scan with magnetoencephalography, or MEG, has long asked of patients. For a fidgety child, it is close to impossible.

Now picture a different scene. The child wears a lightweight, 3D-printed helmet studded with sensors about the size of a toy building brick, wired to a small backpack [3][4]. She can turn her head and move naturally while the machine records what her brain is doing [3].

That second scene is no longer a lab curiosity. In September 2026, Stanford University switched on a 144-sensor system of this kind, the first on the US West Coast [1]. The same month, a prospective study in the journal Epilepsia reported that the technology closely matched the gold-standard method for locating where seizures begin [2].

The trick behind it is a cloud of atoms, a laser and a bit of quantum physics.

A whisper in a hurricane

Every thought, movement and memory involves electrical currents flowing through neurons. Those currents create magnetic fields outside the head and MEG measures them. The difficulty is size. The brain's field is around 100 femtotesla, roughly a billionth of the strength of Earth's own magnetic field [4]. Trying to detect it is like listening for a whisper in the middle of a hurricane.

For decades, the only sensors sensitive enough were superconducting devices called SQUIDs. They work only when deeply chilled, so they sit inside a cryogenic vessel with an insulating gap between sensor and scalp, which weakens the signal [4]. The sensors are fixed in place, so the head must not move. Physics World put the price of such a system at more than £2 million, before high running costs [4].

Those limits matter most for the people who could benefit most. In epilepsy that does not respond to medication, surgery can help, but only if doctors can pinpoint where seizures start. Mapping that often means placing electrodes inside the skull, an approach called intracranial EEG. Children face an extra hurdle: Wellcome reports that some young patients have waited until around age 18 for the scans they need [3].

A scanner that fits any head, works at room temperature and lets the patient move would change who gets to be scanned and when.

Atoms that feel the brain

The new sensors are called optically pumped magnetometers, or OPMs. Here is what has been shown so far.

  • How the sensor works. Each OPM holds a tiny glass cell of rubidium-87 vapor. A laser lines up the spins of the atoms, a little like turning a crowd of tiny compass needles to face the same way. A magnetic field from the brain tips those spins, changing how much laser light passes through to a detector. The light level becomes the measurement [4]. No cryogenic cooling is needed [3].
  • The biggest system yet, in a shielded room. Stanford's new scanner has 144 sensors, which a researcher there believes is the most of any OPM system worldwide. It sits inside a magnetically shielded room whose metal walls are 25 centimeters thick and weigh 13,000 pounds [1]. Planned studies range from how speech is processed to what grabs an infant's attention [1].
  • A clinical test in epilepsy. In a study published online on September 12, 2026, researchers in China recorded 90 minutes of OPM-MEG from 68 adults with drug-resistant epilepsy. At the level of brain sub-regions, the scans agreed with intracranial EEG about 90 percent of the time. Among 51 patients who went on to surgery, the scans predicted who would become seizure-free with a sensitivity of 85.7 percent [2].
  • Children first. In an earlier study in Brussels, five children aged 5 to 11 with epilepsy were scanned with both technologies. The OPM system recorded epileptic spikes with peaks 2.3 to 4.6 times larger [5], a benefit of sensors that sit right on the scalp [3]. The UK's first pediatric OPM-MEG clinic now operates at the charity Young Epilepsy [3].
  • Out of the hospital. The University of Nottingham is developing a fully mobile OPM-MEG scanner, backed by more than £3 million from the UK Ministry of Defense, to study the effects of blast exposure on military personnel at firing ranges and field hospitals [7].

The quantum part was the easy part

Here is the surprising caveat. Getting rid of the deep freeze did not get rid of the hardest problem, which is magnetic noise. Earth's field, passing cars and building wiring all dwarf the brain's whisper. OPM scanners therefore still sit inside heavy shielded rooms [1] and many add active coils that generate canceling fields so the patient can move without drowning the signal [4]. A wearable helmet, yes. A scanner you can wear down the street, not yet.

The clinical evidence is promising but early. In the Chinese study, the specificity was 65.2 percent [2]. In plain words, the scans were good at identifying patients who would do well after surgery, but less reliable at flagging those who would not. The study involved adults, so its results may not carry over unchanged to young children.

Movement has limits too. In the Brussels study, one child's head movements caused strong artifacts in the data [5]. Some of the most exciting work, such as a Toronto study testing whether early brain signals can predict a later autism diagnosis, has scanned 32 autistic children so far and has not yet published its main results [6].

Finally, OPM-MEG is still a research tool. It needs medical device approval before routine clinical use and the team behind the UK system hopes to reach that in 2027 [3]. Until regulators sign off, every scan is part of a study and none of this is a basis for medical decisions by patients.

How a cloud of atoms reads a brain

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities grounded in today's research. They are not announced plans of any hospital, university or company.

The scan that comes to the patient. If mobile scanners like Nottingham's mature, a shielded van could park outside a rural clinic or a school. A child with suspected epilepsy would be scanned in the parking lot, instead of traveling hundreds of kilometers to a specialist center.

Watching a brain learn to talk. Because babies can wear these helmets, researchers could follow the same children over years, recording how language takes shape as they babble, point and play, rather than only when they can lie still.

A sports-side check. After a hard tackle, a player could put on a sensor cap in a shielded room at the stadium. Doctors would compare the readings with a scan from preseason, adding objective brain data to a call that is hard to make from the sidelines.

Brain maps in motion. Today most brain scans freeze people in place. Wearable MEG could record the brain while someone walks, plays an instrument or talks with a friend, giving neuroscience data about real life rather than about lying in a tube.

Quantum sensors everywhere in the hospital. The same atom-and-laser trick could be adapted to other organs that produce magnetic fields, such as the heart or spinal cord. Wellcome notes that researchers are already exploring spinal cord mapping [3].

Back to the child in the helmet

Return to that seven-year-old. In the old machine, the best she could do was try not to move and often that was not good enough. In the new one, she can look around, fidget and still give doctors a clear signal from her brain.

The takeaway is that a quantum technology does not have to be a giant computer in a refrigerator. Sometimes it is a vial of atoms the size of a toy brick, doing something humbler and perhaps more immediately useful: letting a frightened child be scanned without being asked to turn into a statue.

In Running Out of Cold, we followed the scramble for the deep cold that quantum machines and hospital scanners depend on. Here is a quantum device that sidesteps that problem entirely. And in Medicine for One, we met gene-editing treatments designed for a single patient. A scanner that fits every head, even a toddler's, is personalization of a different kind.
