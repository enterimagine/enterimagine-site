The Spacesuit Swatches That Waited on Mars

For more than four years, a Mars rover has carried five small squares of spacesuit fabric. They have now shown scientists something future crews will need to plan for: the finest Martian dust does not let go.

SpaceRoboticsBiotech

Five squares on a robot's arm

Near the end of Perseverance's robotic arm sits a small calibration plate. Engineers use it to check that the rover's instruments are reading true. One piece of it is engraved with Sherlock Holmes's fictional address and doubles as a geocache [4].

Along its bottom row are five squares of material, each 20 millimeters on a side [1][4]. They are not there for calibration alone. They are pieces of spacesuits [3].

One is the tough woven outer layer of a suit. One is Vectran, a cut-resistant fiber used on glove palms. Two are Teflon, one plain and one with a dust-resistant coating. The last is polycarbonate, the clear plastic of helmet visors [3].

Those squares have now ridden through 1,645 Martian days of driving and drilling [1]. In late October 2026 a team led from Washington University in St. Louis reported what they found on them in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences [1][2].

The headline is small in every sense. The rover's X-ray instrument detected dust grains about 100 nanometers across, roughly 700 times thinner than a human hair [1]. Those grains had stayed stuck through years of rough treatment [1].

A rover built to hunt for signs of ancient life has quietly become the first spacesuit test lab on another planet.

The oldest enemy on the surface

Ask any lunar engineer what worries them. Dust will come up fast. During Apollo, Moon dust scratched visors, wore down the outer layers of suits and damaged seals [1]. Some crew members reported symptoms like hay fever [1]. After one Apollo 17 moonwalk, an astronaut described a sneezy "lunar dust hay fever" that cleared up the next day [6].

Size is what makes dust dangerous to breathe. On Earth, regulators track particles 2.5 micrometers across or smaller, known as PM2.5. These can travel deep into the lungs. Some can even reach the bloodstream [7]. For scale, a human hair is about 70 micrometers wide [7].

Mars dust brings its own chemistry. It is mostly silicate minerals but also carries perchlorates, salts that can disrupt the thyroid gland [1]. It contains reactive iron, which can damage cells [1]. A 2025 review in the journal GeoHealth added silica to the list, a mineral linked to the lung disease silicosis [8].

Until now, though, nobody knew how fine the finest Mars dust really was. Earlier measurements on Mars could only resolve particles down to about 3 micrometers [1]. Anything smaller was predicted, not seen.

That gap matters more each year. NASA updates its Moon to Mars plan annually. The current version sketches round trips of 850 and 980 days for a crewed mission at a 2039 launch opportunity [9]. Crews on such missions would spend months walking, kneeling and working in the dust. Any dust that rides back through the airlock on a glove or a boot can end up in the air they breathe.

Designers need numbers for that. A rover arm on Mars has just supplied some.

What the rover actually measured

The study used PIXL, an instrument on Perseverance's arm that fires a fine X-ray beam at a target. Each chemical element glows back with its own X-ray fingerprint, a little like a barcode [1]. Here is what the team reported.

  • A clever blank page. PIXL cannot detect the elements that make up Teflon. So when it looked at a Teflon sample, any signal at all had to come from dust sitting on top [1].
  • Smaller than ever seen on Mars. PIXL found fine particles under 2.5 micrometers and ultrafine particles around 100 nanometers [1]. The lead author built a method to estimate particle size from the X-ray data [2].
  • Different surfaces, different dust. On PIXL's Teflon sample and on the Vectran glove square, grains averaged about 2 micrometers. On a basaltic glass sample and on the Teflon suit square, sizes reached down to about 100 to 300 nanometers [1].
  • Static picks the winners. Grains pick up electric charge as they collide in the wind. Different materials then attract different sizes, which may explain the pattern [1][2].
  • The small ones stay. Larger clumps shook off over time. The finest particles stayed attached through years of driving and drilling [1]. The squares themselves showed little visible wear [2].
  • Familiar chemistry, with extras. The dust matched the iron- and sulfur-rich makeup seen by earlier missions. PIXL also found extra phosphorus and hints of calcium sulfate [1].

This builds on earlier work with the same squares. In 2025 NASA reported that about half of the material changes seen by the rover's other arm instrument, SHERLOC, happened in the first 200 days. Vectran appeared to change first [5].

Gentler on visors, harder on lungs

Here is the twist. In one way, Mars dust is kinder than Moon dust.

The Moon has no wind to smooth its grains. On Mars, wind has tumbled grains against each other for a very long time. The study's authors compare the result to river pebbles: smoother and rounder [2]. NASA describes Mars dust as less abrasive than lunar dust [1]. That is good news for visors, seals and joints.

But smooth does not mean harmless. The very finest particles are the ones that clung to the suit materials longest [1]. The lead author singled out the smallest particles as the hardest problem for life-support systems [1]. Shaking a suit clean will remove the big clumps. It will not remove the part most likely to end up in an astronaut's lungs.

So the worry moves. It shifts away from scratches on a helmet toward filters, airlocks and indoor air.

Some honest limits apply. These are thumbnail-sized squares bolted to a rover arm, not a suit bending at the knee inside a pressurized airlock. The study measured dust, not health effects. NASA notes that any risk likely depends on how much dust a person meets and for how long [1]. Only some of the five materials feature in the published summary.

There is also a hopeful thread. If static electricity decides what sticks, static can help remove it. NASA's Electrodynamic Dust Shield, which pushes dust off surfaces with electric fields, was tested on the Moon in March 2025 aboard Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost lander [1].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built from today's trends. No agency or company has announced them.

Suits with zoned skins. A future Mars suit uses different outer fabrics in different places. Palms, knees and boots get materials that attract the fewest ultrafine grains, while flexible joints use whatever bends best. The suit becomes a patchwork chosen by dust data.

The airlock that sorts by charge. Before a crew member steps inside, an electric field in the airlock pulls charged grains off the suit and onto a collector plate. The plate is swapped out like a vacuum bag. The dust inside becomes a sample for scientists.

Every lander as a crash-test dummy. Each robotic mission to Mars carries swatches of the next generation of suit materials. By the time people arrive, every candidate fabric has years of real Mars exposure on its record.

Dust forecasts for the crew. Habitat sensors track ultrafine particles indoors the way pollen counts are tracked on Earth. On high-dust days, crews shorten outdoor work or change out filters early.

Robots go out first. Dusty jobs such as hauling soil or clearing solar panels shift to robots. People step outside for the work that truly needs human hands and eyes.

Back to the plate on the arm

Those five squares were packed for Mars in 2020 to see how radiation would age spacesuit materials [3]. Nobody needed them to become dust traps. Yet after 1,645 Martian days they have delivered one of the most practical findings of the mission [1].

The takeaway is simple. Mars dust is smoother than Moon dust, but its smallest grains are stickier than anyone could prove before. Designing for Mars means designing for the dust you cannot see.
