The Night Shift on the Solar Farm

Dust quietly steals a slice of the world's solar power. A fleet of waterless robots, guided by drones and data, now works through the night to win it back.

31 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

EnergyRoboticsAI & Data

Brushes in the dark

It is two in the morning in the desert. The solar farm is silent, its long rows of panels black under the stars. Then, at the end of one row, a small machine wakes up.

It rolls onto the glass and begins to travel the length of the row, a spinning microfiber brush sweeping the day's dust off the panels. No water. No crew. Identical machines across the site do the same thing, row after row, while everyone else sleeps.

This is not a concept. One Israeli maker of these robots, Ecoppia, says its machines clean millions of solar modules every night [5]. In India, a single robot supplier reports cleaning systems commissioned on about 10 gigawatts of solar projects, with contracts covering nearly 16 gigawatts [6].

Why go to all that trouble at night? Because by sunrise, a clean panel and a dusty one are no longer equal. Across the industry, the difference adds up to billions of euros in lost revenue every year [1].

The surprise is how much of the answer depends on timing. Clean too rarely and dust can glue itself to the glass. Clean the wrong way and you scratch the very surface you are trying to protect.

The dust tax

Engineers call it soiling: the dust, sand, pollen, soot and bird droppings that settle on solar panels and block sunlight. It sounds trivial. It is not.

The International Energy Agency's photovoltaic research program, IEA PVPS, estimated in September 2025 that soiling cuts global solar energy output by 4 to 7% on average [1]. It expects droughts, dust storms and extreme weather linked to climate change to make the problem worse [1]. The US National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) puts a typical loss at about 5%, rising to 25% at some dusty sites [2]. Dust can pile up at anywhere from 0.01% to 0.5% of output per day [2].

Those percentages hide big money. MIT researchers calculated that for a 150-megawatt plant, losing just 1% of output costs about $200,000 a year in revenue [3].

The obvious fix is to wash the panels. But the sunniest places on Earth tend to be the driest. MIT estimated that cleaning solar panels worldwide uses about 10 billion gallons of water a year, enough drinking water for up to 2 million people [3]. That water often has to be trucked in and purified, so cleaning can make up about a tenth of a plant's operating costs [3].

Then there is the dew problem. Panels cool down overnight and collect moisture. That damp film binds dust to the glass in a process called cementation. The resulting crust can survive even heavy rain [4]. NREL research suggests that cleaning every day or every other day stops the crust from forming [4].

Daily cleaning by human crews with water across thousands of acres is not realistic. Daily cleaning by robots, at night when panels earn nothing, is a different story.

Robots, drones and a schedule

The night shift has three parts: machines that clean, machines that look and software that decides. Here is what is running today, much of it described by the companies themselves.

  • Robots that power themselves. Ecoppia's robots carry their own small solar panel, which charges their batteries during the day [5]. At night they sweep each row with microfiber elements and no water, then clean their own panel too [5]. The company reports about 9.8 billion liters of water saved since 2013, a figure for which no independent audit has been identified [4].
  • Daily cleaning at gigawatt scale. LEAPTING, a supplier founded in 2022, told Mercom India on October 29, 2026, that its robots now serve about 10 gigawatts of Indian solar projects [6]. According to the company, one robot can cover roughly 1.5 kilometers of panels, about one megawatt per row, moving at 16 to 22 meters per minute [6]. Robots can run daily, where manual crews might visit once every 15 days [6]. In late 2025 it agreed to supply waterless robots for a 2-gigawatt project with Indian energy group Adani [7].
  • Drones that spot trouble. Chinese firm Skysys pairs inspection drones with cleaning robots. It reports deployments at more than 1,400 renewable-energy sites covering over 40 gigawatts [8]. On floating solar farms in Indonesia, its drones produce a diagnostic report in about 15 to 20 minutes, replacing inspections done by boat [8].
  • Software that decides when. Ecoppia says it runs deep-learning analytics on site data to plan cleaning and predict robot faults before they happen [5]. IEA PVPS lists on-site sensors, modeling and machine learning among the tools for measuring and forecasting soiling [1].

The pattern across all of them is the same. Robots are only half the story. The other half is data that tells them where to go and when it is worth it.

One supplier says that across India plus the Middle East, more than 150 gigawatts of solar projects now use cleaning robots [6]. That is a company estimate, but it signals how fast the night shift has spread.

When a brush is the wrong tool

It would be easy to conclude that every solar farm should buy a robot fleet. The evidence says otherwise.

First, dry cleaning has a cost of its own. The MIT team behind a water-free cleaning method warned that dry scrubbing is less effective than washing and can permanently scratch panel glass [3]. Scratched glass lets less light through, which means a small loss that never goes away. Microfiber robot makers design around this, but the trade-off is real and depends on the dust.

Second, the weather matters. NREL figures suggest that regular rainfall can win back around 2% of output on its own [2]. At a site with frequent showers, the case for daily robotic cleaning is weaker than in a desert where it barely rains. The IEA PVPS fact sheet stresses that the right approach is site-specific, built into a plant's design from the start [1].

Third, much of the most exciting data comes from the companies selling the machines. The 150-gigawatt estimate, the water savings and the inspection coverage are all company-reported [4][6][8]. Independent, published comparisons of energy gained against robot costs across many sites are still hard to find.

Finally, robots are machines that live outdoors in sand, heat and wind. They need maintenance too. That is exactly why the leading suppliers now sell monitoring software alongside the hardware [5].

There is also a different path entirely. MIT researchers showed in the lab that an electric charge can lift dust off glass without water or brushes, recovering much of the lost power under controlled conditions [3][4]. It has not been proven at commercial scale [4].

The honest picture, then, is not "robots everywhere". It is robots where the dust is heavy, the water is scarce and the data shows the brushes will pay for their keep.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments. They are not announced plans by any company, utility or research group.

The dust forecast. Weather services already warn of incoming sandstorms. Picture a solar farm whose software reads that warning and holds the robots back until the storm has passed, then sends every one of them out the following night. Cleaning stops being a fixed routine and becomes a response to the sky.

A robot that reads the panel it cleans. A cleaning robot already travels every row, every night. Fit it with a small thermal camera and it becomes an inspector too, flagging a cracked cell or an overheating connector as it brushes past. The drone flies less often. Faults get caught in hours rather than weeks.

Farms that learn from each other. Thousands of sites quietly log how fast dust builds up, when dew forms and how much power each cleaning recovers. Shared across a region, those records let a brand-new plant start with a cleaning plan tuned to its local climate from day one, instead of learning the hard way.

Floating farms with a crew of drones. On reservoirs and lakes, where walking the rows is impossible, drones already inspect panels from above. Imagine small boat-like robots joining them, docking at the edge of each floating array to wipe away bird droppings and algae spray.

Brushless glass. If charge-based dust removal makes it out of the lab, the moving robot could someday give way to the panel itself. A thin conductive coating, switched on at night, nudges the dust off the edge. The night shift would still exist. It would simply be invisible.

Back to the desert at dawn

Return to that solar farm as the sky turns gray. The robots have finished their rows and parked at the ends, charging from their own little panels [5]. The glass is clear. When the sun comes up, the plant starts the day producing what it was built to produce.

Nobody notices. That is the point. Soiling is a slow, invisible tax of a few percent [1][2]. Winning it back takes no breakthrough material, just patient machines plus software that knows when they are worth sending out.

The bigger lesson is about where clean energy goes next. The first era of solar was about making panels cheaper. The next era is about keeping millions of them working at their best for 25 to 30 years [6]. That is a job for robotics and data as much as for physics.

In Planting the Sun, One Panel a Minute, we watched robots help build solar farms faster. The night shift is the sequel: robots that keep those farms earning long after the builders have gone home.
