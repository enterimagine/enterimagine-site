The Molecule That Sorts Electrons by Spin

Every electron carries a tiny quantum compass. Chemists in Japan used one-handed molecules to sort those compasses inside a solar cell, with no magnet in sight.

3 November 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Quantum ComputingEnergyInnovation

A compass in every electron

Hold your hands up, palms facing you. They are mirror images, yet no amount of turning will make the left one sit perfectly on top of the right. Chemists call this property chirality, from the Greek word for hand. Many molecules have it. Sugars, amino acids and DNA all come in left- or right-handed forms.

Electrons have a hidden property of their own called spin. It is a purely quantum feature, but a useful picture is a tiny compass needle that points either "up" or "down." Ordinary electronics mostly ignore which way the needle points. Sorting electrons by spin usually takes magnets.

In August 2026, chemists at the University of Osaka reported something stranger [1]. They added an ultrathin layer of one-handed molecules to the surface of a perovskite solar cell. Electrons flowing through films of those molecules came out with about 60% spin polarization, meaning one compass direction clearly outnumbered the other [1][2]. To picture that figure, imagine a turnstile that lets through 80 people facing north for every 20 facing south. The surplus of 60 out of every 100 is the polarization. The right-handed version favored one direction. The left-handed version favored the opposite [1]. The solar cell also got a little better at turning light into electricity [2].

No magnet was involved. The shape of a molecule did the sorting. That idea could matter far beyond one solar cell.

Why anyone wants to sort spin

Electronics that use spin as well as charge already exist. They are called spintronics. They usually depend on magnetic materials to pick out one spin direction. Magnets work, but they add materials, steps and constraints to every device.

The chirality-induced spin selectivity effect, or CISS, offers a different route. A 2026 review from Shanghai Jiao Tong University describes it as a quantum effect in which chiral structures filter electron spin without any external magnet [5]. The same review lists possible uses in spin valves, spin-based LEDs plus magnetic sensors. It also points to catalysis, including the reactions that produce hydrogen or oxygen from water [5].

That last link is where energy enters the story. Solar cells lose some of their hard-won power at the borders between layers, where positive and negative charges can meet and cancel out before they reach the wires. Perovskites, a family of crystals that can be made from liquid solutions at low temperatures, have drawn intense research interest as a solar material. The best single-junction perovskite research cells now reach about 28% efficiency on the US national laboratory chart that tracks records [9][10].

Getting more out of those borders is one of the field's central puzzles. Chemists already coat perovskite surfaces with thin layers of molecules to heal defects. The Osaka team asked a further question: does the handedness of those molecules matter? Put more broadly, can a quantum property tied to molecular shape help carry charges across the border more cleanly?

From DNA to solar panels

CISS was not discovered in a solar lab. It grew out of curiosity about biology.

  • DNA as a spin filter (2011). Researchers at Israel's Weizmann Institute of Science and Germany's University of Münster attached single layers of double-stranded DNA to gold. Electrons of one spin passed through far more readily than electrons of the other [3]. Longer DNA sorted better. Single strands or damaged DNA did not show the effect [3]. The team linked the preference to DNA's handedness [3].
  • A name and a promise. Weizmann scientists call the phenomenon chiral induced spin selectivity. They have suggested it could lead to spintronic memory that is easier to make and uses less energy, as well as cheaper hydrogen production [4].
  • Water splitting with fewer side products. In a study led from Weizmann and Eindhoven University of Technology, chiral dye coatings on a titanium oxide electrode produced more current than non-chiral versions [6][7]. They also all but eliminated hydrogen peroxide, an unwanted side product [6][7]. The proposed reason is that oxygen gas forms most easily when the electrons involved have matching spins [6].
  • Handedness changes behavior (April 2026). A team led from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem with Weizmann reported in Science Advances that spin interacts differently with each mirror-image form of a molecule as electrons move through it [8]. The effect appears in motion, not at rest. It may also help explain why life favors one hand [8].
  • The Osaka solar cell (August 2026). The Osaka group built hole-transport molecules, which carry positive charges out of a solar cell, on a "bifacial" core with different chemical groups on each face [1][2]. Used as an ultrathin passivation layer, which smooths over defects on the perovskite surface, the right-handed version gave cells a 20.64% efficiency [2]. The mirror-image mixture reached 20.21%. A non-chiral version reached 19.71%. Untreated cells reached 19.48% [2]. Films of the one-handed material also moved holes about three times faster than the mixed or non-chiral films [2]. The team found the same link between handedness and spin direction in two other classes of materials it had studied [1].

A small gain with a big question mark

Read the numbers carefully. Most of the improvement over untreated cells comes from adding a passivation layer at all. The extra boost from using one hand instead of a mixed batch is less than half a percentage point [2]. That is a real difference, but it is a nudge, not a leap.

The cause is also unsettled. The authors write that a direct causal link between CISS and the faster hole movement has not been established [2]. They describe the mobility jump as unexpected and say it raises new questions [1]. Spin sorting and better charge transport sit side by side in the data. Nobody has yet shown that one causes the other.

Durability tells a familiar story. Every version of the cell kept about 70% of its starting efficiency after 45 days in ordinary air, with no meaningful difference between treatments [2]. The team traced the decline to moisture absorbed by water-loving side chains on the new molecules, not to chirality [2].

There is a wider scientific debate too. The Shanghai review lists precise synthesis, mechanisms under working conditions plus standardized measurements as open challenges for the field [5]. Until labs agree on how to measure spin polarization, results from different groups will not always be easy to compare.

So the honest summary is this. Molecular handedness can sort electron spin at room temperature, inside a working device. Whether that sorting is what makes the device better is the question the next round of experiments must answer.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are imagined possibilities grounded in current research. They are not announced plans by the University of Osaka, the Weizmann Institute or any company.

Solar skins with a twist. Picture a perovskite panel built with one-handed layers on both sides of the light-absorbing crystal. One coating ushers spin-sorted holes out of one face while a mirror-image coating guides electrons out of the other. Engineers would tune handedness the way they tune thickness today.

Cleaner green hydrogen. An electrolyzer splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. Imagine its oxygen-side electrode painted with a chiral film that keeps electron spins aligned. Less energy would go into side products like hydrogen peroxide. Electrodes might also last longer because fewer corrosive byproducts form.

Memory without magnets. Chips that store data in spin today rely on carefully grown magnetic layers. A future memory cell might use a single coat of chiral molecules as its spin filter, printed rather than grown. That would make spin-based memory cheaper to manufacture in large volumes.

Handedness on demand in the chemical plant. Many medicines must be made in only one mirror form. If spin-sorted electrons can favor one hand of a reaction, a factory might steer chirality with an electrode instead of an expensive catalyst. The idea connects lab chemistry to the old question of how life chose its hand.

Quiet interfaces for quantum devices. Quantum computers built from electron spins struggle to keep those spins steady. A chiral layer between a spin qubit and its wiring might act as a gatekeeper, letting through mostly the spin the device expects. This one sits furthest from today's results.

Back to your hands

Look at your hands again. Their mirror symmetry once seemed like a curiosity of biology. Now it is turning into a tool. A molecule's handedness can decide which way an electron's tiny compass points as it passes through.

The Osaka solar cell does not change the solar industry by itself. Its gain over a mixed batch is small and its cause is still debated. What it shows is that a quantum effect discovered in DNA can survive inside a practical energy device at room temperature. That is the kind of crossing this series exists to follow.
