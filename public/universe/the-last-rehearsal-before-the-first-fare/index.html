The Last Rehearsal Before the First Fare

Electric air taxis are flying real airport routes in Florida, Texas and Dubai, yet nobody has bought a ticket. Here is what still stands between the rehearsal and the first fare.

1 November 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

Smart MobilityEnergySmart Cities

A bay crossing with history

On the morning of October 30, 2026, a six-propeller electric aircraft lifted off from Albert Whitted Airport on the downtown waterfront of St. Petersburg, Florida. It tilted its rotors forward and headed across the bay to Tampa International Airport [1].

The route was chosen on purpose. On January 1, 1914, a flying boat crossed the same stretch of water on what is described as the world's first scheduled commercial airline flight [1]. More than a century later, a very different machine retraced it.

This time the cabin held no paying customer. The flight was a demonstration under a federal pilot program, with a pilot at the controls and data flowing to regulators rather than fares flowing to a ticket office [1][2].

That is where electric air taxis stand at the end of 2026. The aircraft fly. The first terminals are built. The rules are being written in public. Yet nowhere in Dubai, Florida, Texas or China has a company reported carrying a paying passenger [3][4].

The first fare is close. It is also much harder to reach than the first flight was.

Why the fare is the finish line

A test flight proves that an aircraft can fly. A paid flight proves something harder: that a regulator trusts it with ordinary members of the public, on a timetable, day after day.

That trust is built in stages. In the United States, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) approves a new aircraft design through type certification, which works like a driving test for an entire model rather than for one vehicle. Joby Aviation reports that it is in the fifth and final stage of that process [5]. Archer Aviation says it has entered the last phase of the FAA's sequence. It now targets US paying passengers before 2028 rather than its earlier goal of 2026 [6].

Some regulators are moving faster. Dubai's Roads and Transport Authority (RTA) has said commercial air-taxi flights will begin before the end of 2026 [3]. The first phase will cap daily flights, operating hours and passenger numbers, then grow only as safety and reliability data come in [3]. Joby plans to step from test flights to proving runs, then to free passenger flights, before any full commercial launch [7].

China took an early lead, then slowed. In March 2025 EHang's two operating companies received certificates that allow ticketed sightseeing flights in its pilotless two-seater [8]. By August 2026 the company still described both sites as running trial operations. It said a separate light-aircraft accident in June had slowed approvals for passenger service across the sector [4].

So the question of the moment is easy to ask and hard to answer: who sells the first ticket? And what will it take?

What's real today

The pieces of a working service already exist in the physical world.

  • A terminal beside a giant airport. Dubai's first air-taxi terminal, called DXV, sits next to Dubai International Airport. It covers 3,100 square meters over four floors, includes electric charging and is designed for about 42,000 landings a year [9]. Three more sites are planned, at Dubai Mall, Atlantis The Royal on Palm Jumeirah and American University in Dubai [10][7].
  • A desert flight between two sites. In November 2025 a piloted Joby aircraft flew for 17 minutes from the company's test site at Margham to Al Maktoum International Airport. The RTA called it the first crewed electric air-taxi flight between two locations in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) [10].
  • Aircraft built for short hops. Joby's design seats a pilot plus four passengers on six tilting propellers fed by four lithium-ion battery packs; the company lists a top speed of 200 mph [11]. Each of its six motors weighs 28 kilograms yet can briefly deliver 236 kilowatts [7]. Archer's Midnight is another five-seat design, with 12 propellers and six battery packs, aimed at trips of 20 to 50 miles [12].
  • A federal rehearsal in real airspace. In March 2026 the US Department of Transportation and the FAA picked eight projects across 26 states for an eVTOL Integration Pilot Program, whose data will shape future rules [2]. An eVTOL is an electric aircraft that takes off and lands vertically like a helicopter, then cruises on a wing like a plane. In September Joby flew in Texas, including into the controlled airspace of Dallas Fort Worth International Airport [13]. In late October it moved to Florida [1].
  • A fresh certification path in the UAE. In May 2026 the UAE's General Civil Aviation Authority placed Archer's Midnight on a restricted type certificate track, a route toward limited commercial flying [14].

The hover tax

Here is the twist that rarely makes the promotional videos. An air taxi is at its most efficient on the trips people least associate with taxis.

Lifting off straight up is expensive. In the first and last moments of every flight, the propellers must hold the full weight of the aircraft on thrust alone, like a swimmer treading water. Once the aircraft tilts forward and its wing takes over, each kilometer becomes far cheaper.

A peer-reviewed study in Nature Communications put numbers on that trade-off [15]. On a 100-kilometer trip with a pilot plus three passengers, a battery-powered VTOL produced 52% less greenhouse gas per passenger-kilometer than a gasoline car. It also beat a battery-electric car by 6% [15]. With only one person aboard, it still beat the gasoline car by 35% but produced 28% more than the electric car [15].

Shorter trips flip the result. Below about 35 kilometers, a single-occupant flight did worse than a gasoline car, because the climb and hover dominate [16]. The average commute in the study's US data was about 17 kilometers [16].

That exposes a real tension. The routes planners picture first, short crosstown hops, are where the energy case is weakest. The routes where it is strongest, longer trips with full seats, look more like regional flights than taxis.

Batteries add a second limit. Trade data list EHang's two-seater at about 35 kilometers of range, roughly 21 minutes of flight and around two hours of charging [17]. EHang reports that a mobile battery-cooling vehicle lets each aircraft fly 12 to 15 times a day [17]. A taxi that spends more time on the charger than in the air struggles to earn its keep.

Even the plug is unsettled. Joby uses its own charging system, while Archer chose chargers built on the CCS standard common in electric cars [18]. And safety stays the gate nobody can skip. In China, an accident involving a different light aircraft was enough to slow passenger approvals [4].

None of this means the idea fails. It means the first services that last will probably be careful, full and longer than the brochures suggest.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

These scenarios are speculation by IMAGINE, not announced plans by any company, city or regulator.

The airport shuttle that sells seats in pairs. The likeliest first market is the hop between a city center and its airport on a fixed route. Operators could hold departures until most seats are sold, because a full cabin is exactly where the energy math looks best.

Terminals that double as grid batteries. A rooftop terminal must deliver big bursts of power between flights. Paired with stationary batteries, it could sip from the grid overnight or from rooftop solar by day, then release that energy quickly when an aircraft lands. Our earlier article Batteries in Waiting explored how stored energy can smooth demand like this.

Timetables written by the weather. Because hover is so costly, a headwind or a hot afternoon changes how many trips one charge allows. Dispatchers may plan the day the way sailors plan a voyage, adding or trimming flights as forecasts shift.

Noise maps as city infrastructure. If cruise really is quiet, the loud moments will cluster at takeoff and landing. Cities could treat sound the way they treat traffic, publishing live noise maps around each terminal and steering routes over rivers, highways and rail lines.

The pilot's seat goes to a passenger. Removing the pilot frees a seat, which improves every per-passenger energy figure in the study. That is a long road through certification, but the groundwork is visible: in September 2026 Joby reported a converted cargo plane flying across the United States with no control inputs from its onboard safety pilot [19].

Back over the bay

Return to the bay. In 1914 a flying boat proved that an aircraft could keep a schedule across those waters. In 2026 an electric aircraft repeated the crossing to prove it could be trusted with one.

The hardware exists, the first terminal stands ready and regulators on two continents are writing rules in public. What remains is the least glamorous step: showing that a battery-powered machine can carry ordinary people safely, on time, with enough seats filled to beat the car it replaces.

Dubai may sell the first fare before the year is out, or the date may slip. Either way, the real test will not be the first paid flight. It will be the thousandth, flown on an ordinary afternoon that nobody films.
