Planting the Sun, One Panel a Minute

The world now adds more than 600 gigawatts of solar power a year and much of it is still lifted into place by hand. A new generation of robots is taking over the heaviest part of the job. Here is what they can do and what they still cannot.

28 October 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

EnergyRoboticsAI & Data

Four robots in the Mojave

Near California City, in the sandy heat of Kern County, California, rows of empty steel trackers run toward the horizon. They belong to Bellefield, a complex that the energy company AES is building to hold 1 gigawatt of solar power and 1 gigawatt of battery storage [1].

Along those rows move four machines called Maximo. Each one grips a solar module, a slab of glass and silicon weighing up to about 34 kilograms (75 pounds) [3], swings it into position and sets it on the tracker. Then it reaches for the next one, more than once a minute [2]. By March 2026, working in parallel, the fleet had installed 100 megawatts of panels at the site [1][2].

That might sound routine. It is not. Until recently, most robotic solar installations had been small tests, often a single robot placing a few hundred modules [1].

The world is now building solar faster than it can find people to carry the panels. The robots have arrived just as the panels themselves got too big to carry comfortably.

Bigger fields, heavier glass, fewer hands

Solar is being built at a pace never seen before. The International Energy Agency counts more than 600 gigawatts of solar added worldwide in 2025, about 12% more than in 2024 [5]. The International Renewable Energy Agency, which counts capacity on a different basis, reports 511 gigawatts [6]. Either way, it is a record.

In the United States, installations reached 11.4 gigawatts in the second quarter of 2026, up 45% on a year earlier and utility-scale projects grew 61% [7].

The people who build these plants are harder to find. A US industry analysis projects a gap of about 53,000 solar workers by late 2026 and 86% of solar employers report difficulty hiring [8].

The panels are also heavier than they used to be. Manufacturers keep making modules larger to squeeze more power from each one. A current 700-watt-class module from Trinasolar is about 2.4 meters long and weighs 38.3 kilograms [9]. Lifting hundreds of those a day, in desert heat, is hard physical work. The US Environmental Protection Agency counts 986 heat-related worker deaths in the United States between 1992 and 2022 and 334 of them were in construction [10].

Multiply that by the size of a modern plant. The Goorambat East solar farm in Victoria, Australia, alone holds more than 517,000 panels [12]. Every one of them has to be picked up, carried, aligned and fixed in place.

So the bottleneck is no longer only factories or financing. It is the last 100 meters: getting each panel from the pallet onto the tracker.

The new construction crew

Several companies are now moving from pilots to fleets.

  • Maximo in California. At Bellefield, the fleet grew from one robot to four, with the current version placing 400–500 modules per shift [1]. AES trained the machines in simulation with Nvidia's tools and handles their data on Amazon Web Services [1]. In an August 2026 interview, the company reported more than 180,000 modules and over 100 megawatts installed and up to 24 modules per person per hour, which it says is more than double the industry average [3].
  • Australia builds a robot industry. On October 13, 2026, the Australian Renewable Energy Agency (ARENA) committed up to AUD 32.3 million to two projects. Luminous Robotics receives up to AUD 17.8 million for a factory in Melbourne aiming at about 100 LUMI installation robots by 2029. Built Robotics receives up to AUD 14.5 million for a Brisbane hub to produce 12 robotic pile-driving systems [11].
  • LUMI in the field. Two LUMI robots are already working at Goorambat East, with three more planned for the 440-megawatt Culcairn farm in New South Wales [12]. The company reports that one robot and two workers can install up to 75 modules an hour [12].
  • A Chinese entrant. On September 30, 2026, TrinaTracker, part of Trinasolar, launched an installation robot called Buildex. The company claims it can place up to 90 modules an hour and level itself on slopes of up to 17 degrees [13].
  • Robots before the panels. Built Robotics' RPD 35 drives the steel piles that hold up the trackers. It carries 224 piles at a time and uses satellite positioning and cameras to place each one [14]. Terabase Energy takes another approach: a "field factory" set up on site that pre-mounts modules onto tracker tubes on an automated line, after which rovers carry the finished sections into place. The company says one line running around the clock can install more than 20 megawatts a week [15].

What ties these machines together is perception. Maximo's vision system identifies trackers, modules and people and calculates depth, tilt and placement without being retrained for each new site, according to the company [3]. That is the AI & Data half of the story: a robot that can see where a panel goes is worth far more than one that can only lift it.

The robot does not work alone

Look closely at the claims and a different picture emerges. These robots are not replacing construction crews. They are changing what the crews do.

AES says each Maximo runs with a two-person team, one person driving and one monitoring and that sensors stop the robot when someone steps into its zone [4]. ARENA notes that LUMI places the modules while workers on site still fasten them down [16]. At Bellefield, union technicians work alongside the machines [1].

Most of the headline speeds are company figures, not independent measurements. The "3.5 times faster" and "6.2% cheaper" figures for LUMI appear in ARENA's own description as goals of the project, not results [16]. We found no independent data on cost savings.

Real sites are also messier than demo videos. Robots have slope limits [13]. Pile drivers still need excavators or telehandlers to load them [14]. And contractors must fit each robot's workflow into planning, purchasing and logistics, which PV Tech describes as a real hurdle for the largest builders [1].

The jobs question deserves a straight answer, too. When the Chinese company Leapting's robot made its commercial debut at Culcairn in 2025, it installed about 10,000 modules at around 60 an hour, compared with 90 to 120 per eight-hour day for a person working by hand [17]. Coverage described it as doing the work of three to four people [17]. With a projected shortage of 53,000 workers [8], the near-term effect may be to fill gaps rather than empty jobs. Whether that holds as fleets grow is an open question.

Who does what on a robotic solar site

Machines take the heavy, repetitive driving, lifting and placing; people still run the robots, fasten the panels and check the work [1][4][16].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation ahead. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built from today's trends. They are not announced plans by AES, Luminous Robotics, Trinasolar, Terabase or anyone else.

Panels designed for robot hands. Today's modules are designed to be lifted by people. A future module could carry standard grip points and snap-in clips, the way shipping containers carry standard corner fittings, so any robot from any maker could handle any panel.

The cool-hours shift. Heat is one of the biggest risks on a desert site. A fleet that works through the night under lights could move the heaviest lifting to the coolest hours, leaving people the daytime tasks of inspection and fastening.

The assembly line that walks. Picture a field factory and a robot fleet working as one moving line: piles driven ahead, tracker sections built on site, panels placed behind, the whole system advancing across the land like a combine harvester through wheat.

The builder that stays to maintain. The same perception that places a panel could spot a cracked one. Robots that build a plant could return as its maintenance crew, cleaning, inspecting and swapping damaged modules, which would then go to recycling.

A digital twin from day one. A robot could log every panel it places, with its exact position and time. A solar farm built this way would start life with a complete map of itself, ready for AI tools that predict faults before they cost power.

Back to the desert

Return to those four robots in the Mojave, reaching for the next panel and the next. They are not a sign that people are leaving the solar field. They are a sign that the field has outgrown human muscle alone.

The takeaway: building clean energy is increasingly a construction challenge, not only a technology one. Robots that can see and lift are one answer and in 2026 they moved from test plots to fleets and factories. The people beside them are still essential, but their work is shifting from carrying glass to running machines.

In The Robot in the Next Aisle, we followed robots learning to share space with people. In Where No Hand Can Reach, a robot went where no person could survive. On a solar farm, the robot simply goes where people should not have to strain.
