A Power Bank With a Trailer Hitch

In a Bavarian freight yard, an electric truck spent its downtime lighting a building and charging another truck. The test worked. What stands in the way now has little to do with engineering.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

Smart MobilityEnergy

The cable that ran backward

It is evening at a freight yard near Regensburg, in southern Germany. The day's deliveries are done. A battery-electric truck rolls into its bay, the driver plugs in a thick cable and heads home.

Usually that is the whole story: the truck sips power overnight and leaves full in the morning. On this lot, during a research project that reported its results at the end of October, the cable sometimes ran the other way. The truck's battery pushed electricity back out, keeping a building supplied after dark, topping up a second truck or feeding the public grid [1][4].

The vehicle was a MAN eTGX carrying 480 kilowatt-hours of usable battery energy [1]. A kilowatt-hour is what a one-kilowatt appliance, say a small space heater, uses in one hour. This single truck parks with enough stored electricity to run that heater for 480 hours, about twenty days without a break.

The partners called it a power bank on wheels [4]. The surprise is not that the trick worked. Engineers expected it would. The surprise is where the project says the real obstacles now sit: not in the truck and not in the charger, but in grid connections, contracts and the rules for selling electricity [1][2].

The yard that runs out of amps

Electric trucks need a lot of power and they tend to want it in the same place at the same time. When a whole fleet returns to its depot in the evening, the site can ask the local grid for far more electricity than its connection was built to carry. The project behind the Regensburg test names this directly: at many company sites, the grid connection is too small for a larger electric fleet [1][2].

Fixing that can mean new cables, a bigger transformer and a long wait for the grid operator. The wider grid has a timing problem of its own. Solar and wind produce when the weather allows, not when people need power, so electricity prices swing through the day.

Seen from that angle, a parked truck stops looking like a burden. A regional truck spends many hours standing at its depot. Its battery is large and it is already paid for. If it charges when power is cheap and gives some back when power is scarce, idle hours turn into a modest energy business.

That is the promise of bidirectional charging. An ordinary charger moves current one way, from the grid into the vehicle. A bidirectional charger can reverse the flow, so the battery behaves like a temporary storage unit for whatever it is plugged into: a building, another vehicle or the grid itself.

Why now? Because 2026 is the year this left the test bench for heavy trucks. The April run at Regensburg was billed as the first live demonstration of bidirectional charging with an electric truck [2]. A second truckmaker followed within weeks [3][5]. On October 30 the three-year research project behind the first test presented its conclusions [1].

What happened in the yard

The project is called SPIRIT-E. It ran from August 2023 to October 2026, was led by the Technical University of Munich and brought together eight partners, including MAN Truck & Bus, Fraunhofer IEE, grid operator TenneT and Shell's charging business [1]. Germany's economy ministry funded it [1]. Here is what it and a rival test showed:

  • The cable reversed, at scale. In April, at the Schmid logistics yard near Regensburg, a specially modified eTGX sent about 325 kilowatts back into the grid [4]. The charging station, built by Shell's charging unit, is rated for up to 350 kilowatts in either direction [1].
  • Three ways to share. The partners tested vehicle-to-site (the truck powers its own depot, for example a warehouse at night), vehicle-to-vehicle (one truck charges another) and vehicle-to-grid (the truck feeds the public network) [1][3]. Truck and charger negotiated each transfer using ISO 15118-20, an international standard that lets a vehicle and a charger agree on when and how much to charge or discharge [1].
  • Practice trading. In a test environment, the truck bought electricity when prices were low and sold it when they rose [3]. It was also linked to a virtual power plant, software that bundles many small batteries so they can act like one large one [1]. The researchers also analyzed 2.15 million kilometers of real driving data from six German heavy-truck fleets [2].
  • The savings estimate. The partners consider energy-cost savings of around 10 percent realistic, with up to 20 percent under the best conditions [2][3]. For a truck covering 100,000 kilometers a year, 10 percent equals the energy for roughly 10,000 kilometers of driving [1]. These are project estimates, not results measured across working fleets [3].
  • A bigger plug joins in. In May, Scania reported its own vehicle-to-grid test through the Megawatt Charging System, or MCS, the new high-power plug designed for trucks and buses [3][5]. The company reports outputs of up to 1,000 amps and 750 kilowatts [5]. The MCS standard leaves room for up to 3.75 megawatts in the long run, a specification rather than anything shown so far [5].

MAN says it is working to bring bidirectional charging into series production, though it has not given a date [3].

One cable, current in either direction: the same charger that fills the truck can send its stored energy to the depot, to a second truck or to the public grid, timed by price signals from a virtual power plant [1][3].

The truck still has a day job

Here is the catch that the headline numbers hide. A truck is a truck first. Every hour it sits plugged in as a battery is an hour it is not hauling freight. Fleet operators work hard to keep that downtime short [4]. The partners say the idea fits best with regional fleets that return to the depot on a regular schedule and drive less than 100,000 kilometers a year [1][3]. A long-haul rig that is almost always on the road has little to lend.

The savings also depend on a long list of local details: annual mileage, how long the truck stands still, battery size, electricity prices, grid fees, whether the depot has solar panels and how much connection capacity is available [1]. Change a few of them and the 10 percent can shrink or grow.

Then there is the paperwork problem. The project found no uniform standard yet for selling the flexibility that trucks offer to the electricity market. Many providers use systems that cannot talk to each other [1]. Nobody has settled how the revenue should be split among the logistics company, the truckmaker and the firm that trades the power [1][2]. Smaller haulers, the project adds, will need planning tools and advice before they can take part [1].

One more honest wrinkle comes from the project's own figures. Pairing depot charging with solar panels and a fixed battery delivered savings 25 to 44 percent larger than simply switching to electric trucks [2]. A battery bolted to the warehouse wall can do much of the same job. It never leaves for a delivery. The truck's advantage is that its battery already exists.

So the verdict is cautious. The technology works. Turning it into routine business is what remains, which is how TUM's Markus Lienkamp framed it: the task now is to "standardize and scale the solutions that have been tested" [2].

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE thought experiments built on the tests described above. No company or city has announced them.

The depot that never needed a bigger cable. A regional grocery distributor staggers its charging and borrows energy from parked trucks to run its cold stores through the evening peak. The site stays inside its existing grid connection for years longer, while the money that would have gone into a new transformer goes into more trucks. The drivers notice nothing, except that the evening schedule now comes with a charging plan as well as a route.

Storm duty. After high winds knock out power to a small town, the local utility asks fleets with bidirectional trucks to drive to a water pumping station and a community shelter. Each truck plugs in, keeps the lights and pumps running for hours and drives back to the depot to recharge once the lines are repaired. Emergency planners start listing the depot's trucks alongside its generators, as resources that can move to where the outage is.

The truck that brings its own stage. A touring event company delivers its stage, speakers and lighting by electric truck, then plugs the same trucks into the stage to power the show. The diesel generators that used to rumble behind the crowd stay at home. Between shows, the same trucks top up from the venue's grid connection overnight, so the tour carries its power plant with it from city to city.

The weekend battery. Many delivery trucks stand idle from Saturday afternoon to Monday morning. A virtual power plant pools hundreds of them across a region and offers their combined battery to the grid operator for those quiet hours, when wind and solar output can run ahead of demand. Fleet owners set a floor below which no truck will discharge, so every vehicle is still ready for its Monday route.

Truck-to-truck rescue. On a rural route, a truck that has misjudged its range pulls into a service yard. A second truck with charge to spare connects through a portable bidirectional unit and lends it enough energy to reach the next charger, the electric version of a jump start. Roadside assistance crews begin carrying the adapters in their vans, alongside tow straps and spare fuses.

Back in the yard

Return to the freight yard near Regensburg after dark. The truck in its bay looks exactly as it did before the project: parked, plugged in, quiet. The difference is invisible. The current in that cable can now point either way, depending on what the building, the grid or the next truck needs.

That small reversal changes what a vehicle is. A truck stops being only a load on the grid and becomes, for part of each day, a piece of it. Whether that becomes normal depends less on batteries than on rules, contracts and who gets paid.
