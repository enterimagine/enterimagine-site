The Mechanic Still Climbing to Work

A robot with two seven-jointed arms left Florida in July. It is now spending a year spiraling outward to the satellites that, until now, nobody could ever touch again.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe7 min read

RoboticsSpaceEnergy

A healthy satellite with an empty tank

Somewhere high above the equator, a communications satellite is doing its job perfectly. Its antennas are aimed. Its electronics are fine. Its solar panels still soak up sunlight every day. Yet its owners are already planning its funeral.

The reason is almost embarrassing. The satellite is running low on the propellant that its small thrusters burn to hold it in place and keep it pointed at Earth. When that runs out, a working machine worth a fortune becomes a piece of drifting hardware.

Picture a delivery van with a perfect engine and a full cargo hold, scrapped because nobody can reach it with a jerry can. That has been the economics of high orbit for as long as satellites have lived there.

On July 21, 2026, a SpaceX Falcon 9 lifted off from Cape Canaveral with a spacecraft built to change that ending [1][3]. It carries two robotic arms, a kit of interchangeable tools and three strap-on thruster packs [1][3]. It has not touched a single customer yet. It is still climbing gently on electric thrusters. The climb will take about a year [1][5].

The first mechanic for the most valuable parking lot in space is on its way to work. The question is whether the job it is heading for actually exists.

Launch it, then let it go

The U.S. Naval Research Laboratory (NRL) has a blunt name for the way satellites have always been run: a "launch-and-abandon" model [5]. You build a spacecraft, test it on the ground until you trust it and send it up. After that, nobody ever lays a hand on it again.

That rule shapes everything. Engineers pack in spare parts, duplicate circuits and generous fuel margins, because a single stuck hinge can end a mission. Every satellite is designed as if it will be alone forever, since it always has been.

The stakes are highest in geosynchronous orbit, or GEO. A satellite there circles the planet once a day, matching Earth's spin, so from the ground it seems to hang in one spot. That makes it ideal for broadcasting, weather watching and communications. It also makes it very far away from any help.

Some help already exists, but only of one kind. Northrop Grumman's subsidiary SpaceLogistics operates two Mission Extension Vehicles that latch onto aging satellites and lend them propulsion. The company reports that the pair has delivered more than a decade of combined extra life for several clients [6]. It also says it is the first and still the only company to dock with a working GEO satellite for servicing [6].

That approach works, but it is one big vehicle per job. A real repair economy needs something closer to a roving mechanic: one robot that can visit many satellites, fit parts and move on. DARPA's program goal is precisely that, a servicer with enough fuel and capacity for dozens of operations over several years [2].

A toolbox on a slow spiral

The spacecraft is called the Mission Robotic Vehicle (MRV). It pairs a spacecraft body from SpaceLogistics with a robotic payload from DARPA's Robotic Servicing of Geosynchronous Satellites program, or RSGS [1][2]. Here is what has been confirmed so far.

  • A booster's last flight. The Falcon 9 left Space Launch Complex 40 on July 21, 2026 [1][3]. Its first-stage booster flew for the 32nd and final time, because the trip toward geosynchronous transfer orbit needed the extra performance, SpaceX said [3].
  • Two arms, seven joints each. The payload carries two manipulator arms, each with seven high-strength joints plus a dedicated tool drive [1][5]. Interchangeable tools, cameras, lights and flight software complete the kit [5]. NRL designed and built the robotics with DARPA funding [4][5]. Before delivery in October 2024, both arms went through thermal vacuum tests that included tool changes and calibrations [7].
  • Jet packs in the cargo bay. Three Mission Extension Pods (MEPs) rode along [3][6]. These are small propulsion modules that the arms will attach to client satellites [4]. DARPA and NRL say each pod can add six or more years of life [1][5]; Spaceflight Now reports up to eight years [3]. Australia's Optus and Luxembourg's SES are named as the first pod customers [3].
  • A year of patient climbing. Electric thrusters are frugal with propellant but weak in push, so the MRV will spend roughly a year raising its orbit before demonstrations can start [1][5]. DARPA expects an operational life of more than 10 years [1].
  • A three-way partnership. SpaceLogistics owns and will operate the spacecraft [1][5]. NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center has supported RSGS since 2024 with simulation tools, software checks and a team of flight robot operators [4]. After its first demonstrations, DARPA plans to hand the program to the U.S. Space Force [1].

The job list is broad. DARPA names upgrades, inspections, fixing anomalies and relocating satellites [1]. Northrop Grumman adds repair, disposal and assembly, plus a Passive Refueling Module that it describes as the first refueling interface standard approved by the Space Force [6]. That last claim comes from the company.

Twenty years to the starting line

The surprising part of this story is not the robot. It is how long the robot took to leave the ground.

NRL's work on servicing arms began more than two decades ago under other names, Spaceflight Now recounts, passing through projects called RescueSat, SUMO and FREND before DARPA folded the idea into later efforts [3]. NRL's own team says it has spent nearly 10 years on the RSGS version alone [7]. Only in 2019 did DARPA pick SpaceLogistics as the commercial partner to fly it [3].

That history hints at the real bottleneck. Building a dexterous arm is hard, but it is a solved kind of hard. The harder problem is a business one: who pays for a mechanic before anyone has seen the mechanic work? DARPA frames its goal as a service that can stand on its own feet commercially, in the words of its program manager, "a sustainable, commercially viable service" [1].

There are honest unknowns too.

  • Nothing has been grabbed yet. As of the most recent company and agency pages, the MRV is still en route [4][6]. Every claim about servicing describes a plan, not a result.
  • The numbers do not fully agree. Pod life is quoted as six or more years by DARPA and NRL [1][5] but up to eight years in trade reporting [3]. The real figure will depend on each client satellite.
  • A robot that can grab can be misread. A machine that approaches a satellite and takes hold of it is, technically, the same whether the visit is invited or not. The program is moving toward the U.S. Space Force once demonstrations end [1]. That makes openness about who is being serviced and why part of the job.

None of this makes the mission less interesting. It makes the first docking, sometime after the climb ends, a genuine test rather than a formality.

Imagined futures: when high orbit gets a garage

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments. They are not plans announced by DARPA, NRL, NASA, Northrop Grumman or any satellite operator.

The house call for a stuck hinge. A newly launched satellite reaches orbit with one antenna reflector that refuses to unfold. Today that can cripple a mission. In this future, the owner books a visit. A servicing robot drifts alongside, films the jammed latch from every angle and nudges it free with a soft-tipped tool, under careful watch from operators on the ground.

Satellites designed to be touched. Once servicing is normal, designers stop building every spacecraft as if it will be alone forever. New satellites come with grapple points, standard refueling valves and slots for plug-in modules. They launch lighter, because they no longer need to carry a lifetime of fuel on day one.

Upgrade, do not replace. A broadcaster's satellite is mechanically sound but its electronics feel dated. Instead of building a replacement, the owner pays for a bolt-on module with a newer processor or an extra antenna. The robot installs it in an afternoon and moves on to the next client.

The tow truck for the graveyard. Some satellites fail without warning and cannot move themselves out of the way. A servicer clamps onto one, pushes it up into a disposal orbit above the busy band and frees its valuable slot for a working replacement.

Building what no rocket can carry. Large antennas are limited by the width of a rocket's nose cone. Robots in high orbit could assemble them from stacked parts, the same idea explored on the Moon in The Robots That Walk on What They Build.

The first day on the job

Back to that healthy satellite with the empty tank. For its whole working life, its owners have known exactly how the story ends. In about a year, for the first time, a different ending may be on offer: a robot arrives, takes hold and fits a fresh pack of thrusters.

The takeaway is simple. Space has never lacked clever machines. It has lacked maintenance. If the MRV works, the most valuable orbit around Earth starts to look less like a scrapyard and more like a street with a garage on the corner.
