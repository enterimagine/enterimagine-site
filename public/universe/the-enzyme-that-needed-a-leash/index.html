The Enzyme That Needed a Leash

Software can now write gene-editing proteins that nature never built. The catch is that it only works when biologists tell it what not to touch.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe9 min read

AI & DataBiotechInnovation

A plate full of suicide notes

Somewhere in a Berkeley laboratory, a shallow dish of bacteria spent a night deciding which proteins deserved to exist.

The bacteria had been rigged to kill themselves. Each cell carried a gene for a toxin, switched on and ticking. The only escape was to cut that gene out — and the cells could not do it alone. They needed molecular scissors. Into the dish went 1,980 candidate pairs of them, each one a protein sequence that no living thing had ever made. They had been written by software.

By morning the dish had sorted them. Cells carrying a design that cut survived. Cells carrying a design that merely looked plausible did not. The researchers sequenced what was left and counted: 466 of the 1,980 synthetic enzymes worked; around 8% of them cut better than the natural protein they had been modeled on [1][3].

It is the sort of result that invites a tidy headline. Artificial intelligence now designs the tools of genetic engineering, inventing enzymes that evolution never found.

The tidy headline is wrong. The reason it is wrong is the more interesting story. When the researchers let the models design freely, the output came back almost indistinguishable from proteins that already existed, more than 99% identical to natural relatives [3]. The genuinely new enzymes only appeared once biologists took away the model's freedom and told it, residue by residue, which parts of the protein it was forbidden to touch.

The leash is what made the invention possible.

Too big for the delivery van

Gene editing has a logistics problem that rarely makes the news.

The famous editor, Cas9, is a protein borrowed from a bacterium that uses it to shred invading viruses. Give it a short guide made of RNA and it will find a matching stretch of DNA and cut it. That is the mechanism behind Casgevy, the sickle-cell treatment the US Food and Drug Administration approved in December 2023 as the first CRISPR-based therapy [7]. In its trial, 29 of 31 evaluable patients went at least twelve consecutive months without a severe pain crisis [7].

That approval also shows how blunt the delivery still is. Patients had to undergo myeloablative chemotherapy before their edited cells were returned to them [7]. The editing is elegant; getting it into a person is not.

The usual delivery van is a hollowed-out virus called AAV, chosen because it is mild and good at entering human cells. Its cargo hold is about 4.7 kilobases of genetic code [8]. Cas9 from Streptococcus pyogenes runs to 1,368 amino acids [10]; its gene plus a guide RNA already fills roughly 4.2 kilobases [8]. There is almost no room left for the switches that control when and where the protein is made. The common workaround splits the system across two separate viruses and hopes both reach the same cell [8].

So the search for smaller scissors has been running for years. Nature offers a few: one compact alternative is 1,053 amino acids, another 984 [8]. Smaller still is a family called TnpB, a stripped-down relative of the CRISPR proteins, small enough that there would be space in the van for everything else.

The trouble with TnpB is that it is not very good at its job in human cells. Which raises the question the Berkeley team set out to answer: if evolution handed us a chassis this compact but underpowered, could software finish the engineering?

What the software actually built

The answer arrived in Science on 16 July 2026, in a paper from the Innovative Genomics Institute at the University of California, Berkeley [1][2]. The team calls its synthetic enzymes SynTnpBs. What they did is worth walking through slowly, because the method matters more than the molecules.

  • They started with a structure, not a sequence. AlphaFold2 predicted how the natural TnpB protein folds in space. An inverse-folding model, ESM-IF1, was then asked the reverse question: what other sequences of amino acids would produce this same shape? [1][3]
  • They built the leash out of evolution. A statistical model of how amino acids in this protein family change together across species identified which positions are load-bearing. Those were locked. Everything else the software could rewrite [1][3]. A rival design model tried as a comparison failed outright, mangling the protein's catalytic core at every setting [3].
  • They sampled at scale, then recombined. Ten thousand generated sequences per condition were distilled into consensus designs. The protein's two halves were varied separately, 44 versions of one and 45 of the other, then recombined into 1,980 pairings [3].
  • They let bacteria do the judging. The toxin-survival screen described above sorted the 1,980 pairings and surfaced 466 working enzymes, with roughly 8% outperforming the natural original [3].
  • They checked the winners in human cells. In a standard HEK293T assay that switches off a fluorescent marker, the natural enzyme managed 28%. Two designs reached 46% and 50% [3]. At four real human genes, the best design hit 26% at one site where the natural protein managed 7%, a 3.8-fold improvement [3].

Two further results give the work its weight. A cryo-electron microscopy structure of the most heavily rewritten variant, resolved to 2.8 Å, caught the enzyme gripping its target by a single base pair of DNA, a state nobody had documented before [1][2][3]. And the designs work outside human cells, editing in bacteria and in Arabidopsis plant cells [1][3].

The scale of the rewrite is the headline figure. The synthetic enzymes share only 50–60% of their sequence with the natural protein they came from [3]. Roughly half of a working enzyme was replaced. It still worked. Often better.

None of this is near a patient. Every source treats it as preclinical [2].

A separate thread runs alongside. Profluent published work in Nature in 2025 on OpenCRISPR-1, which it describes as the first AI-generated genome editor to edit human DNA; the company reports reduced off-target cutting in a genome-wide assay, without publishing efficiency figures in its announcement [6]. It released the editor free for research and commercial use [6], an unusual move in a field defined by patent litigation. A not-yet-peer-reviewed preprint from January 2026 tested an OpenCRISPR-1-derived editor in rice with mixed results: base editing beat the Cas9 comparison at one site, 15% against 6.6%, then lost at another [9].

Where the novelty came from

Both roads begin with the same predicted shape. Only the road that forbids the software from touching the load-bearing positions arrives somewhere nature has not already been.

The sharpest scissors may be the sloppiest

Here the story splits. Honesty requires reporting both halves.

One account of the work holds that the synthetic enzymes were more discriminating than the natural original, cutting fewer unintended sites [4]. No numbers accompany that claim. An independent bioinformatics researcher at Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, commenting in a collection of expert reactions gathered by Spain's Science Media Center, read the data the other way: the two standout variants, the most active of the set, also carried the greatest number of off-target sites, meaning power and precision were traded against each other [5].

Both readings are on the public record. The preprint version of the work, which is openly available, measures on-target editing only and contains no off-target analysis at all [3]. Settling the disagreement requires the figures behind the journal's paywall, which this article could not open.

For a reader deciding how excited to be, that is the single most useful fact in the story. An enzyme that cuts a target gene twice as often as the natural version, while also nicking a dozen places it should not, is not obviously an improvement. In a therapy, an off-target cut in the wrong gene is how gene editing goes from treatment to tumor.

The same expert collection raises three further limits [5]. The evolutionary data forming the leash comes only from organisms that survived, a thin slice of the protein diversity that has existed. The study measured editing activity and nothing else, leaving stability, manufacturing cost and protein interactions unexamined. And the protocol cannot yet be aimed at a chosen DNA sequence, which is what a clinician would eventually need.

Two absences are telling too. Nobody in that expert collection raised delivery or immune response [5]. A synthetic protein that human immunity has never encountered is a reasonable thing to worry about; the silence is a gap in the public discussion rather than an endorsement.

Which brings the story back to its own framing. The models did not outthink evolution. Left to themselves they copied it, returning sequences over 99% identical to proteins that already existed [3]. The novelty came from biologists deciding what the software could not change, using billions of years of evidence about which parts of a protein cannot be touched. Evolution was not the competitor here. It was the instruction manual.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Everything below this line is speculation: physically plausible extrapolations from the work described above, not announced plans, products or timelines. No figures in this section are measured.

The editor that fits in one van. The point of starting from a compact protein is cargo space. An editor small enough to travel in a single AAV, with room left for the switches that control where it turns on, would remove the two-virus workaround entirely. That is less exciting than inventing a new enzyme and considerably more useful to anyone trying to treat a liver or a retina.

Specificity as a design target. Every design in this work was screened on one question: did it cut? A screen that scored off-target cutting at the same time, with the same throughput, would let the software optimize for precision rather than stumble into it. The Barcelona critique is really a specification for the next experiment.

Editors built for a single patient. The method takes a fold and returns thousands of candidates in silico. Pointed at a rare mutation carried by a handful of families worldwide, the same loop could in principle produce an editor matched to that exact sequence. The bottleneck would not be design. It would be the regulatory question of how you run a trial for a treatment with four eligible patients.

Crops edited with tools nobody owns. The rice work hints at where this lands first. Agricultural editing faces no immune system and no delivery problem; an editor released under an open license sidesteps the patent thickets that have slowed CRISPR in food crops. A public-sector plant breeder with a free editor is a different economic actor than one negotiating for a license.

Evolution as a reusable instruction manual. The constrained-redesign trick is not specific to gene editors. Any protein family with deep sequence data and a solved structure could in principle be put through the same loop: the enzymes that break down plastics, that fix nitrogen, that fold other proteins. If the leash generalizes, the interesting number stops being how many enzymes were designed and becomes how many protein families have enough evolutionary history to be redesigned safely.

Back to the plate

Return to that dish of bacteria, rigged to die unless rescued.

It is worth noticing what the screen actually was. Nearly two thousand proposals, no theory capable of ranking them, plus a biological process that sorted the working from the plausible overnight at almost no cost. The software did not know which designs would cut. Neither did the researchers. The bacteria decided.

That division of labor is the shape of the thing. Prediction models propose at a scale no laboratory could match by hand. Evolutionary data narrows what they may propose. Living cells return the verdict. Each part does what the others cannot.

The risk in reporting results like this is the word "designed", which suggests an engineer with a blueprint. What happened here was closer to a very fast, very broad guess, aimed by biology and judged by biology. The guess was good: half a protein rewritten; it still works [3].

Whether it works safely is the open question; the public record currently disagrees with itself about it [4][5]. That disagreement deserves to be settled in print before anyone describes these enzymes as better.

In The Archive That Fits in a Pill, we looked at DNA as a place to store information rather than a thing to edit. Here the same molecule is the workpiece; the tools arrive from a different direction entirely.

Next time, we stand in a desert at three in the morning, where the cheapest way to clean a solar panel may be to charge it and let the dust walk off by itself.
