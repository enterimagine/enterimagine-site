The Clock Inside the Nucleus

For more than seven decades, the world's best clocks have counted the jumps of electrons. In October 2026, two labs showed a clock can count something far smaller and better shielded: a flip inside an atom's nucleus. Here is why that matters for navigation, physics and the hidden timing behind everyday life.

2 November 2026By @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

InnovationQuantum ComputingSpaceSmart Mobility

A crystal that keeps time

In a lab in Vienna sits a small, clear crystal. It looks like nothing special: a sliver of mineral that could pass for a piece of window glass. Inside it are atoms of thorium-229, an unusual isotope with a secret.

Shine ultraviolet light on that crystal at precisely the right color, near 148 nanometers. The thorium nuclei absorb it [7]. Miss the color by the slightest amount and they ignore it. That fussiness is exactly what a clockmaker wants.

On 7 October 2026, two teams working independently, one in Vienna and one in Beijing, published papers in Nature describing the first working clocks that keep time with atomic nuclei rather than with electrons [3][4]. The Vienna team, led from TU Wien with Germany's national metrology institute PTB, reports a clock that steers itself using the thorium nuclei alone and ran for more than a day without anyone touching it [1]. The Beijing team, from Tsinghua University and the Beijing Academy of Quantum Information Sciences, built its clock around a powerful new ultraviolet laser [3][5].

The Vienna group has been working toward this moment since 2008 [3]. Its leader still calls the technology far from its target performance [3].

So why are physicists so excited about a clock that is not yet the best clock? Because of where it keeps its time.

The invisible metronome

Most people never think about clocks this precise, yet they lean on them constantly.

Take satellite navigation. A receiver works out its position by timing radio signals from several satellites, solving for its three coordinates plus its own clock error, which is why it needs at least four satellites in view [8]. Getting a position to within about a meter requires timing accurate to the nanosecond, a billionth of a second [8]. Phone networks, fiber-optic links, financial data centers and surveying all rely on the same kind of shared, ultra-steady time [3].

Today that time comes from atomic clocks, which count the rhythm of electrons jumping between energy levels in atoms such as cesium or strontium. They are astonishing machines. But electrons sit on the outside of the atom, where stray electric and magnetic fields, heat and vibration can nudge them. Keeping the best optical atomic clocks stable demands ultrahigh vacuum chambers plus laser cooling [4].

A nucleus is different. It is more than 10,000 times smaller than the atom around it [1], tucked inside a shield of electrons. Think of the difference between a candle on a windowsill and a candle inside a sealed lantern: the lantern flame flickers far less. In principle, a clock that counts a nuclear transition could be steadier still. Some day it might be simpler too, perhaps even working inside a solid crystal rather than a vacuum chamber [4].

The catch has always been reaching the nucleus at all. Thorium-229 is the rare exception with a nuclear transition low enough in energy, about 8.4 electronvolts, for a laser to drive it [7]. For decades, physicists could not even pin down exactly where that transition sat. The last two years changed that.

Two clocks, one month

The road to a working nuclear clock ran through a quick series of milestones. Here is what has been verified.

  • 2024: the first tick. In April 2024, the Vienna and PTB teams showed for the first time that a laser could flip thorium-229 nuclei into their excited state [1]. In September 2024, a collaboration led at JILA in Colorado, working with Vienna and other partners, made the cover of Nature by measuring that transition against a strontium atomic clock to 12 significant digits [7]. The precision of the thorium measurement had improved by 11 orders of magnitude in five years [7]. Later that year, Vienna used the nuclei as a timing reference while still leaning on a conventional atomic clock [1].
  • October 2026: Vienna's self-steering clock. The new TU Wien and PTB device no longer needs that crutch. A laser shines on a thorium-rich crystal grown in Vienna; if the laser drifts off the exact frequency, the nuclei absorb less light, the drop is detected and the laser is nudged back [1][2]. It ran unattended for more than 24 hours, with a measured relative precision of about one part in 10¹⁵ over a day [1]. That is often translated as one second of error in 30 million years, a projection from the measurement rather than a test of that length [2].
  • A first science run. The Vienna team already used its clock to look for dark matter, searching for tiny wobbles or slow drifts in the nuclear transition over periods from 20 seconds to a day [3]. Reporting on the paper says the results compete with the best atomic clocks for one kind of dark matter interaction and go beyond earlier limits for another [3].
  • October 2026: Beijing's high-power route. The Tsinghua team took a different path. It built what it describes as the first continuous-wave vacuum ultraviolet laser at 148 nanometers. It grew its crystal from just 1.4 micrograms of thorium-229, about one two-hundredth of what the other team used [5]. The Tsinghua group reports short-term stability nearly ten times better than the contemporary system [5]; independent coverage likewise describes the Beijing clock as the better performer of the two [4].

The difference in approach came partly from supply. Vienna had more thorium, so it used more nuclei and a gentler laser; Tsinghua had less, so it pushed a stronger laser through a smaller sample [4]. Both worked. As the Beijing team's leader put it, the agreement shows the concept is robust [3].

Not the best clock, yet

Here is the honest caveat. It is a big one: neither nuclear clock beats the clocks we already have.

The Vienna team says plainly that its prototype does not reach the level of the world's best optical atomic clocks [1]. Coverage of both papers puts the gap in perspective: the new devices would drift by about a second over millions of years, while the best conventional atomic clocks would take billions [4]. Headlines suggesting nuclear clocks could make atomic clocks obsolete are getting well ahead of the evidence; the researchers say only that nuclear clocks could eventually surpass them [2].

The obstacles are concrete.

  • Thorium is scarce. Thorium-229 is hard to obtain. The amount on hand shaped each team's design [4][5]. A technology that depends on micrograms of a rare isotope will need a reliable supply before it can spread.
  • Lasers and crystals. Both teams name stronger lasers and better thorium-doped crystals as the next steps [1][5]. The Beijing laser is new hardware in its own right [5].
  • Two trade-offs, one wish. One group had more nuclei, the other more laser power. The Tsinghua team's leader has said that combining both could improve stability substantially [4]. That is a promise of progress, but it also shows how far there is to go.

There is a subtler point too. A better clock does not automatically mean better navigation in your car or phone. Satellite positioning depends on a whole chain of satellites, signals, receivers and corrections. Researchers list navigation among the long-range applications [3][6], but no one has shown that a nuclear clock improves a real navigation system today.

What October 2026 proved is narrower but still historic: a nucleus can be the pendulum. Everything else is engineering.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation zone. The scenarios below are imagined by IMAGINE. They are not announced plans of TU Wien, PTB, Tsinghua University or any company. They carry no forecasts or invented figures.

The gravity map in a backpack. Clocks tick at slightly different rates depending on the pull of gravity around them. Researchers have suggested portable nuclear clocks could map underground variations in gravity [6]. Picture a survey team walking a hillside with a rugged crystal clock, reading the hidden density of the rock beneath them: an aquifer here, a buried void there.

A spacecraft that carries its own time. Finding your way far from Earth is hard. Improved deep-space navigation is one of the uses researchers mention [6]. Imagine a probe heading for the outer planets with a solid-state nuclear clock on board, steering by its own steady heartbeat during long silences.

The constant watchers. Physicists wonder whether the so-called constants of nature are truly constant [6]. A network of nuclear clocks on different continents could compare notes every second. If the ticking of nuclei ever drifted relative to the ticking of electrons, that tiny disagreement could point to new physics, perhaps even to dark matter [3].

The backup heartbeat for a city. Cell networks, fiber links and data centers all depend on shared, steady time [3]. A compact nuclear clock in a city's network hub could keep that shared rhythm steady through an outage, so traffic signals, payment systems and phone calls stay in step until the signal returns.

A clock without the vacuum. Because the nuclei sit inside a solid crystal, future versions might skip the vacuum chambers and laser cooling that today's best clocks need [4]. Picture a precision clock that fits in an ordinary equipment rack and can be shipped like any other instrument.

Back to the crystal

Return to that small, clear crystal in Vienna. For more than a day, with no one at the controls, its thorium nuclei quietly refused every wrong color of light and accepted only the right one. That refusal was the clock.

It is not yet the world's best timekeeper. It may not be for years. But in one month, two labs on opposite sides of the world showed that the most protected rhythm inside an atom can be counted, steered and put to work. The pendulum has moved from the outside of the atom to its core.
