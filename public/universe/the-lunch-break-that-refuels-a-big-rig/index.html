The Lunch Break That Refuels a Big Rig

Megawatt plugs can now refill a heavy truck during a driver's break. The harder job is finding the power behind them.

undefinedBy @EnterImagine · IMAGINE Scientific Universe8 min read

EnergySmart MobilitySmart Cities

A thick cable at the harbor gate

Picture a gravel lot a short drive from the cranes at the Port of Long Beach. A tractor-trailer rolls in, its driver climbs down and a cable as thick as a forearm locks into a socket on the side of the cab. No diesel pump. No hum of an idling engine. On November 5, 2026, a charging company cut a ribbon on exactly this kind of scene: the first two Megawatt Charging System chargers at its harbor site, built for heavy trucks serving the port [1].

The next day, on the other side of the Atlantic, a truck charging network opened its fifth Belgian hub at Temse, between Antwerp and Ghent. Four of its eight bays can deliver up to 1,500 kilowatts through the new megawatt plug [2].

Fifteen hundred kilowatts is a strange number to hold in your head. It is three to four times the ceiling of the fastest car chargers [7], pushed through one connector into one vehicle. The point is not raw spectacle. It is time. A long-haul driver already stops for a mandatory break; the question is whether a truck battery can refill in that same window.

That is the bet behind megawatt corridors: that electric freight will not need new habits, only a much bigger plug. The surprise is that the plug is now the easy part.

Why a car charger will not do

Heavy trucks are the hardest corner of road transport to electrify. They carry enormous batteries, run long shifts and earn money only while they move. A fleet manager cares less about range on paper than about how many minutes a truck spends parked at a charger instead of on the road.

Ordinary fast chargers were designed around passenger cars. One Illinois clean-transport group puts light-duty DC fast charging at a ceiling of roughly 350 to 500 kilowatts [7]. Feed a big rig's battery at that rate during a short rest stop and it leaves with a fraction of what it needs.

So the industry wrote a new plug. The Megawatt Charging System, or MCS, is a single connector for the heaviest vehicle classes (Class 6 through 8 in US terms). The charging alliance that steers it lists requirements of up to 1,250 volts and 3,000 amperes of direct current [3]. Multiply those two numbers and you reach a theoretical 3.75 megawatts, roughly seven to ten times the car-charging ceiling above. The connector also carries a digital conversation between truck and charger over Ethernet, using the ISO 15118-20 protocol [3]. Think of it as a handshake before the heavy lifting: the two sides agree on what to send before a single electron flows.

Then there is the clock. In Europe, network operators design around the rest break drivers already take. One operator says trucks at its demonstration site reached 80% or a full charge within breaks of 30 to 45 minutes [4]. If that holds in daily service, the charge disappears into time the driver would have spent parked anyway.

The scale of what is still missing is stark. Germany's automotive industry association estimates that Europe needs about 35,000 MCS charging points by 2030, against roughly 730 heavy-duty points above 350 kilowatts across the EU today [4].

The corridors taking shape

The last few months have turned MCS from a demonstration into something closer to a network. Here is what has been confirmed.

  • The truck-makers' own network keeps adding hubs. Milence, a joint venture of Daimler Truck, Traton and the Volvo Group, opened its Temse hub on November 6 with four CCS bays (the plug many trucks use today, at around 400 kilowatts) plus four hybrid bays offering MCS at up to 1,500 kilowatts [2]. In September the company said it ran more than 2,000 charging points at almost 40 hubs in nine markets. It is aiming for 70 hubs by the end of 2027, with more than half offering MCS [4].
  • Trucks are arriving with the socket fitted. At the IAA Transportation show in September, Milence reported that MAN has fitted MCS as standard on its eTGX since July 2026. The same release cited Scania describing a starting point of 1,000 amperes and 750 kilowatts, with potential beyond 3 megawatts later [4].
  • US freight hubs are going big. In April, a hub opened in San Bernardino, California, with 76 high-power ports, 9 megawatts of capacity and chargers able to reach 1.2 megawatts through MCS. Its operator expects it to serve more than 200 medium and heavy trucks a day [5]. At the Port of Long Beach, the November ribbon-cutting marked what the operator calls the first of 30 megawatts of MCS capacity it is bringing online [1].
  • Truck stops are joining in. In January, Tesla and Pilot Travel Centers announced MCS-capable charging for the Tesla Semi at selected Pilot sites along corridors including I-5 and I-10, with four to eight stalls per site at up to 1.2 megawatts each. A Tesla map from late February showed 66 planned locations in 15 states, two of them operating at the time [6].
  • More networks are announced. Trade reporting in November noted Tesla's plan, announced in September, for 21 European truck sites with more than 100 MCS points from 2027. The same report described a consortium of E.ON, Voltix and GreenWay planning about 330 MCS chargers at 55 locations, backed by about 70 million euros in EU infrastructure funding [2].

Several of these figures are company-reported targets rather than built capacity. Even so, the direction is clear: the plug is standardized, trucks carry it and sites are switching on.

The plug is not the bottleneck

Here is the counterintuitive part. The MCS connector is specified for up to 3.75 megawatts [3]. The chargers being installed today top out between about 1 and 1.5 megawatts [2][4][5]. The trucks on the road may accept less still: Scania's stated starting point is 750 kilowatts [4]. A plug rated for the future is already waiting on the hardware at both ends.

The bigger constraint sits behind the fence. A charging hub is a new industrial customer for the local grid. A heavy one. The Illinois group estimates that a single four-stall MCS site could draw more than 5 megawatts at full tilt [7], an author's estimate rather than a measured figure. The San Bernardino hub runs on 9 megawatts of capacity supplied by its utility [5]. Delivering that much power to a new site can mean new substations or new lines, work that often moves slower than pouring concrete for parking bays.

That is why batteries are showing up on site. At its September demonstration, Milence paired a 1,000-kilowatt charger with a storage system rated up to 1 megawatt and 2 megawatt-hours, specifically for locations where the grid connection is limited [4]. The battery fills slowly from a modest connection, then releases its energy in a rush when a truck plugs in. It works like a water tower: the pipe from the reservoir stays thin while the tower supplies the morning surge.

There is a second honest caveat. Many of the numbers in this story are plans. Targets for hubs, stalls and megawatts come from the companies building them [1][4][6]. Tesla's February map listed 66 planned truck-charging locations, yet only two were operating at the time [6]. Corridors are real where trucks are charging, not where pins are dropped.

Put simply, the race has moved from the connector to the substation.

Imagined futures

IMAGINED

Speculation, not announced plans. The scenarios below are IMAGINE's own thought experiments, built on the trends described above. None of them describes an existing product, site or commitment.

The rest stop that gives power back. The MCS requirements already discuss bidirectional charging [3]. Picture a highway hub on a summer evening, when demand peaks and solar output fades. Parked trucks with spare charge feed a little energy back to the grid, then top up again overnight when power is plentiful. The rest stop starts to behave like a small power plant that happens to have parking.

The harbor that times its trucks. At a container port, cranes, gate queues and chargers all keep their own schedules. Imagine port software that knows when a vessel will finish unloading and books each drayage truck a charging slot that ends just as its container is ready. The truck charges while it would have waited anyway. The grid sees a smooth load instead of a rush.

The booked corridor. Long-haul planning tools already route around traffic. Picture them reserving charging bays along a 1,000-kilometer run, shifting slots in real time when a truck is delayed. Drivers stop hunting for a free plug. Hub operators know their load an hour ahead, which helps them plan what they draw from the grid.

The quiet street at dawn. In a city that has electrified its delivery vans and trucks, the early-morning rumble of diesel engines near homes fades. Picture planners redrawing loading rules once quiet electric trucks can serve shops before sunrise without waking the street. Freight timing changes because the noise is gone, a smart-city effect that starts at a charger.

The depot as neighborhood battery. A city delivery fleet charges at its depot every night. Picture that depot agreeing with the local utility to soak up surplus wind at 3 a.m. and to pause charging during a heat wave. A logistics yard becomes one of the grid's flexible tools.

Back at the harbor gate

Return to that gravel lot near the cranes. The truck with the thick cable is not a prototype on a test track any more. It is one of the first customers of a corridor that now runs, in patches, from Long Beach to the Belgian lowlands [1][2].

The takeaway is simple. The plug has been settled. What decides how fast freight goes electric is the less glamorous chain behind it: grid connections, on-site storage and trucks that can accept the power on offer. Watch the substations, not the sockets.

This series has stood where energy meets mobility before. Batteries in Waiting was our first look at it on land; The Ferry That Had to Hitchhike took a similar question to sea. Megawatt corridors add a new twist to both stories: the truck stop is becoming part of the grid.
